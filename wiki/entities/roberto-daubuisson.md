# Roberto D'Aubuisson

Roberto D'Aubuisson was a Salvadoran army officer and far-right political figure identified as the leader of El Salvador's death squads during the country's civil war of the late 1970s and 1980s.[1] He was a graduate of the U.S. Army's [[school-of-the-americas|School of the Americas]] (SOA), the Panama- and later Georgia-based training institution whose alumni the article describes as "among the most repressive tyrants in Latin America."[1]

## Death squad leadership

Between 1979 and 1985, roughly 40,000 people were murdered by right-wing death squads in El Salvador, and D'Aubuisson was described as the most notorious symbol of those squads — the product of an alliance between far-right military officers and one of the hemisphere's most reactionary oligarchies.[1] He was implicated in the plot to assassinate the populist Archbishop Óscar Romero; three-quarters of the Salvadoran officers implicated in that killing had been trained at the SOA.[1] D'Aubuisson also participated in numerous other murders, including the massacre at the village of El Mozote, where more than 900 men, women, and children were killed.[1]

## U.S. patronage and the 1983 warning

The article argues that D'Aubuisson thrived because American leaders who feared a communist takeover in El Salvador deliberately turned a blind eye to state-sanctioned terrorism directed at Marxist guerrillas, their supporters, and suspected sympathizers.[1] Responsibility for that policy is traced to President [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]] and included Vice President [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]], U.N. Ambassador Jeane Kirkpatrick, Senator Jesse Helms, and then-obscure National Security Council aide [[oliver-north|Oliver North]].[1]

Washington acted only when domestic outrage — intensified by the rape and murder of three American nuns — threatened to push Congress to cut off aid to El Salvador.[1] On December 11, 1983, Bush met with El Salvador's provisional president Álvaro Magaña and warned that unless the right-wing slaughter stopped, U.S. aid would be jeopardized.[1] According to a senior participant, North, accompanying Bush, slipped Magaña an untraceable piece of paper without letterhead or watermark listing eight military officers and one civilian — all close D'Aubuisson associates — identified as death squad leaders.[1] None was ever brought to trial; all either went into "gilded exile" or dropped out of sight, and the death squad killings dropped sharply.[1] The episode demonstrated both that the United States knew who the killers were and that the killing could be slowed when real pressure was applied.[1]

The author's reading is that the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] around Bush cared about the massacres only when congressional money was at risk, not because of the tens of thousands of dead.[1]

D'Aubuisson died of cancer in February 1992.[1]
