# National Guard

The National Guard is the reserve military force of the United States, organized by state and available for both state and federal activation. During 2025 it became the central instrument of the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration's domestic law enforcement strategy in major American cities.[1]

## Domestic deployments under Trump

In August 2025, Trump deployed National Guard troops onto the streets of Washington, D.C. as part of a federal crackdown on crime in the capital.[1] On Monday, 15 September 2025, he signed an executive order extending comparable action to [[memphis|Memphis]], Tennessee, sending National Guard troops there and creating the [[memphis-state-task-force|Memphis Safe Task Force]], which he described as a "replica" of the Washington operation.[1]

Trump also pushed for similar military involvement in policing in Baltimore and Chicago.[1] Like Memphis and Washington, D.C., both are Democratic strongholds, a pattern noted by critics of the deployments.[1]

## Controversy

The Memphis deployment was endorsed by Tennessee governor [[bill-lee|Bill Lee]] but opposed by the city's mayor, who acknowledged that the governor and president had the authority to place the Guard in Memphis but stated that he had not asked for it and did not think it was the way to drive down crime.[1] Police statistics indicating that overall criminal offences in Memphis were at a 25-year low were cited against the premise of the order.[1]

## Chicago deployment blocked, December 2025

In December 2025 the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] temporarily blocked President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from deploying the National Guard in Chicago, where the administration said Guard forces were needed to protect federal immigration officers conducting [[operation-midway-blitz|Operation Midway Blitz]].[2] In a three-page unsigned ruling, the justices held that "at this preliminary stage, the Government has failed to identify a source of authority that would allow the military to execute the laws in Illinois," noting that the President had not invoked a statute providing an exception to the [[posse-comitatus-act|Posse Comitatus Act]].[2] Justices [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]], [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] and [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]] dissented, with Alito calling the decision "unwise," "imprudent" and "puzzling."[2]

## El Paso border stampede (March 2024)

A stampede of migrants overwhelmed National Guard troops deployed along the Rio Grande in El Paso, Texas, in an incident characterized in charging documents as a "border riot."[3] The [[texas-department-of-public-safety|Texas Department of Public Safety]] booked about 220 people on riot charges, and on March 31, 2024 magistrate judge [[humberto-acosta|Humberto Acosta]] ordered all rioting participation cases released on their own recognizance.[3] See [[el-paso-border-riot-case]].[3]

## Federalization dispute of June 2025

In June 2025, thousands of Guard members were federalized by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and deployed to Los Angeles amid protests over immigration raids, prompting litigation by California.[4] U.S. District Judge [[charles-breyer|Charles Breyer]] ruled on June 12 that the mobilization was illegal, exceeding the president's statutory authority and violating the Tenth Amendment, and ordered the troops returned to the control of Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]] by noon the next day.[4] The [[united-states-court-of-appeals-for-the-ninth-circuit|9th Circuit Court of Appeals]] stayed that order the same night and set a hearing for the following Tuesday, leaving the Guard federalized through the weekend and through the "No Kings" protest events.[4]

## Historical use against domestic unrest

The National Guard is a reserve force of the U.S. military that can be called on by state governors or by the president to respond to domestic emergencies or to participate in overseas combat and civilian missions.[5] Presidents have also, rarely, federalized the Guard to intervene in protests on U.S. soil.[5] In 1794, President George Washington "called upon state militia and volunteer units to suppress" the Whiskey Insurrection, a series of violent riots in western Pennsylvania against the federal liquor tax, according to the National Guard Bureau.[5] With some exceptions, presidents "avoided calling upon the militia in response to civil disturbances" for most of the 1800s, relying instead on state forces, but the practice became more common during the civil rights era when the federal government clashed with officials in Southern segregationist states; Presidents Dwight D. Eisenhower, John F. Kennedy and Lyndon B. Johnson at different times federalized the Guard "to enforce the expansion of civil rights and to ensure public order."[5]

In the spring of 1992, Los Angeles burned after a jury acquitted police officers who beat Black motorist [[rodney-king|Rodney G. King]]: dozens were killed, more than 1,500 injured, and property damage was estimated at about $500 million.[5] President [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] said he saw "the brutality of a mob, pure and simple," pledged to "use whatever force is necessary to restore order," and ordered thousands of Guard troops into the city — acting on the request of California officials.[5] Thirty-three years later, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] ordered 2,000 [[california-army-national-guard|California National Guard]] troops into Los Angeles to intervene in protests against his administration's immigration raids, this time against the wishes of Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]], who said state and local authorities had the protests under control.[5] The deployment prompted [[california-v-trump-administration-national-guard-deployment|California's lawsuit]] challenging the federalization as an "unprecedented power grab."[5]

## Portland and Chicago deployments, October 2025

In early October 2025, US District Judge [[karin-immergut|Karin Immergut]] issued a temporary injunction blocking President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s order deploying National Guard troops from other states to Portland, Oregon, where protesters had gathered outside an [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|Immigration and Customs Enforcement]] facility.[6] White House Press Secretary [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]] called the ruling "untethered in reality and in the law" and said the president has the right to call up the Guard whenever he deems it appropriate; the administration appealed.[6]

On Monday, October 6, 2025, Illinois and the city of Chicago filed suit to block the federalization and deployment of any state's National Guard to Illinois, arguing the principle separating the military from domestic affairs was "in peril" and that the state was being singled out for its political leadership.[6] Their attorneys contended that federal law permits such action only in cases of rebellion or invasion and that the move violated the [[posse-comitatus-act|Posse Comitatus Act]].[6]

## Washington, D.C. shooting (November 2025)

On November 26, 2025, two West Virginia National Guard members on patrol a short distance from the White House were shot at close range at approximately 2:15 p.m.[7] U.S. Army Specialist [[sarah-beckstrom|Sarah Beckstrom]] died of her wounds the following day, while U.S. Air Force Staff Sergeant Andrew Wolfe remained in critical condition.[7] The suspect, [[rahmanullah-lakanwal|Rahmanullah Lakanwal]], was an Afghan national who had served alongside U.S. troops and worked with the [[cia|CIA]] as part of a partner force in Kandahar before being admitted to the United States in September 2021.[7]

The attack intensified debate over the Guard's deployment in the capital and over the political environment surrounding it. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that Representative [[elissa-slotkin|Elissa Slotkin]] had forecast violence involving the National Guard less than a month earlier, while [[canncon|CannCon]] argued that opponents of the administration had "politicized the military, subverted the Constitutional authority of the Commander in Chief, and injected themselves in a chain of command they are NOT a part of."[7] The episode is treated in detail at [[2025-washington-dc-national-guard-shooting|the 2025 Washington D.C. National Guard shooting]].[7]

## Election standby, November 2024

Ahead of the [[2024-us-general-election|2024 general election]], the National Guard was placed on standby as a precaution against potential civil unrest in several states, including Washington, Oregon and Nevada.[8] Washington governor [[jay-inslee|Jay Inslee]] announced the activation the Friday before Election Day, citing the [[2024-pacific-northwest-ballot-box-fires|ballot drop box fires]] and saying that "the southwest region of Washington state has already experienced specific instances of election-related unrest."[8] He did not disclose troop numbers but said they would be available to support law enforcement from Monday through Thursday, and noted the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] warning that threats to election infrastructure remained high.[8] Oregon governor [[tina-kotek|Tina Kotek]] said the Guard was standing ready in her state as leaders called for peaceful protests.[8]

Reporting cited in the brief observed that Oregon had deployed the Guard alongside a unified command for election security in 2020, and that states had increasingly activated the Guard for election-related cybersecurity work.[8]

## Supreme Court rejection of Illinois deployment (December 2025)

On December 23, 2025 the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] rebuffed the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] over its plan to deploy National Guard troops in Illinois over the strenuous objections of local officials, turning away in an unsigned order an emergency request that argued the troops were needed to protect federal agents involved in immigration enforcement in the Chicago area.[9] Though preliminary and limited to Chicago, the decision was expected to bolster similar challenges to Guard deployments in other cities, with the opinion setting significant new limits on the president's ability to order them.[9] Illinois Governor [[jb-pritzker|JB Pritzker]] called it "an important step in curbing the Trump Administration's consistent abuse of power."[9]
