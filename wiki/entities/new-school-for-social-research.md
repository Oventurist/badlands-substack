# New School for Social Research

The New School for Social Research is a New York institution founded in 1919 in Greenwich Village, which Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] characterizes as the American branch office of the [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]] and the incubator of the North American technocracy movement.[1]

## Founding and funding

The New School was set up in 1919 and received funding from the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]].[1] Among its founders were [[thorstein-veblen|Thorstein Veblen]], Charles A. Beard, John Dewey and James Harvey Robinson.[1] Ehret writes that Beard, Dewey and Robinson were radical followers of the theories of the British writers John Ruskin and William Morris, whom he characterizes as imperialists advocating guild socialism under world control, and that the institution therefore served as an entry point for Oxford ideologues and London School of Economics Fabians into the United States.[1]

Veblen's New School study group gave rise to the [[technical-alliance|Technical Alliance]], which [[howard-scott|Howard Scott]] joined after the First World War and from which he later built [[technocracy-inc|Technocracy Inc.]].[1]

## Refuge for the Frankfurt School

From 1933 the New School became a hub for academics leaving Nazi Germany, the group later known as the [[frankfurt-school|Frankfurt School]].[1] Ehret names Erich Fromm, Max Wertheimer, Hannah Arendt and Leo Strauss among the most influential figures absorbed into the American elite academic system through the New School.[1] He quotes the historian Jeffrey Steinberg, who wrote that in the Germany of the 1920s and 1930s there were Jews who were Nazis but who, like Strauss and the Frankfurt School's "gaggle of left-wing Nietzscheans" — Theodor Adorno, Max Horkheimer, Leo Lowenthal, Herbert Marcuse and others — had no chance of party advancement because of Hitler's antisemitism, and so left Germany to pursue "more 'universal' fascist ideas and policies abroad, particularly in the United States and Great Britain."[1]

## In the technocracy story

When [[columbia-university|Columbia University]] president [[nicholas-murray-butler|Nicholas Murray Butler]] expelled Scott's committee in January 1933 and announced a special task force on technological progress, Alvin Johnson of the New School was named to the commission alongside Edmund Day of the Rockefeller Foundation, [[walter-lippmann|Walter Lippmann]], Benjamin Anderson of Chase National Bank and professors from Yale, Harvard and the University of Chicago.[1] Ehret reads this as the rebranding of technocracy under respectable institutional sponsorship rather than its suppression.[1]
