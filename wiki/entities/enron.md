# Enron

Enron was an energy and commodities company headquartered in Houston, Texas, whose 2001 bankruptcy became one of the most infamous corporate collapses in American history.[1] The company filed for bankruptcy after whistleblowers and investigative journalists revealed that executives had been artificially inflating the company's revenues and concealing losses from investors.[1]

## The audit relationship

Enron's audit firm, [[arthur-andersen|Arthur Andersen]], was responsible for reviewing and attesting to the accuracy of the company's financial statements — providing assurance that shareholders and the broader capital markets could trust those statements as true and accurate.[1] Instead, Andersen helped Enron conceal its financial problems and advised on complex accounting techniques used to hide massive debts and inflated profits.[1]

The Badlands Media essay "US Elections Must Be Independent," by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], uses the Enron case as the canonical illustration of the three classic threats to [[auditor-independence|auditor independence]]: familiarity, self-interest, and self-review.[1]

- **Familiarity**: Andersen had been Enron's auditor since the company's founding in 1985, and the relationship was cozy, evidenced by a revolving door between the firm and the company. Several key Enron executives, including Chief Accounting Officer Richard Causey, had previously worked at Andersen; Causey was indicted on securities fraud charges in 2004, pleaded guilty in 2005, and was released from prison in 2011 after serving a little over five years.[1]
- **Self-interest**: Enron was among Andersen's largest and most lucrative clients, generating $52 million in fees in 2000 alone. Losing the account would have been a substantial financial loss, which ostensibly influenced the firm to prioritize its own interests over its professional and ethical obligations. Complicity compounded this: once Andersen advised Enron on hiding losses, continuing the cover-up served the firm's own exposure.[1]
- **Self-review**: Roughly half of the 2000 fees — about $27 million — came from consulting work, including strategy consulting on expansion, acquisitions and deal structuring; technology consulting on financial reporting, risk management and trading systems; and performance management and executive compensation design. Andersen also reportedly advised on structured finance, tax strategy, risk management, and the complex derivatives and trading strategies central to Enron's business model. Because Andersen was shaping and directing the business, it was in effect auditing its own work.[1]

## Aftermath

The combination of familiarity, self-interest and self-review in the Andersen/Enron relationship misled investors, regulators and the public, and compromised the integrity of the audit process and profession.[1] As the scandal unraveled, Andersen shredded "a literal ton" of documents, deleted digital files, and obstructed justice; public oversight entities demanded accountability on the reasoning that markets cannot function without public trust.[1] The scandal resulted in the dissolution of Arthur Andersen, reducing the "Big Five" accounting firms to the "Big Four."[1]

## Contrast with elections

Ashe in America argues that the accountability response to Enron stands in stark contrast to US elections, where obstruction of election audits and transparency efforts produces no comparable scandal, and where those calling to preserve public trust in the [[2020-election-fraud-claims|2020 election]] were instead demonized and prosecuted.[1]
