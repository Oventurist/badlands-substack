# U.S. Department of Transportation

The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) is the federal cabinet department responsible for national transportation policy, including regulation of the shipment of hazardous materials. Its hazardous-materials authority placed it at the center of a February 2023 federal inquiry into [[neuralink|Neuralink]].[1]

## Neuralink pathogen investigation (2023)

On February 9, 2023, the Department said it was investigating [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s brain-implant company Neuralink over the potentially illegal movement of hazardous pathogens.[1] A Department spokesperson disclosed the probe to Reuters after the [[physicians-committee-for-responsible-medicine|Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine]] wrote to Secretary [[pete-buttigieg|Pete Buttigieg]] with records it had obtained suggesting unsafe packaging and transport of implants removed from monkey brains.[1] The spokesperson said the agency took the allegations "very seriously."[1]

Badlands Media contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] noted that the Department, then led by Buttigieg, was heading the investigation, and questioned the timing of the probe in relation to the [[twitter-files|Twitter Files]] disclosures.[1]

## Termination of New York congestion pricing (February 2025)

On Wednesday, February 26, 2025 the department sent a letter to top New York transportation officials informing them that the termination of New York City's congestion pricing plan would be effective on March 21, adding federal pressure after Governor [[kathy-hochul|Kathy Hochul]]'s White House appeal to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] failed.[2] The letter asked for an "orderly cessation" of the program; the [[metropolitan-transportation-authority|Metropolitan Transportation Authority]] indicated it was unlikely to comply, with Hochul countering that she would propose "orderly resistance" instead and noting the matter was in court.[2]

## Automatic airline refund rule (April 2024)

On Wednesday, April 24, 2024, the Biden administration issued final rules requiring airlines to automatically issue cash refunds for canceled flights and significant delays and to better disclose fees for baggage and reservation cancellations.[3] The Transportation Department said airlines would have to provide automatic cash refunds within a few days of a qualifying disruption.[3]

Under the prior regulatory framework, airlines themselves determined how long a delay had to last before refunds were triggered; the [[dot-airline-refund-rule|new rule]] eliminated that discretion by defining a significant delay as at least three hours for domestic flights and six hours for international ones.[3] Airlines remained free to offer an alternative flight or travel credit, but passengers were given the right to refuse and take cash instead.[3] Badlands commentary treated the measure, alongside the FTC's [[noncompete-agreement-ban|noncompete ban]], as a rare public-facing win for the administration that nonetheless entrenched administrative agency authority over private enterprise.[3]
