# Alfred Richard Orage

Alfred Richard Orage (1873–1934) was a British editor, occultist and Theosophist who published the influential New Age magazine from 1906 to 1922 and coined the term "Social Credit."[1] Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presents him as the mentor of [[c-h-douglas|Major C. H. Douglas]] and as the manager of a controlled "bottom-up" opposition to Fabian state planning.[1]

## New Age and Social Credit

Orage was a leading Theosophist, a [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]] associate, and personal assistant to the mystic philosopher George Ivanovich Gurdjieff.[1] He published one of Douglas's first works, "A Mechanical View of Economics," in New Age in 1919, and it was there that he coined the name "Social Credit."[1] [[aleister-crowley|Aleister Crowley]] was also published regularly in Orage's journal, and Ehret writes that both men advanced the same pseudo-anarchist thesis that a global revolution was needed to overthrow corrupt national governments on the grounds that these were run entirely by evil Jewish bankers, a claim advanced in such documents as the [[protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion|Protocols of the Elders of Zion]] promoted by virtually every fascist of the early twentieth century, Orage and Crowley included.[1]

## The 1912 break

Ehret argues that Orage's movement was nothing more than a controlled opposition to the centralized socialism promoted by his Fabian counterparts, from whom he apparently broke in 1912.[1] That year saw numerous splits among occult organizations into rival agencies pushing what occultists call "right hand" and "left hand" paths in order to steer social debate dialectically: Rudolf Steiner's Anthroposophists split from Annie Besant's Theosophists, and Crowley's British branch of the Ordo Templi Orientis split from other European branches.[1] It was also, in Ehret's reading, the year the world was set up for a violent transformation into a New Age — hence the title of Orage's journal.[1]

## Guild socialism

After the break, Orage abruptly abandoned top-down state planning in favour of "guild socialism," a total rejection of state planning in which anarchistic bottom-up self-organization would supposedly emerge if everyone were left free to act as they wished, moderated only by a confederation of medieval trade guilds and free markets.[1] Following the nineteenth-century guild socialists John Ruskin and William Morris, Orage demanded a restoration of artisanal, low-technology production and a "back-to-nature" ethos of Gaia worship later revived, Ehret says, in the modern environmental movement; another name for the philosophy was anarcho-syndicalism.[1]

In response to Orage's break, the London Fabian Society created The New Statesman in 1913 to promote its top-down technocratic planning model in direct opposition to New Age's bottom-up guild socialism, and [[walter-lippmann|Walter Lippmann]] set up the American version, The New Republic, in 1914.[1] Ehret argues that the dispute was manufactured, pointing out that the other leading exponents working with Orage to advance guild socialism — George Douglas Howard Cole, Samuel George Hobson and Arthur Joseph Penty — were themselves Fabians and followers of Ruskin and Morris who published regularly in New Age.[1] [[bertrand-russell|Bertrand Russell]], also a Fabian, publicly championed the same guild-socialist programme.[1]
