# Abraham

Abraham is the patriarch common to Judaism, Christianity and Islam, and in the Badlands Media essay arabian nights the founder of the [[kaaba]] and the ancestor of both [[hashim-ibn-abd-manaf|Hashim]] and [[prophet-muhammad|Muhammad]].

## Birth and the confrontation with Nimrod

The source relates that [[nimrod]], the wicked Babylonian king, was warned by his astrologers that a baby descended from Noah would be born who would bring an end to idolatry. Nimrod ordered the slaughter of all newborns in Mesopotamia; Abraham's mother escaped into the wilderness, where he was born.

Abraham later confronted Nimrod to end the practice of idolatry. Nimrod ordered him burned at the stake — fire being central to Nimrod's godless worship — and witnesses saw Abraham emerge unscathed, leading them to believe him a prophet of God. Nimrod then challenged him to a public debate, intending to disprove monotheism. Asked what his God could do that Nimrod could not, Abraham answered that God is the giver of life and death; Nimrod had two prisoners brought out, executed one and pardoned the other, and claimed the same power. Abraham replied, "Well, my lord God makes the sun rise from the East. Can you make it rise from the West?" Confounded and humiliated, Nimrod banished him from Mesopotamia.

## Mecca and the Kaaba

The belief recorded in the source is that Abraham eventually settled in Mecca and constructed a stone sanctuary on the same foundation where his ancestor Adam had built one as a place to worship the one God. This structure is the Kaaba, which Islam considers mankind's first house of worship; elsewhere the article describes it as built by Abraham and his son Ishmael. The Muslim reading of the conflict with Nimrod, as the source presents it, is that it represents the divine triumph of logic and reason over self-indulgent narcissism, and the quintessential case of monotheism against polytheism — good against evil, a recurring theme in Islamic history and tradition.

## The three angels

The article also recounts the episode, accepted in all three major religions, in which Abraham and his wife Sarah, childless and aged 86, were visited by three angels who told them they would bear a son. Before hearing their news, Abraham received them with warm hospitality, washing their feet and feeding them his best bread, milk and finest calf — paying forward the kindness shown to him by strangers during his travels after his exile from Mesopotamia. Hashim, and all Islamic scholars since, held up this behaviour as exemplary and urged all followers to practise the same kindness toward visitors.
