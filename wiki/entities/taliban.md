# Taliban

The Taliban is the Islamist militant movement that fought U.S. and coalition forces in Afghanistan. In Badlands Media's Benghazi reporting, the Taliban appears as an end recipient of American-supplied weapons that were originally routed through the Libya arms pipeline.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

## Stinger missiles in Kunar province

Citing the New York Post, Badlands recounts that on July 25, 2012, Taliban fighters in Kunar province targeted a U.S. Army CH-47 Chinook helicopter with a new-generation Stinger missile.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) The missile failed to detonate properly; the pilot recovered control and made a hard landing, and an explosive ordnance disposal team recovered a fragment of the missile casing bearing a complete serial number.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) That serial number tracked the Stinger back to a lot recently signed out by the [[cia|CIA]] — not to stocks from the anti-Soviet jihad of the 1980s.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

Special Operations sources cited in the same reporting believed the Stinger came from the same lot the CIA turned over to the Qataris in early 2011, weapons that [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s [[state-department|State Department]] had intended for anti-Gaddafi forces in Libya.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) Those sources believed [[qatar|Qatar]] delivered between 50 and 60 of those Stingers to the Taliban in early 2012, along with an additional 200 SA-24 Igla-S surface-to-air missiles.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) Qatar was also expected to hold five Taliban commanders released from Guantanamo for a year before permitting them to return to Afghanistan.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

## Significance in the Badlands account

Badlands describes this transfer as treason: American weapons, approved for sale under the authority of the Secretary of State, ending up in the hands of enemy fighters killing U.S. troops.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) The episode is presented as one of the mid-2012 exposures — alongside the intercepted Libyan weapons ships and the reporting on CIA smuggling through [[turkey|Turkey]] — that, in the article's telling, made it urgent for the operation to be shut down before Congress learned the whole truth.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

## Wrongful detention designation (2026)

On March 9, 2026, Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] designated [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] a "State Sponsor of Wrongful Detention" in response to the Taliban's detention of US nationals and other foreign citizens on grounds Washington considered unjustified or politically motivated.[2] The [[state-department|State Department]] classification is applied to governments that detain foreign nationals to obtain diplomatic leverage or concessions, and enables increased pressure and coordination to secure detainees' release.[2] Badlands commentary has separately counted the Taliban among the actors that at some point pledged loyalty to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and, through him, to the cause of regional peace.[2]

## Russian partnership (2026)

On May 27, 2026, the Taliban government of [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] concluded a military-technical cooperation agreement with [[russia|Russia]] at an international security forum in the Moscow region, following a meeting between Afghan Defense Minister [[mohammad-yaqub|Mohammad Yaqub]] and Russian Security Council Secretary [[sergei-shoigu|Sergei Shoigu]].[3] Shoigu had said on May 14 that Moscow had built a dialogue with the Taliban and was developing a full partnership with Kabul, and he publicly urged Western states to unfreeze Afghan assets and take responsibility for post-conflict reconstruction.[3] The accord is treated at [[russia-afghanistan-military-cooperation-agreement-2026|the 2026 Russia–Afghanistan military cooperation agreement]].[3]

Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] framed the deal as a continuation of August 2024, when President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] invited a Taliban delegation to the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum, where its members drew attention touring the venue with shopping bags and newly bought dress shoes.[3] He noted reporting that same week that the Biden administration's [[state-department|State Department]] had "mistakenly" sent the Taliban $239 million, and recalled that under the 2020 [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]] signed by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], the Taliban hunted down and killed the ISIS-K operatives responsible for the Abbey Gate bombing that killed 13 US soldiers during the 2021 withdrawal.[3]

## The 2021 evacuation

Testimony delivered to the [[house-foreign-affairs-committee]] in March 2023 by Marine sergeant [[tyler-vargas-andrews]] described how the [[state-department]] turned Afghans away from the Kabul airport during the American evacuation, in his words "condemning them to death" at the hands of the Taliban.[4] The same hearing addressed the [[abbey-gate-bombing|Abbey Gate bombing]] of August 26, 2021, in which thirteen American service members were killed.[4]

## Post-withdrawal aid flows (2021–2023)

A report issued in early November 2023 by [[john-sopko|John Sopko]] of the [[special-inspector-general-for-afghanistan-reconstruction|Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction]] found that Afghanistan had received roughly $11 billion in U.S. foreign aid since the country's collapse in August 2021, delivered in part as cash shipments of about $80 million every 10 to 14 days since the Taliban's takeover.[5] The [[united-nations|United Nations]] assured Sopko that the money sat in designated U.N. accounts at a private bank and was not deposited in the central bank or provided to the Taliban.[5] The report nevertheless noted that the Taliban had stolen foreign aid before and had prevented the poorest members of the population from receiving aid designated for them, using methods including siphoning cash from U.N. shipments, collecting royalties, and charging fees on cash shipments.[5]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that after billions of dollars of U.S. military equipment were left in Kabul, the Taliban used that equipment to hunt down and largely eradicate what remained of ISIS in the country — behaviour he read as consistent with the original structure of the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]] signed under President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], and as evidence for [[devolution|Devolution]] and "Sovereign Alliance–Regions of Responsibility" theories.[5]


## Russian recognition (July 2025)

In July 2025 Russia became the first nation to formally recognize the Taliban's Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan, opening an embassy and treating Kabul's de facto rulers as legitimate partners for the first time since the movement's return to power in 2021.[6] Taliban envoy Gul Hassan presented copies of his credentials to Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Andrey Rudenko on July 1, and Moscow made recognition official two days later, following an April decision to remove the Taliban from Russia's national list of terrorist organizations after more than twenty years.[6] The Taliban welcomed the gesture as a possible catalyst for wider international engagement.[6]

Badlands commentary argued that Americans had been extensively misled about the movement's nature, relaying Brian Lupo's account of fighting in Afghanistan from 2010 to 2013 in which he doubted whether his adversaries were Taliban at all rather than foot soldiers of drug cartels working the poppy fields near his forward operating base — fields the commentary alleged the [[cia|CIA]] used to fuel the American opium epidemic.[6] The piece credited the Taliban with shutting down those cartels after 2021, driving out ISIS and al-Qaeda, and hunting down the ISIS-K operatives responsible for the Abbey Gate bombing that killed thirteen U.S. service members, and praised the movement for honoring the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]] with President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] despite having no reason to expect his return to office.[6] See [[russian-recognition-of-the-islamic-emirate-of-afghanistan|Russian Recognition of the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan]].[6]

## Post-withdrawal US funding (SIGAR, August 2023)

A report by the [[special-inspector-general-for-afghanistan-reconstruction|Special Inspector General for Afghan Reconstruction]] found that the Biden administration had provided $2.35 billion to [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] over the two years following the 2021 withdrawal, despite the country's return to Taliban rule.[7] Roughly $1.7 billion of that sum "remained available for possible disbursement" at the time of writing, and reporting on the findings suggested a sizable portion would likely reach the group's coffers.[7] According to the report, the Taliban viewed international aid as a "revenue stream" and were "comfortable accepting foreign support insofar as they can closely monitor the organizations, including restricting and controlling them, and claim some credit for the provision of the benefits."[7] SIGAR head [[john-sopko|John Sopko]] had told the House Foreign Affairs Committee in April that he "cannot assure this committee or the American taxpayer we are not currently funding the Taliban," and accused the administration of obstructing his inquiries.[7]

[[badlands-media|Badlands]] writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] connected the funding to the analysis of [[just-human|Just Human]] on the Kabul withdrawal, recalling the theory that the withdrawal represented the consummation of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]], which was structured to return the Taliban to power in Kabul while funding and arming them sufficiently to act as a long-term bulwark against ISIS extremists.[7] That the Taliban regained Kabul, acquired billions of dollars of US military equipment, and then received a further $2 billion in funding was, in his framing, a strong [[devolution|Devolution]] proof.[7]

## Killing of the ISIS-K Abbey Gate mastermind (2023)

In April 2023 senior U.S. administration officials said the [[isis-k|ISIS-K]] leader responsible for planning the 2021 [[abbey-gate-bombing|Abbey Gate]] attack at Kabul airport — which killed 13 U.S. service members and more than 100 Afghan civilians — had been killed by the Taliban in Afghanistan.[8] The [[white-house|White House]] declined to name the man but described him as the "mastermind" of the assault on one of the main evacuation entry points.[8] Officials said they had learned of the death weeks earlier and needed time to confirm it, adding that government experts held "high confidence" that he "was indeed the key individual responsible."[8] The Taliban did not inform the United States of the killing; Washington reached its determination through its own intelligence gathering and monitoring of regional threats and actors.[8]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that [[just-human|Just Human]] had projected as early as August 2021 that the Taliban had been charged by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], under the framework of the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]], with helping eliminate the globalist-funded terrorist threat of [[isis|ISIS]] — and that they were now doing so.[8] He attributed the relative silence of the [[media-industrial-complex|Media Industrial Complex]] on Afghanistan to the fact that events there were going "pretty well."[8]

## Delegation to the St. Petersburg forum (June 2024)

In June 2024 a Taliban delegation — outlawed in Russia at the time — led by Minister of Labor and Social Affairs Abdul Manan Omari arrived in [[russia|Russia]] to take part in the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum (SPIEF), an Afghan embassy official told [[tass|TASS]].[10] Zamir Kabulov, Russian special presidential envoy for Afghanistan and director of the [[russian-foreign-ministry|Foreign Ministry]]'s Second Asia Department, said the delegation would also include the head of the Afghan Chamber of Commerce and Industry, and that the Russian foreign and justice ministries had reported to President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] that the Taliban could be removed from the list of banned organizations.[10] Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] said the initiative reflected objective reality.[10] The forum ran June 5–8, 2024 under the theme "The Formation of New Areas of Growth as the Cornerstone of a Multipolar World," organized by the Roscongress Foundation with TASS as information partner.[10]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] used the visit to argue that Western media coverage of the Taliban had been systematically deceptive, pointing to [[zak-paine|Zak Paine]]'s interview with the British traveler Lord Miles Routledge, who described his 2023 detention in Afghanistan as closer to being an honored guest — housed with servants, given internet access, taken to a shooting range, served tea and delicacies, and given a private tour of the Tora Bora caves.[10] Routledge, who refers to his hosts as his "Talibros," also recounted the Taliban capturing ISIS and Al Qaeda operatives, some of them Western nationals radicalized online who had no real understanding of the Quran.[10] The contributor compared these accounts to his own interview with Mike Harris, financial editor for Veterans Today and a 2006 Republican primary candidate for governor of Arizona, who has traveled to Afghanistan for years attempting to help the Taliban build a real economy so that Afghans would be less vulnerable to recruitment by Western intelligence into mercenary groups such as [[isis|ISIS]] and [[al-qaeda|Al Qaeda]].[10]

He tied this to [[just-human|Just Human]]'s research on the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]], under which the Taliban pledged to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] that they would hunt ISIS, Al Qaeda and other [[cia|CIA]]-linked mercenaries in exchange for U.S. withdrawal — a promise he argued they kept by killing the [[isis-k|ISIS-K]] operatives responsible for the 2021 [[abbey-gate-bombing|Abbey Gate]] attack, and by shutting down the CIA's heroin network after regaining control of the country.[10] He further noted that the Russian Foreign Ministry had reported it would work with the Taliban to hunt CIA-backed terrorists, while the Taliban stated they had traveled to Russia seeking economic investment.[10]

## Second anniversary of the return to power (August 2023)

The Taliban marked the second anniversary of its return to power with a public holiday on August 15, 2023, celebrating the takeover of Kabul and the establishment of what it described as security across the country under an "Islamic system."[9] Movement spokesman [[zabihullah-mujahid|Zabihullah Mujahid]] said in a statement, "On the second anniversary of the conquest of Kabul, we would like to congratulate the mujahid nation of Afghanistan and ask them to thank Almighty Allah for this great victory," adding that overall security was ensured, the entire territory was managed under a single leadership, an Islamic system was in place and "everything is explained from the angle of Sharia."[9] Security was tight in the capital as soldiers stepped up checks, and convoys of Taliban members gathered at Massoud Square near the abandoned US embassy building, some carrying weapons while others took selfies as anthems played and boys sold the movement's white flag inscribed with the Islamic declaration of faith.[9]

## Russian delisting (October 2024)

In October 2024, [[vladimir-putin|Putin]]'s envoy for Afghanistan [[zamir-kabulov|Zamir Kabulov]] said Russia had made "a principle decision" to remove the Taliban from its list of terrorist organizations, telling state media TASS that the foreign ministry and security service were "putting finishing legal touches" on the removal and that the final decision would "hopefully" be announced soon.[11] The group had been added to Russia's blacklist in 2003 for backing separatists in the North Caucasus.[11]

At the annual diplomatic forum on Afghanistan in Moscow, at which Taliban Foreign Minister Amir Khan Muttaqi was present, Russian Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] called on Western countries to lift sanctions on the Taliban, urging them "to acknowledge responsibility for the post-conflict reconstruction of Afghanistan, lift sanctions restrictions, and return Kabul's appropriated assets."[11] Lavrov praised the current Afghan leadership for its fight against drugs and the Islamic State without expressly naming the Taliban.[11]

## Bid to join BRICS (2024)

In September 2024, despite not being formally recognized by any country, the Taliban government of Afghanistan announced that it was seeking to join the [[brics|BRICS]] economic forum.[12] Deputy spokesman Hamdullah Fitrat said that "countries with major resources and the world's biggest economies are associated with the BRICS forum, especially Russia, India, and China," and that Kabul was "keen to expand our relations and participate in the economic forums of the BRICS."[12] The Afghanistan Ministry of Foreign Affairs said it hoped to be invited to the BRICS summit scheduled for October 22–24 in the Russian city of Kazan, but that there was "no information so far" on whether the Taliban could attend.[12]

While no state has extended formal recognition, China and Russia have come closest, hosting Taliban delegations for talks and maintaining embassies in Kabul after the US–NATO withdrawal of August 2021; both have shown willingness to invest in Afghanistan and tap its natural resources, and both have welcomed the Taliban's fight against the rival Islamic State Khorasan (ISIS-K).[12] Analysts quoted in the coverage suggested the Taliban's accession would hand Washington "low-hanging fruit" with which to denigrate BRICS on the world stage, particularly amid US frustration at deepening China–Russia–India cooperation.[12] Badlands Media commentary treated the move as evidence that emergent sovereign states can bypass the international rules-based order, the [[united-nations|United Nations]] and [[nato|NATO]], escaping dollar-denominated sanctions and IMF debt by joining an alternative financial architecture.[12]

## The opium ban and the 2024 UNODC report

After the Taliban banned narcotics production in 2022, opium cultivation in Afghanistan — long the world's dominant supplier — fell by 95 percent in a single year, contributing to a roughly 75 percent decline in global production even as output in Myanmar rose 36 percent.[13] The [[united-nations-office-on-drugs-and-crime|United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime]] warned in its World Drug Report published in late June 2024 that the crash could drive up overdose deaths as heroin users switched to synthetic opioids already proving lethal in Europe, and that heroin purity was expected to decline.[13] Preliminary field observations suggested a possible slight increase in cultivation in 2024, but not a return to pre-ban levels.[13]

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the report as an inadvertent confession, writing that critics were complaining because [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] turned control of Afghanistan over to the Taliban and "the Talibros immediately ended the heroin industry," which he identified as the reason for the subsequent explosion in synthetic fentanyl.[13] He argued that blaming the Taliban for overdose deaths caused by pharmaceutical-grade synthetics inverted responsibility, and that the corporate press was unintentionally making the case for prosecuting Big Pharma.[13]

## Russian rapprochement, May 2024

By May 2024 Russia had moved further than any other major power toward normalizing relations with the Taliban.[14] Analyst [[andrew-korybko|Andrew Korybko]] catalogued a rapid sequence of signals: a Russian diplomat's statement on May 16 that the Afghan Taliban were no longer Russia's enemy; a May 17 announcement by Deputy Prime Minister Alexei Overchuk that Afghanistan would expand the range of goods it exports to Russia; a May 24 assessment by the FSB director that the Taliban could stabilize Afghanistan if left to its own devices; and, on May 27, both an invitation for the Taliban to attend the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum and a proposal from Russian ministries to [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] to remove the group from the terrorist list.[14] Korybko argued the driving motive was a strategic energy deal with Pakistan that would complete Russia's "Ummah Pivot" and Greater Eurasian Partnership, and that Moscow now regarded the Taliban as a regional security provider containing [[isis-k|ISIS-K]].[14] He noted the Taliban nonetheless remained international outcasts over their failure to form an ethno-politically inclusive government and their treatment of women, with engagement proceeding on pragmatic economic and security grounds.[14]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the developments as [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] signals bearing the imprint of the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]], describing Russia's reliance on the Taliban against ISIS-K as a continuation of [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s post-Doha vision, highlighting the Taliban's role brokering Russia–Pakistan energy talks, and framing Moscow's outreach as built around multipolarity designed to avoid vassal-state dependence on major powers such as China.[14]

## Reception of China's ambassador (September 2023)

On Wednesday, September 13, 2023, the Taliban hailed the arrival of China's new ambassador to Afghanistan, [[zhao-sheng|Zhao Sheng]], with fanfare, saying it was a sign for other nations to come forward and establish relations with them.[15] Zhao's car swept through the tree-lined driveway of the Presidential Palace escorted by a police convoy; he was greeted by uniformed troops and met top-ranking officials including Mohammad Hassan Akhund, who heads the administration, and Foreign Affairs Minister [[amir-khan-muttaqi|Amir Khan Muttaqi]].[15] It was the first time since the Taliban takeover that an ambassador to Kabul had been given such lavish protocol.[15]

The Taliban seized power in August 2021 as U.S. and [[nato|NATO]] forces withdrew after two decades of war; their leaders remain under sanctions, no country recognizes them as Afghanistan's legitimate rulers, and the country's United Nations seat is still held by the former Western-backed government led by Ashraf Ghani.[15] Only a handful of nations maintain working diplomatic missions in Afghanistan, among them China — the world's second-largest economy — with both sides open about a desire for closer ties, especially commercial ones.[15]

## Rejection of Trump's Bagram demand (September 2025)

In September 2025 the Taliban government rejected U.S. President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s bid to retake [[bagram-air-base|Bagram Air Base]], four years after the chaotic American withdrawal from Afghanistan left the sprawling facility in Taliban hands.[16] Chief Taliban spokesman [[zabihullah-mujahid|Zabihullah Mujahid]] urged Washington to adopt a policy of "realism and rationality," recalling that under the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]] the United States pledged that "it will not use or threaten force against the territorial integrity or political independence of Afghanistan, nor interfere in its internal affairs."[16] Hamdullah Fitrat, deputy spokesman of the Taliban-run Afghan government, said Kabul had made clear in all negotiations that the country's "independence and territorial integrity are of the utmost importance."[16] The rejection followed Trump's warning that if the base were not returned, unspecified "BAD THINGS ARE GOING TO HAPPEN!!!"[16] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the confrontation as staged "WrestleMania kayfabe" between two sides that were in fact on friendly terms.[16]

## January 2025 prisoner exchange and bounty threat

In late January 2025 the Taliban, which controls the government of Afghanistan, released two Americans in exchange for a Taliban figure in a prisoner swap, part of its continuing effort to obtain international legitimacy.[17] The Americans were identified as [[ryan-corbett|Ryan Corbett]], detained in August 2022 while on a business trip, and William McKenty, as confirmed by the [[house-foreign-affairs-committee|House Foreign Affairs Committee]] in a statement released on January 21.[17] That statement referred to "the other Americans who remain wrongfully detained and held hostage in Afghanistan" without providing a number.[17]

On Saturday, January 25, Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] indicated that Washington might place a "very big bounty" to locate top Taliban leadership after learning there may be more American hostages than previously reported.[17] Billions of dollars in international funds remained frozen, and the Taliban continued to seek normalized relations with the United States while many countries still refused to recognize its rule.[17]

## Opium eradication

The Taliban government drastically reduced opium cultivation in [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] — a country that until recently produced 90% of the world's heroin — after announcing a prohibition on poppy farming in April 2022, citing religious belief and the social harms of opioids.[18] Geospatial analysis by Alcis found production down roughly 80% nationally within a year and 99% in Helmand Province, with enforcement carried out by counter-narcotics brigades armed with little more than sticks.[18] The success prompted questions about the seriousness of two decades of U.S. eradication efforts and raised expert concern that shrinking heroin supply could accelerate [[fentanyl-crisis|fentanyl]] use.[18] See [[taliban-opium-eradication-campaign|Taliban opium eradication campaign]].[18]
