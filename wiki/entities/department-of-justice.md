# Department of Justice

The United States Department of Justice (DOJ) is the federal executive department responsible for the enforcement of federal law and the administration of justice. Badlands Media's "America for Sale — Part 2" treats the DOJ as an institution captured at its highest levels under [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] and only partially reclaimed under [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## Alleged settlement slush fund

Internal DOJ documents obtained by the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] under Chairman [[bob-goodlatte|Bob Goodlatte]] were said to confirm the existence of a departmental "slush fund," with officials deliberately excluding conservative groups from eligibility.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) The committee concluded the DOJ had subverted Congress's budget authority by using settlements extracted from financial institutions to funnel money to left-wing activist groups — roughly $1 billion over two years.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) The article argues Obama used the department not only to threaten political enemies but to shake down corporations.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

The DOJ also announced the corruption charges that led Swiss police to arrest much of [[fifa]]'s leadership in 2015, and it was the DOJ that levied the $780 million penalty on [[ubs]] to avert prosecution over its assistance to American tax evaders.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## Workaround under Sessions

Because the author regarded the DOJ and [[fbi|FBI]] as thoroughly corrupt when Trump took office, he credits Attorney General [[jeff-sessions|Jeff Sessions]] with bypassing both: assigning Inspector General [[michael-horowitz|Michael Horowitz]] to investigate in place of the FBI, and U.S. Attorney [[john-huber|John Huber]] of Utah to prosecute in place of the Washington DOJ.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) A [[qanon|Q]] drop encapsulated this as "IG = FBI, Huber = DOJ (no DC)."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) Sessions is also credited with ending the settlement slush fund.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## Epstein Files Transparency Act production (2026)

In February 2026 the department sent [[congress|Congress]] a formal production letter stating that it had complied with the [[epstein-files-transparency-act|Epstein Files Transparency Act]] and describing a review and redaction process aimed at protecting victim-identifying information.[2] DOJ said it had identified more than 6 million potentially responsive pages and released over 3 million responsive pages in a single day along with more than 2,000 videos and roughly 180,000 images, bringing total production to nearly 3.5 million pages, while withholding or redacting about 200,000 pages on privilege grounds.[2] The letter included a list of "politically exposed persons" referenced in the files, which the department and press accounts stressed was not itself an allegation.[2]

The production drew bipartisan criticism, with Rep. [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] questioning Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]]'s accountability and Rep. [[nancy-mace|Nancy Mace]] reporting that co-conspirator memos remained hidden.[2]

## Fulton County records seizure

The department also faced litigation over its use of election materials seized from [[fulton-county|Fulton County]], Georgia, in January 2026. The [[naacp|NAACP]] and allied NGOs filed a Rule 41(g) emergency motion seeking to confine federal use of the data to the criminal investigation described in the warrant and to bar its use for national voter roll programs, immigration enforcement, interagency sharing or public disclosure.[2]

## Request for House Intelligence Committee records (March 2026)

In March 2026 the Justice Department requested records from the [[house-intelligence-committee|House Intelligence Committee]] tied to former [[cia|CIA]] Director [[john-brennan|John Brennan]] as part of a reported criminal inquiry.[3] The request centered on documents generated or held by the committee during prior intelligence oversight activities and was described as a "rare instance" of the executive branch seeking access to legislative branch materials in a prosecutorial context.[3] Coverage noted the move signaled potential escalation beyond routine information-gathering, though no charges had been announced and the scope of the inquiry remained unclear, and that it could raise separation-of-powers issues, negotiations, or legal action over compliance and the limits of executive authority.[3] The committee's vote to cooperate split along party lines.[3]

## Fulton County election filings and Epstein releases (2026)

On February 10, 2026, the Department of Justice filed court documents in federal court disclosing that the [[fbi|FBI]] is actively investigating potential improprieties in [[fulton-county|Fulton County]]'s handling of ballots from the 2020 presidential election.[4] The affidavits, authored by Special Agent [[hugh-evans|Hugh Evans]], describe missing ballot images, inconsistent totals, chain-of-custody problems, duplicate images, missing SHA verification hashes, and absentee ballots without mailing creases, and state that the records themselves may prove violations of federal election law.[4] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that the seized records are now in DOJ custody and argued the unsealed warrant reads like a review of the Why We Vote catalog on Georgia.[4]

In early February 2026 the department also released millions of additional pages of documents related to [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]]'s federal investigations, including previously redacted materials, triggering the political fight over the [[epstein-narrative|Epstein narrative]].[4]

## Cities Church investigation (January 2026)

The Department placed former CNN host [[don-lemon|Don Lemon]] "on notice" for potential federal charges after he was alleged to have joined a mob of activists that stormed [[cities-church|Cities Church]] in St. Paul, Minnesota during a Sunday service.[5] Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]] said the incident could violate the [[ku-klux-klan-act|Ku Klux Klan Act]] and the [[face-act|FACE Act]], emphasizing zero tolerance and noting that disrupting religious services is not protected speech, with the Civil Rights Division and the [[fbi|FBI]] investigating.[5] Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] spoke directly with the affected pastor, affirming that attacks on law enforcement and intimidation of Christians would face the "full force of federal law."[5]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the Department's use of the KKK Act against figures like Lemon reversed years of the statute being deployed against conservatives, calling it a boomerang onto "the same political class that spent years weaponizing it."[5]

## National Fraud Enforcement Division (2026)

On January 28, 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced the nomination of [[colin-mcdonald|Colin McDonald]] as the first Assistant Attorney General for National Fraud Enforcement, heading a new [[national-fraud-enforcement-division|division]] created within the Department to combat fraud against taxpayers and federal programs.[6] The role targets large-scale misuse of federal funds and related financial crimes; primary reporting did not describe it as limited to election fraud, though election-related financial crimes may fall within its scope.[6] Trump justified the division by citing fraud schemes uncovered in Minnesota and California allegedly costing hundreds of billions of taxpayer dollars.[6]

## Hur recordings FOIA litigation (2026)

The Department obtained audio recordings and transcripts of [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s 2016–2017 private conversations with ghostwriter [[mark-zwonitzer|Mark Zwonitzer]] as part of Special Counsel [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]]'s investigation into Biden's handling of classified documents.[7] It initially withheld most of the records under FOIA exemptions but later agreed to release redacted versions to the [[heritage-foundation|Heritage Foundation]], [[mike-howell|Mike Howell]], and the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]].[7]

Biden intervened in the litigation to block the disclosure; a lower court denied his preliminary injunction, and in July 2026 the [[us-court-of-appeals-for-the-dc-circuit|D.C. Circuit]] denied his emergency appeal, citing the public interest in assessing whether the government had gone easy on a former vice president and extending an administrative stay until August 3.[7]

## Election oversight and the Brennan probe (April 2026)

In April 2026 the Department requested access to roughly 865,000 Detroit ballots as part of a federal review of election procedures, warning that legal action could follow if local officials did not comply; the demand was tied to federal record-retention and election oversight laws, reportedly including Civil Rights Act provisions requiring jurisdictions to preserve election materials.[8] Detroit officials raised concerns about the scope and logistics of turning over such a volume of ballots, including storage, chain-of-custody, and administrative burden.[8]

In a related development, the Department selected [[joseph-digenova|Joseph DiGenova]] — who previously represented President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in challenges to the 2020 election — to take over the criminal investigation into former [[cia|CIA]] Director [[john-brennan|John Brennan]], following the removal of prosecutor Maria Medetis Long.[8] DiGenova was placed in a senior advisory role to acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]], overseeing the case out of the Southern District of Florida, with former Cannon law clerk Chris DeLorenz also joining.[8]

## June 2026 actions

On June 2, 2026, the Department said it had secured a superseding indictment against the [[southern-poverty-law-center|Southern Poverty Law Center]], adding allegations that the organization used roughly $4.1 million in tax-exempt donations to pay informants embedded in hate organizations who then took part in recruiting members and obtaining materials linked to cross burnings and Klan regalia; the SPLC faces 11 counts of wire fraud, bank fraud and conspiracy to commit money laundering and has pleaded not guilty.[9] The updated charging document reportedly dropped language about "false or misleading" statements to banks following the Supreme Court's ruling in *Thompson v. United States*.[9]

The same week the Department confirmed that [[kurt-olsen|Kurt Olsen]] had joined the US Attorney's Office for the Southern District of Florida as a senior attorney, reporting to US Attorney [[jason-reding-quinones|Jason Reding Quinones]] on the [[grand-conspiracy-probe|grand conspiracy inquiry]] into whether prior investigations and prosecutions of President [[donald-trump|Trump]] amounted to criminal conspiracy; that effort is supervised by [[joe-digenova|Joe diGenova]], counselor to acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]].[9] Separately, the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of Michigan charged two [[national-institutes-of-health|NIH]] researchers with conspiracy to smuggle monkeypox into the United States and making false statements.[9]

## Internal doubts about the Mar-a-Lago raid

An August 10, 2022 email from senior DOJ official [[patty-stemler|Patty Stemler]] to National Security Division attorney [[sophia-brill|Sophia Brill]], sent two days after the [[fbi|FBI]] raid on [[mar-a-lago|Mar-a-Lago]], questioned whether [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had declassified the seized documents and observed that "the President as Commander in Chief" is not necessarily bound by standard declassification procedures, by analogy to the pardon power.[10] The email — obtained by Just the News and discovered during the Department's own review of federal law enforcement actions — underscored early internal doubts about the legal footing of a search personally approved by Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]], which followed a May 2022 letter from Trump attorney [[evan-corcoran|Evan Corcoran]] asserting broad declassification authority.[10]

Badlands treated the disclosure as a recurring signal that the federal [[weaponization-of-government|weaponization]] investigation remains active.[10]

## Cuba and biolab enforcement (2026)

In May 2026 the Department's indictment of former Cuban president [[raul-castro|Raúl Castro]] was cited as part of intensified pressure on [[cuba|Cuba]] surrounding the [[office-of-foreign-assets-control|OFAC]] probe of the [[nuestra-america-convoy|Nuestra America Convoy]].[10] In the same period federal prosecutors under [[sigal-chattah|Sigal Chattah]] dismissed the firearms complaint against [[ori-solomon|Ori Solomon]] in the [[las-vegas-biolab|Las Vegas biolab]] case without prejudice, citing the interests of justice.[10]

## Election integrity and bar-discipline litigation (May 2026)

In May 2026 the Department participated in [[white-house|White House]] interagency talks to expand federal involvement in voter verification and mail-in ballot rules alongside the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] and the [[united-states-postal-service|US Postal Service]].[11] Civil Rights Division head [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]] and her deputies took part, and internal disclosures named associate deputy attorney general Aakash Singh, principal associate deputy attorney general Trent McCotter, and counselor to the attorney general Henry Whitaker among the participants.[11] Under the executive order [[ensuring-citizenship-verification-and-integrity-in-federal-elections|"Ensuring Citizenship Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections"]], DOJ is directed to prioritize prosecution of state and local officials who distribute ballots to ineligible voters.[11]

The same month, the Department filed a complaint against [[dc-bar|District of Columbia]] disciplinary authorities alleging they were improperly using the attorney discipline process to regulate the official actions of federal government lawyers, seeking to nullify the prosecution of former Assistant Attorney General [[jeff-clark|Jeff Clark]] over internal deliberations related to potential fraud in the 2020 presidential election.[11] Officials argued the disciplinary process improperly targets executive branch deliberations and risks restricting federal attorneys from providing candid legal advice, and framed the action as advancing President [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s executive order on ending the [[weaponization-of-government|weaponization of the federal government]] and a related memorandum on preventing abuses of the legal system and federal courts.[11]

## February 2026 voter-roll lawsuits

On 26 February 2026 the Department filed federal lawsuits against Utah, Oklahoma, Kentucky, West Virginia, and New Jersey for failing to provide complete voter registration lists as required under federal voting laws, bringing the running total to 29 states plus the District of Columbia.[12] Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] and the Department's Civil Rights Division stated that the actions enforced the [[national-voter-registration-act|National Voter Registration Act]] and the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]] to ensure accurate and transparent voter rolls.[12]

## Grants to Fani Willis

Reports in February 2026 confirmed that the Biden-era Justice Department had awarded approximately $18 million in federal grants to the Fulton County district attorney's office headed by [[fani-willis|Fani Willis]] during the period in which she was prosecuting former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in Georgia.[12] The funds were distributed through DOJ grant programs related to violence reduction and victim services, and public records did not show that they were specifically earmarked for the Trump prosecution.[12]

## March 2026 ISIS prosecution and Arizona election probe

In March 2026 the Department announced terrorism-related charges against two [[isis|ISIS]] supporters accused of attempting to detonate explosive devices during protests.[13] Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pamela Bondi]] called it "an alleged ISIS-inspired act of terrorism that could have killed American citizens," and Deputy Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] said the men had allegedly sought to inflict mass casualties in service to ISIS "with the hope of exceeding the carnage of the Boston Marathon bombing," pledging prosecution to the fullest extent.[13]

In the same period the [[fbi|FBI]] obtained [[maricopa-county|Maricopa County]] election records through a grand jury subpoena as part of a federal criminal investigation into election administration from 2020 through 2024 — an inquiry preceded by Rep. [[abe-hamadeh|Abe Hamadeh]]'s request that the Department investigate [[runbeck-election-services|Runbeck Election Services]], and described as part of ongoing federal work on election issues in multiple states.[13]

## April 2026: Senate records request in the RussiaGate probe

Earlier in 2026 the Justice Department requested transcripts and records from two Senate committees involving former [[cia|CIA]] Director [[john-brennan|John Brennan]], setting a February 23 deadline for compliance.[14] As of the following Thursday night the [[united-states-senate|Senate]] had not transmitted the requested materials, a delay reported to be affecting part of an investigation based in Fort Pierce, Florida.[14] Aides to Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] said he was seeking unanimous consent to release the records while discussing the matter with Democrats to avoid a floor vote, and prosecutors had recently been allowed to review one Senate Intelligence Committee report in a secure room.[14] Former U.S. Attorney [[joseph-digenova|Joseph diGenova]] was reported as scheduled to begin work the following Monday as a lead prosecutor on Russia-collusion matters.[14]

## April 2026: SPLC indictment

In April 2026 the Justice Department indicted the [[southern-poverty-law-center|Southern Poverty Law Center]] on an 11-count federal indictment in Montgomery, Alabama, charging wire fraud, bank fraud, and conspiracy to commit money laundering.[15] Prosecutors allege the organization defrauded donors by secretly funneling more than $3 million between 2014 and 2023 to paid informants and leaders inside the Ku Klux Klan, neo-Nazi groups, and other extremist organizations it publicly claimed to oppose.[15] Acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] said the SPLC "was not dismantling these groups. It was instead manufacturing the extremism it purports to oppose by paying sources to stoke racial hatred."[15] SPLC chief executive [[bryan-fair|Bryan Fair]] confirmed the probe and indictment and said the organization would not be "intimidated."[15]

## Monaco referral and the Lineberger indictment (2026)

In May 2026 the [[american-accountability-foundation|American Accountability Foundation]] referred former Deputy Attorney General [[lisa-monaco|Lisa Monaco]] to the department, alleging she shielded [[microsoft|Microsoft]] from cybersecurity enforcement before joining the company in 2025 as President of Global Affairs.[16] The referral asked departmental inspectors general to investigate possible ethics, recusal, and revolving-door violations by Monaco and other officials, and whether Microsoft faced [[false-claims-act|False Claims Act]] exposure; it also highlighted the department's [[civil-cyber-fraud-initiative|Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative]], which pursued other contractors for allegedly lesser misconduct.[16]

The referral coincided with the indictment of former federal prosecutor [[carmen-lineberger|Carmen Lineberger]], accused of emailing herself a sealed, unreleased portion of Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]]'s report on the classified-documents investigation into [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from her DOJ account, disguising the file under names such as "Bundt_Cake_Recipe.pdf."[16]

## Role in the election executive order litigation (2026)

President [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s executive order on election administration directs the Justice Department to prioritize investigations into state and local election officials who issue federal ballots to people deemed ineligible to vote.[17] In July 2026 a federal appeals court denied a Justice Department request to lift an injunction against key portions of the order in 23 Democrat-led states, leaving Judge Talwani's injunction intact while the litigation proceeds toward a likely [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruling.[17]

## EPA green energy referrals (2026)

In June 2026 [[environmental-protection-agency|EPA]] Administrator [[lee-zeldin|Lee Zeldin]] made criminal referrals to both the EPA inspector general and the Department of Justice alleging self-dealing in the distribution of Biden-era green energy grants funded by the [[inflation-reduction-act|Inflation Reduction Act]].[18] The referrals covered awards including the [[greenhouse-gas-reduction-fund|Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund]]'s $20 billion routed through eight nonprofit intermediaries, the $2 billion [[power-forward-communities|Power Forward Communities]] grant, and a $20 million award to [[young-gifted-and-green|Young, Gifted & Green]].[18] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] urged the department and other agencies to prosecute those responsible, writing "Prosecute all of them."[18]

## Reported end of the weaponization fund (2026)
The Trump administration was reported in June 2026 to be preparing to eliminate a Justice Department initiative known internally as the [[weaponization-fund|weaponization fund]], established to support investigations and legal actions tied to alleged political abuse by federal agencies during prior administrations.[19] Officials reportedly viewed the effort as politically burdensome and legally difficult to sustain as attention shifted to broader institutional reforms and budget priorities, with critics arguing it had created expectations of sweeping criminal accountability that proved hard to deliver in court.[19] The reported move came amid wider restructuring across the department and renewed debate in Trump-aligned circles over whether systemic reform or high-profile prosecutorial action should be the primary strategy.[19]

## SPLC superseding indictment (June 2026)

The Justice Department filed a superseding indictment against the [[southern-poverty-law-center|Southern Poverty Law Center]] on June 2, 2026, charging the organization with wire fraud, bank fraud, false statements to a federally insured bank, and conspiracy to commit money laundering.[20] Prosecutors allege that former SPLC Intelligence Project director [[heidi-beirich|Heidi Beirich]], identified as "Employee-2," directed more than $1.2 million in donor funds over roughly two decades to an informant known as "F-9" who was embedded in the neo-Nazi [[national-alliance|National Alliance]], with about $140,000 flowing into joint accounts the two shared between 2015 and 2021.[20] The indictment further alleges the SPLC paid informant [[randolph-dilloway|Randolph Dilloway]] $6,000 to falsely claim responsibility for a 2014 burglary at the National Alliance's West Virginia headquarters, and contends the organization financially supported genuine extremist groups while publicly denouncing them and labeling conservative organizations "hate groups."[20]

## National Fraud Enforcement Division (April 2026)

On April 7, 2026, Acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] announced the launch of the [[national-fraud-enforcement-division|National Fraud Enforcement Division]], adding 93 dedicated prosecutors across every federal judicial district to work exclusively on fraud cases.[21] Blanche said the Department was already managing more than 8,000 ongoing fraud matters, describing them as only part of a much broader problem of abuse, and said the initiative would coordinate closely with the [[task-force-to-eliminate-fraud|Task Force to Eliminate Fraud]] led by Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] as a "supercharging" of enforcement.[21]

## Fulton County 2020 election subpoena (2026)

In April 2026 the Department issued a grand jury subpoena to Fulton County, Georgia, seeking names, positions, addresses, and phone numbers for thousands of workers involved in the 2020 presidential election there, including volunteers, temporary poll workers, and bus drivers who operated mobile voting sites.[22] Fulton County moved to quash on May 4, 2026, calling the demand grossly overbroad and arguing the statute of limitations for related federal crimes had expired; commentary sympathetic to the Department noted that motions to quash grand jury subpoenas rarely succeed because grand juries investigate rather than adjudicate.[22] See [[fulton-county-2020-election-grand-jury-subpoena|the Fulton County 2020 election grand jury subpoena]].[22]


## National coordinator for human trafficking and child exploitation (2026)

In June 2026 Acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] appointed longtime federal prosecutor [[alessandra-serano|Alessandra Serano]] as the department's national coordinator for human trafficking and child exploitation cases.[23] The position consolidates oversight of cases handled across all 93 US Attorney's Offices as well as specialized units within the DOJ Criminal Division, and was elevated within the deputy attorney general's office; the coordinator also works with the Office of Justice Programs on victim services funding and is expected to help deliver an updated departmental strategy on [[child-trafficking|trafficking and exploitation]] within 120 days.[23] The appointment followed a department announcement of an investigation into the status of roughly 300,000 unaccompanied immigrant children, including concerns that some may have been exploited through labor or sex trafficking via so-called "super sponsors."[23]

## Criminal investigation of Jerome Powell (January 2026)

The Department opened a criminal investigation into [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] Chairman [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] related to his congressional testimony concerning renovations to the central bank's headquarters.[24] Powell publicly confirmed the investigation in a statement on Sunday, January 11, 2026, though no corresponding press release about the probe appeared on the Department's website.[24] The [[white-house|White House]] denied involvement in the matter.[24]

Several Republican lawmakers responded publicly, with Senator [[thom-tillis|Thom Tillis]] criticizing the probe and vowing to block confirmation of Federal Reserve nominees while it remained open, and House Financial Services Chair [[french-hill|French Hill]] offering a character defense of Powell.[24] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the Republican pushback as evidence that the investigation had struck at an institutional nerve, observing that the probe had "all the right people in a tizzy."[24]

## Antifa funding task force (March 2026)

In March 2026 the Justice Department was reported to be assembling a task force to probe [[antifa|antifa]]-linked funding, while US attorney offices were directed to assign domestic terrorism coordinators.[25] The effort paralleled a joint [[fbi|FBI]]–[[internal-revenue-service|IRS]] initiative, described as a "mission control command center" based at the FBI, to investigate nonprofits suspected of funding [[domestic-terrorism|domestic terrorism]] or political violence, with IRS Criminal Investigations agents on temporary one-year assignments.[25] Both followed a December memo from Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] instructing federal agencies to prioritize investigations of antifa and other extremist groups — citing potential violence, anti-American and anti-religious ideologies, and tax fraud — and to compile intelligence on such groups for the FBI, building in turn on a September executive order from [[donald-trump|President Trump]] on domestic terrorism issued after the 2025 assassination of [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]].[25]

## 2026 global insider trading indictments

In May 2026, the Department unsealed charges against thirty defendants in a decade-long insider trading scheme alleged to have netted tens of millions of dollars in illicit profits from confidential information on nearly 30 merger and acquisition deals taken from premier U.S. law firms.[26] Nineteen defendants were arrested for appearances in federal courts including Los Angeles, Fort Lauderdale, and New York, while two defendants located in [[russia|Russia]] and [[israel|Israel]] were considered fugitives; the investigation remains ongoing.[26] U.S. Attorney [[leah-foley|Leah B. Foley]] said the nation's financial markets and professional firms should be free of the fraud and breaches of duty alleged.[26] The case is covered in detail at [[2026-global-insider-trading-prosecution|the 2026 global insider trading prosecution]].[26]

## Trump classified map inquiry (2023)

The Justice Department's broad investigation into [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s handling of classified documents after he left office included a focus on a map containing sensitive intelligence information, according to April 2023 reporting.[27] Investigators, working under special counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]], questioned witnesses about whether Trump had displayed the map to aides and visitors, including aboard a plane and possibly to a journalist writing a book, while weighing charges that could include obstruction of justice.[27] Independent commentators noted the inquiry's public surfacing coincided with the [[discord-classified-documents-leak|Discord leak]] of more than 100 genuinely classified documents.[27]

## IRS whistleblower allegations in the Hunter Biden case (2023)

In June 2023 two [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]] whistleblowers alleged that the Department under Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] had buried evidence of [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s tax crimes and had stopped U.S. Attorney [[david-weiss|David Weiss]] from bringing charges in two different jurisdictions during the previous year.[28] Representative [[jason-smith|Jason Smith]] said Weiss had also been denied appointment as special counsel.[28] IRS recommendations for felony charges reportedly emerged from the Department as two misdemeanors, and investigators seeking a search warrant for the guest house of the former Vice President were rebuffed by prosecutor [[lesley-wolf|Lesley Wolf]].[28]

Separately, commentary in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] alleged that [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]] chief executive [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] effectively influenced Department enforcement priorities, noting that the DOJ announced a crackdown on short sellers of bank stocks about two weeks after Dimon publicly suggested one.[28]

## Clawback of January 6 defendant donations (2023)

In May 2023 the Department of Justice sought to recover donations made to January 6 defendants, an effort [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] contrasted with the [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]]-endorsed bail fund available to protesters during the 2020 George Floyd riots.[29] Associated Press reporting described the Department seeking to seize more than $25,000 raised by Texas resident [[daniel-goodwyn|Daniel Goodwyn]], who had promoted a donation website on [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]]'s former [[fox-news|Fox News]] show, and found across more than 1,000 January 6 cases that prosecutors were asking judges to enhance fines on top of prison sentences to offset supporter donations.[29] Prosecutors acknowledged that soliciting help with attorney expenses is not improper, but questioned in some cases where the money was going because many defendants had government-funded legal representation; most of the fundraising took place on [[givesendgo|GiveSendGo]] after defendants were barred from mainstream platforms.[29] Badlands commentator [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] characterized the practice as symbolic "human sacrifice" performed by the establishment political class in service of "the god of parasitic tyranny and authoritarian control."[29]


## Apple antitrust action (2024)

In March 2024 the department pursued an [[united-states-v-apple-antitrust-case|antitrust case against Apple]], seeking to compel the company to share its secure communications network with other networks.[30] The [[brownstone-institute|Brownstone Institute]] linked the action to press secretary [[jen-psaki|Jen Psaki]]'s May 5, 2021 warning that platforms must stop amplifying COVID-19 and election "misinformation," in which she also noted President [[joe-biden|Biden]]'s support for "a robust antitrust program."[30] Critics including [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] treated the case as an information-control measure rather than a consumer-welfare action, noting its timing just after Supreme Court arguments in [[murthy-v-missouri|Murthy v. Missouri]] recast government coercion of platforms as mere "encouragement."[30]

## Wei espionage conviction and the deleted press release (2025)

In August 2025 the Justice Department, through the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of California, announced the espionage conviction of U.S. Navy sailor [[jinchao-wei|Jinchao Wei]], who sold Navy secrets to a Chinese intelligence officer for $12,000.[31] U.S. Attorney Adam Gordon said the verdict served as "a crucial reminder that the Department of Justice will vigorously prosecute traitors."[31] Badlands reported that the department subsequently appeared to delete the press release from its website, forcing reliance on an archived copy, and flagged the removal as notable and worth tracking.[31]

## Redactions in the Comey leak memos (2025)

Declassified memos reported by Just the News showed that federal prosecutors had gathered evidence from [[james-comey|James Comey]]'s top lieutenants that he authorized the leak of classified information to reporters just before the 2016 election, yet declined to bring criminal charges.[31] The relevant passages had originally been redacted by the Justice Department in versions sent to [[congress|Congress]], until Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] intervened to eliminate the redactions and dispatch new versions to the House and Senate Judiciary committees.[31] Badlands framed the episode, alongside the abandoned [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]] probe, as evidence of DOJ corruption shielding political elites.[31]


## Post-Kirk enforcement posture (September 2025)

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said on 15 September 2025 that he would consider designating [[antifa|Antifa]] and other left-wing groups as domestic terrorists, indicating he would proceed if he found support from Cabinet officials and the Department of Justice.[32] The same day, White House Deputy Chief of Staff [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] pledged on *The Charlie Kirk Show* that the administration would use "every resource we have at the Department of Justice, Homeland Security and throughout this government to identify, disrupt, dismantle and destroy these networks," referring to left-wing groups accused of promoting violence following the assassination of [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]].[32] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the Kirk case as a test for Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] and FBI Director [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]].[32]

## Assange and Hunter Biden matters, August 2023

Ambassador [[caroline-kennedy|Caroline Kennedy]] said in August 2023 that any plea deal for [[julian-assange|Julian Assange]] would be "up to the Justice Department," which declined to comment on the reports.[33] In the same period, special counsel [[david-weiss|David Weiss]] told a federal court that [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s plea deal and pretrial diversion agreement were not in effect, rejecting defense claims that the diversion agreement remained "valid and binding" and had been "largely dictated" by DOJ prosecutors: "The Government never said the proposed diversion agreement was in effect because it is not."[33]

## January 6 caseload disclosure (January 2025)

On January 6, 2025 the Justice Department released figures revealing for the first time how many uncharged [[january-6-prosecutions|January 6 cases]] prosecutors believed remained pending as [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] prepared to retake office: as many as 200 more people could be charged, including 60 suspected of assaulting or impeding police officers during the Capitol riot.[34] About 1,583 people had already faced federal charges for their role in the attack, more than 600 of them felony charges for assaulting or resisting police.[34]

The probe was described by department leaders as the largest federal prosecution in its history, and the release of the pending-case estimate was characterised as a signal flare to Trump, who had indicated he expected to pardon many participants but had repeatedly mischaracterised the status of the cases.[34]

## Indictment of Gal Luft (2023)

Days after Israeli whistleblower [[gal-luft|Gal Luft]] publicly detailed allegations against the family of [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], the Justice Department charged him with failing to register under the [[foreign-agents-registration-act|Foreign Agents Registration Act]] and with Iranian sanctions violations, alleging he conspired to act in China's interest.[35] The July 12, 2023 [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] presented the timing as evidence of a weaponized department, with [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] describing the prosecution as projection and as an attempt to cover up alleged Biden family activity.[35]

## Handling of the Pence and Hunter Biden matters (February 2023)

In February 2023 the Justice Department was in talks with [[mike-pence|Mike Pence]]'s legal team over scheduling an [[fbi|FBI]] search of his Indiana home for classified material, having already directed agents to collect documents there on January 19 of that year.[36] Pence's attorneys contended that by taking the material directly rather than through the [[national-archives-and-records-administration|National Archives]], the department "bypassed the standard procedures."[36]

The same week, [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s lawyer [[abbe-lowell|Abbe Lowell]] wrote to the department's National Security Division — along with the Delaware attorney general and the [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]] — seeking investigations into alleged efforts to "weaponize" the contents of the [[hunter-biden-laptop|Hunter Biden laptop]].[36]


## Opposition to release of the Biden interview tapes, July 2024

On Thursday, July 18, 2024, the Department of Justice told a federal judge that combined efforts by two conservative groups and a coalition of mainstream media organizations to obtain "properly withheld" audiotapes of President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s interview with special counsel [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]] through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit should fail.[37] Among its arguments, the department warned that the threat of "deepfakes" and "privacy harms" to an uncharged individual were real and that compelled disclosure "would create a perverse incentive" against executive branch cooperation with Congress.[37] It disputed that there was "any meaningful countervailing public benefit" to releasing the recordings, noting that a transcript had already been published and that Hur had testified before Congress about his report and his non-charging recommendation regarding the willful retention and disclosure of classified materials, while acknowledging that the tapes contained "new information not contained in the written transcript" — namely the "sound, tone, and pacing" of Biden's answers, described as the "non-lexical aspects" of the recordings.[37]

## November 2025 actions

In early November 2025 the Department's Civil Rights Division, through Assistant Attorney General [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]], issued a formal demand that the [[fulton-county|Fulton County]] Board of Registration and Elections produce all election records responsive to a [[georgia-state-election-board|Georgia State Election Board]] subpoena, invoking the [[civil-rights-act-of-1960|Civil Rights Act of 1960]] and citing compliance with the [[national-voter-registration-act|National Voter Registration Act]] and [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]]; the county was given 15 days to comply.[38]

The Department also reviewed concerns about foreign involvement in the [[new-york-city-mayoral-election-2025|New York City mayoral race]] after British MP [[jeremy-corbyn|Jeremy Corbyn]] joined a Democratic Socialists of America phone bank for [[zohran-mamdani|Zohran Mamdani]], though it did not open a formal investigation.[38] In Michigan, the Department charged two men over an alleged [[isis|ISIS]]-inspired Halloween terror plot, with Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] releasing the complaint on November 3.[38]

## Jeremy Brown pardon dispute and Virginia voter roll suit, 2025

In court filings reported in early February 2025, the Justice Department acknowledged that [[oath-keepers|Oath Keeper]] [[jeremy-brown|Jeremy Brown]]'s Florida weapons-possession case was directly related to January 6, leaving his supporters bewildered that he remained imprisoned after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s January 6 pardon.[39] Brown's attorney [[carolyn-stewart|Carolyn Stewart]] argued the filings showed the DOJ, the defense, and a US district judge all agreed the Florida case — which carried a sentence of more than seven years — was a January 6 case, and said the department had nonetheless prevented his release.[39]

Separately, the DOJ withdrew a lawsuit against Virginia, originally filed under the Biden administration, that sought to bar the removal of noncitizens from the Commonwealth's voter rolls.[39] The withdrawal comported with a series of filings ramping down Biden-era lawfare following Trump's January 20 inauguration.[39] The suit had been initiated in October after Virginia moved to exclude roughly 1,600 individuals identified as potential noncitizens; a federal judge ordered the state to reinstate them, but the Supreme Court lifted that injunction, allowing Virginia's list maintenance program to proceed.[39] Badlands commentary by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the reversal a sign the DOJ was depoliticizing its workforce and caseload, and tied it to a broader election integrity push including a Wyoming proposal to ban electronic voting equipment.[39]

## Defense of the mass firings of federal workers (March 2025)

In an opposition filing submitted on Monday, March 10, 2025, the Justice Department argued that state attorneys general in 20 states suing the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] over its mass firings of federal workers had "no hope of success," contending that "third parties cannot interject themselves" into government-employee affairs.[40] Filing on behalf of the [[department-of-agriculture|Department of Agriculture]], the [[department-of-education|Department of Education]] and other federal agencies being sued by unions in Washington, D.C., Maryland and 18 other states where government workers live, the DOJ said: "The States cannot circumvent that channeling scheme by asserting downstream harm from those employment actions."[40] The filing added that "nor do the States have any legitimate claims of their own," and was reported by [[law-and-crime|Law & Crime]].[40]

## March 2025 stopgap funding reductions

Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]]'s March 2025 [[continuing-resolution|continuing resolution]], passed by the House on March 11, maintained current spending levels through September 30 while reducing Justice Department state and local law enforcement assistance.[40]

## Epstein "client list" memo (July 2025)

On Monday, July 7, 2025, the Department of Justice released a memo stating that sex offender [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] did not keep a "client list" linking celebrities and public officials to sex trafficking, a declaration that angered many of the administration's own supporters.[43] The memo followed months in which Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] had said significant Epstein material ignored by the Biden administration would come to light, and her February comment to Fox News that such a document was "sitting on my desk."[43] Department spokesperson Chad Gilmartin, alongside White House Press Secretary [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]], told reporters that Bondi had meant all of the Epstein files were on her desk rather than a specific list of co-conspirators.[43] Badlands contributors questioned why this version of the files could not have been released on day one and whether five months of investigation had been spent confirming nothing.[43]

## Russia sanctions enforcement (2022–2023)

By March 2023 the Justice Department, working alongside the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]], had contributed to the seizure of more than $500 million in assets belonging to Russian oligarchs and their supporters, and had secured indictments of more than 30 individuals and two corporate entities on charges including sanctions evasion and money laundering.[44] A department fact sheet described the actions as a response to "Russia's unprovoked, full-scale invasion of Ukraine" and pledged the use of "all tools at [their] disposal to impose accountability and serious costs on Russia and its enablers."[44] Badlands contributor [[just-human|Just Human]] questioned whether the [[us-sanctions-campaign-against-russian-oligarch-networks|campaign]] was in fact aimed at Russia or at a transnational "Swamp" spanning Russia, Europe and the United States.[44]

## Charges against Russian media executives (September 2024)

On September 4, 2024, the Justice Department charged two Russian media executives over an alleged illegal scheme to influence the November 2024 election with pro-Russian propaganda, a day before [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] joked at the [[eastern-economic-forum|Eastern Economic Forum]] that Moscow supported [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]].[45] The following day, special counsel [[david-weiss|David Weiss]]'s prosecutors were caught by surprise when [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] reversed course and pleaded guilty to all nine federal tax charges.[45] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] argued that while mainstream outlets treated alleged foreign funding of an influence operation as destabilising the 2024 cycle, actual interference in US elections was unlikely ever to have a measurable impact on results.[45]

## Dismissal of the Trump prosecutions (November 2024)

On 25 November 2024 Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] moved to dismiss both the federal election subversion case and the classified documents case against President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], writing in a six-page filing that "the [Justice] Department's position is that the Constitution requires that this case be dismissed before the defendant is inaugurated," and stressing that "this outcome is not based on the merits or strength of the case against the defendant."[46] Judge [[tanya-chutkan|Tanya Chutkan]] formally dismissed the election case without prejudice that afternoon.[46] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the collapse as the end of "one of the prime narrative deployments of the current age" and predicted that the department would be "cleaned out and reformed" before taking on a great deal of actual work.[46]

## Post-election leadership and reform speculation

After [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]]'s withdrawal, Trump named [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] to lead the department, while [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] was reported as likely to receive a senior post at either the department or the [[fbi|FBI]]; Axios reported that Patel's appointment was considered "deeply controversial" within the department, which the outlet acknowledged Trump regarded as "the heart of the so-called 'Deep State.'"[46]

## 2025 Russia collusion grand jury

On Monday, August 4, 2025, Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] signed an order for a federal prosecutor to present evidence to a grand jury to consider charges related to the [[russiagate-hoax|Russia collusion hoax]], according to multiple sources who confirmed the move to Just the News.[47] The step followed criminal referrals sent to the department by Director of National Intelligence [[tulsi-gabbard|Tulsi Gabbard]], who stated in July that she had transmitted evidence concerning U.S. intelligence officials allegedly politicizing intelligence connected to the Russia collusion investigation, calling the matter a "treasonous conspiracy."[47] The convening of a grand jury does not guarantee charges; the grand jury's role is to recommend charges if it believes sufficient evidence exists to justify prosecution.[47]

Separately, a Justice Department strike force established by Bondi is examining whether the 2016 [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] campaign effort to link [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] was a political dirty trick or a criminal conspiracy.[47] The department also announced the conviction of former NFL player [[leshon-johnson|Leshon Johnson]] on six felony counts under the federal Animal Welfare Act for operating a large-scale dog fighting and trafficking venture in Oklahoma.[47]

## Maxwell pardon question, October 2025

Asked on October 6, 2025 whether he would free [[ghislaine-maxwell|Ghislaine Maxwell]] through a pardon, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he would speak to the Department of Justice about it, on the same day the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] declined to hear her appeal.[48] [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] commentary from [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] in the same issue argued more broadly that the DOJ, the federal court system, the Department of Education, and the Department of Homeland Security should not be propped up, contending that a loss of faith in the federal government is a required feature of Trump's plan.[48]

## Minnesota sanctuary lawsuit, September 2025

On Monday 29 September 2025 the Department of Justice sued the state of [[minnesota|Minnesota]] together with [[minneapolis|Minneapolis]] and [[saint-paul|St. Paul]] over so-called [[sanctuary-cities|sanctuary city]] policies.[49] Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] announced that "Minnesota officials are jeopardizing the safety of their own citizens by allowing illegal aliens to circumvent the legal process."[49] The 34-page complaint asks a judge to invalidate sections of the state constitution, city codes in both Twin Cities, and Hennepin County administrative orders, with federal prosecutors arguing that policies preventing local cooperation with Immigration and Customs Enforcement are unconstitutional.[49] Both cities maintain ordinances prohibiting cooperation with federal immigration enforcement.[49]

## Review of the Tina Peters conviction (March 2025)

On Monday, March 3, 2025, the Justice Department said it would review the conviction of [[tina-peters|Tina Peters]], the former clerk of Mesa County, Colorado, found guilty of state charges of tampering with voting machines in a failed attempt to prove they had been used to rig the 2020 election against President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[50] [[yaakov-m-roth|Yaakov M. Roth]], acting assistant attorney general for the Department's civil division, filed a statement of interest declaring that "reasonable concerns have been raised about various aspects of Ms. Peters's case," citing the Attorney General Memorandum "Restoring the Integrity and Credibility of The Department of Justice" (February 5, 2025) and Executive Order 14147, "Ending the Weaponization of The Federal Government" (January 20, 2025).[50] The New York Times characterized the decision as an example of the Department under Trump's control using its powers to support those who acted on his behalf and to pursue those who opposed him.[50]

Separately, Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] discussed terminations and turnover at the Department and the [[fbi|FBI]] in a Fox News interview the same evening without addressing the forced departure of New York field office head [[james-dennehy|James Dennehy]].[50]

## December 2025 actions

Lawyers for former [[cia|CIA]] Director [[john-brennan|John Brennan]] accused the department of attempting to steer a Miami criminal grand jury matter to a favored judge, alleging in a letter to chief U.S. District Judge [[cecilia-altonaga|Cecilia M. Altonaga]] that prosecutors were manipulating grand jury and case assignment procedures to place the case before U.S. District Judge [[aileen-cannon|Aileen Cannon]], and citing alleged leaks that violated grand jury secrecy rules.[51] U.S. Attorney [[jason-reding-quinones|Jason Reding Quinones]] had informed them the grand jury was examining Brennan's role in the [[2017-intelligence-assessment-on-russian-interference|2017 Intelligence Community Assessment]].[51]

Separately, the department's Civil Rights Division, through its newly established Second Amendment Section, filed a [[doj-lawsuit-against-dc-firearm-laws|federal lawsuit]] against the District of Columbia and its [[metropolitan-police-department-of-the-district-of-columbia|Metropolitan Police Department]], arguing that D.C.'s firearm registration laws and enforcement practices impose a de facto ban on AR-15s and other commonly owned semi-automatic firearms in violation of the [[second-amendment|Second Amendment]].[51]

## June 2025 election and immigration litigation

In late June 2025 the Justice Department pursued several high-profile actions reported in the Badlands News Brief.[52] It ran a probe, authorized by President Donald Trump and directed by Attorney General Pam Bondi, into the unlawful use of online fundraising platforms to make "straw" or "dummy" contributions, running parallel to House Republican subpoenas of ActBlue officials.[52] It sued Robert Page, the Orange County, California Registrar of Voters, over his refusal to provide records on the removal of non-citizens from voter rolls and alleged failure to maintain an accurate list under the Help America Vote Act, with Assistant Attorney General Harmeet K. Dhillon of the Civil Rights Division announcing the case.[52] The Department also sued the U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland over a standing order requiring clerks to automatically issue injunctions halting removal or status challenges for any alien detained in Maryland who files a habeas petition, arguing the order bypasses procedural requirements for preliminary injunctions, ignores congressional intent, and violates Supreme Court precedent.[52]

## Live Nation-Ticketmaster antitrust lawsuit

In May 2024 the Justice Department filed an antitrust lawsuit against [[live-nation-entertainment|Live Nation Entertainment]], parent company of [[ticketmaster|Ticketmaster]], alleging unlawful domination of the live-events market.[53] Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] said the conduct was "anti-competitive and illegal" and that "it is time to break it up."[53] The complaint charged that the companies had used their "power and influence to insert themselves at the center and the edges of virtually every aspect of the live music ecosystem," freezing innovation and imposing real costs on Americans.[53] The action followed the 2022 collapse of Ticketmaster's presale for [[taylor-swift|Taylor Swift]]'s Eras Tour and the Biden administration's earlier campaign against ticketing "junk fees."[53]

## December 2025 pipe bomb announcement and the Colorado records dispute

The Justice Department scheduled a news conference for December 4, 2025 to formally announce the FBI's first arrest in the nearly five-year January 6 pipe bomb investigation.[54] Separately, Colorado Secretary of State Jena Griswold publicly refused to comply with a department request for Coloradans' sensitive voting information, saying the DOJ 'can take a hike' because it has no legal right to the data.[54]
