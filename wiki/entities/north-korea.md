# North Korea

North Korea features in "America for Sale — Part 2" chiefly through the secret 2009 mission in which former president [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] traveled to Pyongyang to secure the release of two American journalists sentenced to twelve years of hard labor for spying.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## The 2009 hostage mission

The trip was undertaken with the blessing of the [[barack-obama|Obama]] administration and was planned in consultation with its national-security experts.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) Travelling with Clinton were [[doug-band|Doug Band]], [[john-podesta|John Podesta]], and aides Justin Cooper and Jon Davidson.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) According to the article, the mission took place at the request of North Korean leader [[kim-jong-il|Kim Jong Il]] — a detail the author treats as unexplained and significant.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

Emails released by [[judicial-watch|Judicial Watch]] showed Band asking [[huma-abedin|Huma Abedin]] on July 27, 2009 to obtain diplomatic passports for himself and two others, despite none of them working for the [[state-department|State Department]]; Abedin replied "Okay. Will figure it out."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) The article argues such passports would allow the party to skip security and scrutiny.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## Hostages as cover

The author contends that hostages have been "an enemy playbook strategy for many decades," citing the Iran hostages as cover for the CIA and shadow government's Iran-Contra operation, and hostages in Obama's Iran Nuclear Deal as cover for a massive secret money transfer to Iran.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) He raises the question of what the 2009 North Korea hostage story was itself covering for, promising to reveal the connections — including links to Huma Abedin, Doug Band and [[chuck-grassley|Chuck Grassley]] — in a subsequent installment.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) A [[qanon|Q]] drop consisting solely of a photograph from the Pyongyang meeting, posted November 24, 2017 without comment, is treated as a deliberate pointer to this episode.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## The 1994 Agreed Framework

Part 3 of the series traces the relationship further back, arguing that Bill Clinton and Kim Jong Il already had a working relationship before 2009.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) On October 18, 1994, Clinton announced the [[agreed-framework|Agreed Framework]], a four-page nuclear agreement under which North Korea would shut its main reactor at Yongbyon, abandon two others and seal weapons-usable fuel, in exchange for American oil to replace lost fuel and the construction of two new "light fuel" plants.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) The article notes that North Korea broke the agreement almost immediately, resuming weapons development at an underground facility.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

The author argues the deal was never intended to prevent proliferation: supplying oil to a sanctioned North Korea freed cash for its weapons program and thereby accelerated it, and the true subject of the deal was oil and money.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Clinton lifted [[sanctions|sanctions]] on North Korea and, five months later, imposed severe sanctions and an oil embargo on [[iran|Iran]] — a contradiction the article says is explained by a plan to source Iranian oil for delivery to Pyongyang, brokered by the sanctions-busting commodities trader [[marc-rich|Marc Rich]].[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

## A closed financial system

North Korea is presented, alongside Iran, as a "closed financial system" — a country cut off from the world banking system by sanctions and therefore able to move money outside money-laundering law and congressional oversight.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) In the author's framing this makes sanctioned states an asset rather than a target for the [[cia|CIA]] and shadow government, which he says stood behind the nuclear programs of all such "rogue states."[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Rich's pioneering uranium-for-oil commodity swaps of the 1980s are offered as the mechanism by which North Korea and Iran obtained uranium.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

## The 2009 trip revisited

Part 3 returns to the 2009 Pyongyang visit with additional detail from a memo attached to a hacked email published by [[wikileaks|WikiLeaks]] from John Podesta's account: Kim Jong Il asked that Clinton "return often, officially and unofficially," and invited him back to the DPRK on holiday, exchanges the article says read as friendly rather than adversarial.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Doug Band, then working for the [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]] and [[clinton-global-initiative|Clinton Global Initiative]], appears standing behind Kim Jong Il in the photograph Q posted.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Coming four years after Clinton secured Kazakh uranium mines with [[frank-giustra|Frank Giustra]], the author speculates that the 2009 "hostage" trip concealed a deal that included uranium for North Korea.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

## Military alliance with Russia (2026)

In April 2026 North Korea unveiled a museum honoring soldiers it says helped "liberate" [[russia|Russia's]] Kursk region, with [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]] presiding over memorial events and senior Russian officials in attendance, including Defense Minister [[andrey-belousov|Andrey Belousov]].[3] The ceremony framed North Korean troops as having fought alongside Russian forces in the [[ukraine|Ukraine]] war, marking one of the clearest public acknowledgments yet of Pyongyang's direct military role.[3]

The event coincided with expanded defense cooperation between the two states, including agreements described as long-term or "comprehensive" military partnerships, with reported coordination across weapons transfers, training and strategic alignment.[3] The [[kursk-liberation-museum|museum]] and its memorial messaging are described as serving both a domestic legitimacy function and an international signal of a hardened alliance structure opposing Western-backed Ukraine, with Russian attendance underscoring the operational integration emerging between Moscow and Pyongyang.[3]

Badlands commentary credits North Korean special forces with holding the northern front at a critical moment and preventing Ukrainian and NATO-backed forces from reaching the Kursk Nuclear Power Plant, and reports that captured Western armor — including an M1A1 Abrams, a Bradley Fighting Vehicle and a Leopard 2 gifted by [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] — is displayed as trophies.[3]

## Wonsan-Kalma tourism project

In July 2024, [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]] surveyed plans for the [[wonsan-kalma-tourist-zone|Wonsan-Kalma zone]], a tourism development on the country's eastern coast announced in 2015, where North Korea had been building luxury hotels and recreational facilities to attract international visitors.[4] The project reportedly suffered setbacks from shortages of construction materials caused by toughened UN sanctions and COVID restrictions, with preparations discussed for an opening by May 2025.[4] The resort sits adjacent to a rocket-testing site.[4]

Badlands commentary tied the project to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 2018 pledge to help transform the North Korean coastline into a tourist destination after he became the first U.S. president to enter the country at the DMZ, and to claims in the memoirs of former South Korean president [[moon-jae-in|Moon Jae-in]] that Kim had been "desperate" to rid North Korea of nuclear weapons since that summit.[4]

## Reported troop deployment to Russia, October 2024
In October 2024, South Korea's National Intelligence Service said North Korea had shipped 1,500 special forces personnel to Russia's Far East for training and acclimatisation at local military bases ahead of combat alongside Russian troops in Ukraine, and local media citing the NIS reported that Pyongyang had decided to dispatch 12,000 troops organized into four brigades.[5] The reports, partly based on satellite imagery, prompted Seoul to signal that it might supply defensive and even offensive weapons to Ukraine.[5]

A North Korean representative dismissed the allegations at a United Nations General Assembly committee meeting, saying: "As for the so-called military cooperation with Russia, my delegation does not feel any need for comment on such groundless, stereotype rumors aimed at smearing the image of the DPRK and undermining the legitimate, friendly, and cooperative relations between sovereign states."[5]

Badlands treated the accompanying viral material — a purported Chinese mercenary claiming 153 Chinese nationals had died alongside many North Koreans, video of North Korean troops arriving at a Far Eastern Russian base to be called "very handsome allies," and a photograph of the alleged 3,000-man "Special Buryat Brigade" posing with a flag showing Kim Jong Un and Vladimir Putin shaking hands — as "a troll inside of a troll wrapped in a psyop."[5]

## Wonsan Kalma resort and tourism strategy

In June 2025 North Korea announced the opening of the [[wonsan-kalma|Wonsan Kalma]] beach resort on its east coast, which leader [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]] hoped would boost tourism, with domestic visitors admitted from July 1, 2025 and no announced date for foreign visitors.[6] State media agency [[korean-central-news-agency|KCNA]] claimed the resort could host 20,000 visitors along a four-kilometre beach with hotels, restaurants, shopping malls, and a water park, none of it independently verifiable.[6] Reporting noted that the country, heavily sanctioned for decades over its nuclear weapons programme, remains among the world's poorest and directs most resources to its military and to monuments — often in Pyongyang — that embellish the cult of the Kim family, which has ruled since 1948.[6]

## End of the border loudspeaker broadcasts (June 2025)

North Korea appeared to halt its cross-border loudspeaker broadcasts as of Thursday morning, June 12, 2025, according to South Korea's military — a day after Seoul suspended its own loudspeaker campaign.[7] South Korean President [[lee-jae-myung|Lee Jae-myung]], who had taken office that week, ordered the suspension in a move aimed at easing tensions and reopening dialogue with Pyongyang.[7] Joint Chiefs of Staff spokesperson Col. Lee Sung-Jun said "there was no region where North Korea's noise broadcasts to the South were heard today," noting the broadcasts had still been audible until around 11 p.m. the previous night; Pyongyang offered no official confirmation.[7] Badlands commentator [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] noted that the North first responded by playing soothing, calming music before turning the speakers off entirely, and joked that the episode marked the end of the "Poop Balloon Wars."[7]

## June 2024 Putin visit and the sanctions question

In June 2024, Russian President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] traveled to Pyongyang for a summit with [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]], after which he called publicly for revision of the [[united-nations-security-council|UN Security Council]] sanctions regime against North Korea, describing it as an indefinite restrictive regime "inspired by the United States and its allies."[8] He said Moscow and Pyongyang would continue to oppose sanctions strangulation as an instrument of Western hegemony, and at a press conference compared the decades-old sanctions to the Nazi [[siege-of-leningrad|siege of Leningrad]].[8]

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary described North Korea as having been propped up for decades as a puppet boogeyman by the [[cia|CIA]] and the [[state-department|State Department]], with the Kim family installed as figurehead — a framing under which Putin's visit represented a symbolic reversal.[8] Commentator [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that the trip functioned as a public relations campaign to reintroduce Kim to Western audiences so that North Korea could ultimately be recognized as part of an emerging free world, and speculated that Russia might become the country's "champion and liberator."[8] The episode is treated in full at [[2024-putin-visit-to-north-korea-and-vietnam|the 2024 Putin visit to North Korea and Vietnam]].[8]

## Trash balloon campaign and missile tests (September 2024)

In September 2024 North Korea continued its campaign of sending trash-filled balloons into [[south-korea|South Korea]], a tactic [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]] devised in May of that year after the South sent 20 large balloons carrying over 300,000 leaflets criticizing him and 2,000 USB sticks containing K-pop content across the border.[9] A barrage launched between Sunday evening and early Monday morning caused flight suspensions at South Korea's main international airport.[9] Badlands Media noted that [[reuters|Reuters]] had tracked every wave of the launches — counting more than 6,000 balloons, mapping flight patterns and termination points, and cataloguing the contents — and mocked the scale of effort devoted to the story.[9] The brief added that Pyongyang had also been testing new ballistic missiles with "super large" warheads, and that corporate media had branded Kim [[vladimir-putin|Putin]]'s "most dangerous ally."[9]

## Reopening to foreign tourists (2025)

In early 2025, the North Korean government began allowing foreign tourists to enter the country on guided tours for the first time in years, though visitors remained under close watch by sanctioned tour guides and limited to approved areas.[10] Tourists were permitted to visit only the city of Rason, described as one of the least visited cities in the country; located in the northeast near the Russian and Chinese borders, Rason was designated North Korea's first special economic zone in 1991.[10]

The reopening coincided with national birthday celebrations for the late Kim Jong Il, the former leader who died in 2011, and followed a group of Western tourists making the first such trip in February since the country closed itself off during the COVID-19 pandemic.[10] Rowan Beard, head of Young Pioneer Tours, said that "the North Koreans aren't robots. They have opinions, goals, and a sense of humor. And in our briefing we encourage people to listen to and understand them."[10] Western tourists described the trip as "extremely" controlled, escorted throughout by approved guides who ushered them into a beer factory, a school, and a pharmacy; tour leader Ben Weston stated, "You can't leave the hotel without the guides."[10] The school visit included children's dancing performances staged against animations of missiles flying through the air, and one traveler said, "The roads are awful, the pavements are wobbly, and the buildings are weirdly constructed."[10]

## Demolition of inter-Korean road and rail links (October 2024)

On Tuesday, October 15, 2024, North Korea blew up the northern sections of the unused road and rail routes that once linked it with [[south-korea|South Korea]], in what the Associated Press described as a symbolic display of anger.[11] The choreographed demolition came days after the North claimed the South had flown drones over its capital, Pyongyang, and amid an exchange of threats between the rivals.[11] AP framed the act as underlining North Korea's growing anger toward South Korea's conservative government, following leader [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]]'s vow to sever relations with the South and abandon the goal of peaceful Korean unification.[11] Observers judged it unlikely that Kim would launch a preemptive large-scale attack, given that near-certain massive retaliation by the superior forces of the United States and South Korea would threaten Pyongyang's survival.[11]

Badlands contributor Burning Bright read the episode through [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s commentary on it, arguing that in three clipped sentences Trump laid out an "ultra condensed Hegelian Dialectic": the reported Actual as the problem, a blunt summation of the reaction the Collective Mind is meant to have, and then the STORY of the solution set off in narrative quotes.[11] He concluded that "Trump the Peacemaker" is the last story, and that Kim, Vladimir Putin, and other members of what he called the Sovereign Alliance would help actualize it.[11]
