# Smartmatic

Smartmatic is an election technology company whose origins, ownership history, and intellectual property lineage are central to Badlands Media's argument that American elections are structurally unverifiable. In the June 9, 2026 *Badlands Brief*, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], drawing on research by [[canncon|CannCon]], described Los Angeles as "the only jurisdiction in the US that still uses Smartmatic," while arguing that Smartmatic code nonetheless has "a founding role in the code bases of the other US election systems in use around the country."[1]

## Founding and Delaware incorporation

According to the account presented in the Brief, Smartmatic Corp. was incorporated in Delaware in April 2000 by Alfredo Jose Anzola, and headquartered in Boca Raton, Florida, in Palm Beach County — just seven months before the disputed 2000 presidential recount centered on hanging chads in Broward and Palm Beach counties.[1] Badlands treats that timing and geography as more than coincidence.[1]

## Venezuela and the Chávez recall

Four years after incorporation, in 2004, Smartmatic won its first contract with Venezuela, ahead of the recall election of [[hugo-chavez|Hugo Chávez]], and did so months after receiving investment from the Venezuelan government.[1] The Brief quotes the *New York Times* reporting that seven months before the voting contract was awarded, a Venezuelan government financing agency invested more than $200,000 into a smaller technology company owned by some of the same people as Smartmatic, which joined the Smartmatic bid as a minor partner; in return the agency received a 28 percent stake and a board seat occupied by a senior government official who had previously advised Chávez on elections technology. Venezuelan officials later insisted the money was merely a small-business loan, repaid before the referendum.[1] That smaller company, [[bitza|Bitza]], simultaneously wrote the software used in Smartmatic voting machines.[1]

## Sequoia acquisition and divestiture

In 2005 Smartmatic purchased [[sequoia-voting-systems|Sequoia Voting Systems]] from the British firm [[de-la-rue|De La Rue PLC]] for $16 million — representing a $19 million loss for De La Rue against its $35 million investment four years earlier.[1] Two years later Smartmatic announced it would divest Sequoia, but an April 2008 Delaware Court filing revealed that Smartmatic retained a $2 million unsecured promissory note from the divestiture and, more significantly, that Sequoia was still using Smartmatic's intellectual property.[1]

## The IP-lineage argument

Badlands summarizes the resulting chain as follows: Smartmatic buys Sequoia; [[election-systems-software|ES&S]] divests [[premier-election-solutions|Premier]] to [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion]]; Smartmatic sells Sequoia while retaining an interest and continuing IP use; Dominion buys Sequoia.[1] The conclusion drawn is that Smartmatic intellectual property is "ostensibly present throughout the system, despite its hot potato M&A activity," that Americans with oversight authority know this, and that they nonetheless "pretend that American elections are real and impervious to manipulation" because they are direct beneficiaries of the system.[1]

The Brief also cites a letter from [[john-c-bonifaz|John C. Bonifaz]] to the National Institute of Standards and Technology asserting that Dominion "depends upon secret source code created and owned by Smartmatic, a foreign controlled company with ties to the Venezuelan government led by Hugo Chavez."[1]

## Relevance to the 2026 Los Angeles mayoral primary

Because Los Angeles retains Smartmatic equipment, Badlands treats the 2026 mayoral primary — in which [[nithya-raman|Nithya Raman]] advanced to a runoff against [[karen-bass|Karen Bass]] ahead of [[spencer-pratt|Spencer Pratt]] — as a live test case. As Ashe in America put it, "Spencer Pratt and Smartmatic in LA in 2026 might be about a lot more than who is going to run the City of Angels."[1]

## Lindell sanctions and the 2026 dismissal

Smartmatic entered the [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion]] litigation against [[mike-lindell|Mike Lindell]] as a third-party defendant after Lindell countersued; those counterclaims were largely unsuccessful.[2] Smartmatic obtained sanctions against Lindell for frivolous filings, with fines exceeding $92,000 by early June 2026 after he failed to pay.[2] When Dominion's successor [[liberty-vote|Liberty Vote]], [[mypillow|My Pillow, Inc.]], a Dominion subsidiary, and Lindell jointly stipulated to dismiss the case with prejudice in June 2026, Smartmatic was not a party to that agreement, leaving its claims against Lindell outside the settlement.[2]


## 2025 federal bribery and money laundering indictment

In October 2025, federal prosecutors indicted Smartmatic on money laundering and bribery charges, alleging that the firm and its executives paid over $1 million in illicit payments to Philippine election officials to secure government contracts.[3] According to a superseding indictment filed in Miami federal court, the payments took place between 2015 and 2018 as Smartmatic sought a deal to assist in administering the Philippines' 2016 presidential election and to ensure prompt payment for its services.[3]

Roger Alejandro Piñate Martinez and Jorge Miguel Vasquez allegedly orchestrated over $1 million in bribes to Juan Andres Donato Bautista, former Chairman of the Philippines' [[comelec|COMELEC]], intended to secure and maintain contracts for SGO Corporation Limited, including favorable VAT reimbursements and other government payments.[3] The scheme was reportedly funded by inflating the cost of voting machines for the 2016 Philippine elections, with the conspirators concealing the bribes using coded language, fake contracts and loan agreements, and by routing funds through bank accounts in Asia, Europe, and the Southern District of Florida.[3] Three former Smartmatic executives, including co-founder Roger Piñate, had been charged in 2024 in connection with the same scheme, though the company itself was not previously named as a defendant.[3]

The case came as Smartmatic continued to pursue a $2.7 billion defamation lawsuit against [[fox-news|Fox News]], alleging the network broadcast "false" claims that it helped manipulate the outcome of the 2020 U.S. presidential election.[3]

## Fox News access to indictment materials (May 2025)

In May 2025, [[fox-news|Fox News]] secured a significant procedural victory in the defamation lawsuit Smartmatic had filed against the network when a New York appeals court overturned a lower court ruling and granted Fox access to materials from the 2024 federal bribery indictment against several Smartmatic executives.[4] Fox followed the ruling by filing a 156-page brief the next day outlining its arguments for summary judgment, building on an initial request made May 1.[4]

The underlying charges, brought in August 2024, named three current and former Smartmatic executives — including company co-founder and president Roger Piñate — in connection with an alleged bribery scheme in the Philippines.[4]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the ruling "a pleasant surprise," recalling that commentators had mocked Fox when news of the voting vendor's alleged corruption broke, because the network had previously settled with a related voting system vendor despite having cause to believe that the vendor's security promises were "vaporware lies."[4] She pointed to earlier assurances that voting machines could not connect to the internet, and said it was encouraging that Fox appeared to be fighting back this time.[4]

## August 2024 Philippines bribery indictment

On August 12, 2024, ABC News reported that the president of Smartmatic and two other executives had been charged by the US [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] with bribing the head of the Philippine election commission to win business during the 2016 election.[5] An indictment filed in the Southern District of Florida alleged that between 2015 and 2018 co-founder [[roger-alejandro-pinate-martinez|Roger Alejandro Pinate Martinez]], 49, and [[jorge-miguel-vasquez|Jorge Miguel Vasquez]], 62, together with others, caused at least $1 million in bribes to be paid to [[juan-andres-donato-bautista|Juan Andres Donato Bautista]], 60, the former chairman of the Philippines' Commission on Elections ([[comelec|COMELEC]]).[5] The men allegedly overpaid contracts using a slush fund to conceal the bribes, and prosecutors said the proceeds were laundered through bank accounts in Asia, Europe and the United States, including in the Southern District of Florida.[5]

## Corporate lineage and the Venezuela question

Writing as [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], Badlands Media used the indictment to restate the company's documented lineage.[5] In April 2000, seven months before Bush v. Gore, Alfredo Jose Anzola incorporated "Smartmatic Corp." in Delaware; the company gained its first client four years later in the 2004 recall election of Hugo Chavez.[5] In 2005 Smartmatic purchased [[sequoia-voting-systems|Sequoia Voting Systems]] from the British firm De La Rue Plc, and in October 2006 the New York Times published "U.S. Investigates Voting Machines' Venezuela Ties," reporting a formal inquiry into Smartmatic and its Oakland, California subsidiary.[5] Two years later Smartmatic announced it would divest Sequoia, but Delaware court records later revealed that the divestiture was incomplete and that Sequoia continued to use Smartmatic intellectual property — that is, code.[5] On June 4, 2010, [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]] announced it had acquired the assets of Sequoia, then serving nearly 300 jurisdictions in 16 states, including all intellectual property covering software, firmware and hardware for Sequoia's optical scan and DRE voting solutions.[5] Ashe in America summarized the chain as "Dominion = Sequoia = Smartmatic."[5]

On that basis the commentary asked why politicians of both parties, along with media, NGOs and influencers, dismiss Venezuela's historical role in US election technology as a conspiracy theory invented by [[sidney-powell|Sidney Powell]] to help [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[5] It cited prior public acknowledgements of voting-machine problems by [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]], Senator [[amy-klobuchar|Amy Klobuchar]], former Attorney General [[eric-holder|Eric Holder]] — whose Justice Department required a divestiture in the Election Systems & Software/Premier Election Solutions matter — and [[john-c-bonifaz|John Bonifaz]], described as one of the leftists who coordinated litigation against the author for election denialism.[5] The author concluded that "'Safe and secure' is a house of cards, and the wind is blowing," and credited [[brian-lupo|Brian Lupo]] for the underlying research.[5]

## Anti-SLAPP countersuit ruling (January 2024)

On Wednesday, January 24, 2024, a New York judge ruled that [[fox-news|Fox News]] could put Smartmatic on the defensive over whether the voting machine company's $2.7 billion lawsuit against the network — filed in Manhattan in 2021 over its coverage of the 2020 election — was designed to chill the network's speech.[6] Fox had earlier entered a $787.5 million settlement with [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]], avoiding a trial on whether it knowingly spread false claims about [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s defeat to appease viewers, but still faced potentially billions more in liability from the Smartmatic action.[6]

Taking advantage of New York's broad journalistic protections, Fox countersued under the state's anti-SLAPP statute — "Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation" — which is designed to prevent litigation intended to chill free speech.[6] Smartmatic argued that the success of Dominion's suit proved the merit of its own, while Fox contended that Smartmatic's enormous damages demand had no basis in fact.[6] In a seven-page ruling, Manhattan Supreme Court Justice David Cohen wrote that there had yet to be any ruling on whether Smartmatic's claims had a substantial factual basis, noting that the defendants' argument was "that plaintiffs' alleged damages are so extenuated from their actual lost profits that they were pleaded and/or sought in order to chill defendants' free speech rights," and that "that argument has not yet been adjudicated in any court."[6] Smartmatic maintains that Fox bears financial responsibility for giving air time to allegations that the company rigged the 2020 presidential election, an issue it argues a Delaware judge already resolved in Dominion's case.[6]

## Lindell defamation ruling

A federal judge ruled in 2025 that MyPillow founder Mike Lindell had defamed Smartmatic with 'false statements' alleging that the company's machines helped rig the 2020 presidential election, one of several legal disputes still surrounding Lindell as he weighed a run for governor of Minnesota.[7]

## Fox News public records lawsuit (January 2025)

In late January 2025, [[fox-news|Fox News]] sued Los Angeles County to obtain public records concerning the county's relationship with Smartmatic, filing days after a New York-based appeals court declined to dismiss Smartmatic's $2.7 billion defamation suit against the network over allegedly false claims about rigging of the 2020 election.[8] The complaint alleges evidence of corruption between county officials and Smartmatic employees during the bidding process for voting technology, describing the two as "entities with recent and extensive allegations of corruption in government contracts" and asserting that Fox had "already uncovered evidence showing personal benefits, including some undisclosed, flowing from Smartmatic to one County official."[8] See [[fox-news-lawsuit-against-los-angeles-county|the lawsuit]].[8]
