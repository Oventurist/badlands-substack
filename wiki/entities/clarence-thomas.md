# Clarence Thomas

**Clarence Thomas** is an Associate Justice of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]].[1]

## Louisiana v. Callais

In the April 2026 decision in [[louisiana-v-callais|Louisiana v. Callais]], Thomas joined the Court's 6–3 ruling striking down Louisiana's second majority-Black congressional district as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander, but issued a separate opinion saying he would have gone further.[1] Thomas argued that the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]] should not be used to challenge how states draw congressional districts at all.[1] In his view, the statute is meant to protect access to the ballot rather than to regulate how states design district maps, and redistricting decisions therefore fall outside its scope.[1] He wrote that no claims under that section should succeed in future districting cases.[1]

His position, if adopted, would go beyond the majority opinion authored by [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]], which left Section 2 in place while narrowing its application in [[2026-redistricting-battle|redistricting]] disputes.[1]

## Dissent in the 2026 birthright citizenship ruling

On June 30, 2026, Thomas dissented from the Supreme Court's decision upholding [[birthright-citizenship|birthright citizenship]] under the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] and striking down President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 2025 executive order limiting it.[2] He argued that the majority had twisted the amendment beyond its original Reconstruction-era meaning by recognizing a constitutional right to citizenship for the children of "foreign birth tourists and illegal aliens."[2] He warned that the ruling devalues American citizenship and "may not stand the test of time."[2] See [[2026-supreme-court-birthright-citizenship-ruling|the ruling]] for full coverage.[2]

## Supreme Court argument before Clarence Thomas (December 2025)

In December 2025 Elias argued a case before the Supreme Court of the United States, an appearance conservative outlet Revolver characterized as a humiliation.[3] The account described Elias as a political fixer and "Clinton-world errand boy" whose reputation rests on cable-news appearances rather than courtroom performance, and who helped inject the Russia collusion narrative into American media by turning opposition research into a national preoccupation.[3]

According to the coverage, Elias — accustomed to friendly anchors who never cross-examine him — "ran straight into a brick wall called [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]]."[3] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that Elias appears impaired whenever he must operate on the facts, the evidence, and the law, contrasting his confidence in the MSNBC echo chamber with his performance before the Court, and quoted Thomas telling him, "I still don't understand what you're saying."[3] She framed Elias's continued liberty as a "solid litmus test" for progress in restoring justice and the rule of law.[3]

## Federalist Society appearance
Guardian reporting recounted Thomas as the featured speaker at a gathering of Texas members of the Federalist Society during Donald Trump's first term, introduced by Fifth Circuit Judge Edith Jones after Matthew Kacsmaryk, then vice-president of the group's Fort Worth chapter, opened the evening.[4] Jones used the occasion to praise the four new conservative judges Trump had appointed to the Fifth Circuit and to credit "the Federalist Society, to Leonard" — a reference to Leonard Leo, the activist who took a seat across from Thomas and who the report suggested may have been the most important person in the American legal system in the room.[4]

## Criticism of Section 230

In October 2020, Thomas took issue with [[section-230|Section 230]] of the Communications Decency Act in a dissent in Malwarebytes Inc. v. Enigma Software Group USA.[5] Badlands Media contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] cited Thomas, along with several members of Congress, as among those who have long called out the provision's use as a weaponized tool against speech that does not conform to platform-approved narratives.[5]

## Scrutiny of Jack Smith's appointment (April 2024)

During oral arguments on April 25, 2024 in the case concerning whether former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] is immune from prosecution for official acts carried out during his presidency, Thomas asked Trump's attorney [[d-john-sauer|John Sauer]], "Did you, in this litigation, challenge the appointment of special counsel?" — a reference to [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]], who was appointed by Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]].[6] Sauer replied that Trump's attorneys had not raised such concerns "directly" in the case before the Court, but said the question "points to a very important issue here," given the prosecution's reliance on a "presumption of regularity."[6] He argued that this "runs into the reality that we have here an extraordinary prosecutorial power being exercised by someone who was never nominated by the president or confirmed by the Senate at any time."[6] Sauer added that he agreed with the analysis of former Attorneys General [[edwin-meese-iii|Edwin Meese]] and Michael B. Mukasey, who had submitted an amicus brief on March 19, 2024 arguing that irrespective of the immunity question, Smith "does not have authority to conduct the underlying prosecution."[6]

## Ethics controversy and the 2023 code of conduct

Concerns about alleged ethical improprieties among the justices ignited in April 2023, after the nonprofit outlet ProPublica reported that Thomas had gone on trips paid for by Republican megadonor Harlan Crow.[7] Sustained pressure over undisclosed travel and gifts culminated on November 13, 2023, when the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] announced it would adopt the [[2023-supreme-court-code-of-conduct|2023 Supreme Court Code of Conduct]], a 14-page guidance document setting rules for accepting travel opportunities and reaffirming the justices' commitment to the statute governing financial disclosure.[7]
