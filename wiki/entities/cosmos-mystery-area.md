# Cosmos Mystery Area

The Cosmos Mystery Area is a roadside tourist attraction in the Black Hills of South Dakota, of the type generally known as a "gravity hill" or "mystery spot." It is the central setting of the [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay [[a-mysterious-masterclass-in-multi|"A Mysterious Masterclass in Multi Sensory Deception"]] (August 30, 2024), whose author visited during the fifth [[great-american-restoration-tour|Great American Restoration Tour]] stop in nearby Deadwood.

## Origin story and tour

The attraction's own account, quoted in the article, states that "in 1952, two college boys searched the Black Hills looking for the perfect spot to build a summer cabin" and instead "stumbled into the mysterious world of the Cosmos." The pair reportedly camped out to investigate the "odd phenomena," concluded there was "something of interest to the general public," and repaired the cabin to produce the demonstrations shown to visitors.

As described in 2024, admission cost $15 for a roughly 40-minute guided tour. The tour opened with an optical illusion concerning the relative height of volunteers, proceeded up a hill past ponderosa pines interspersed with trees whose branches grow in contorted directions — attributed by the guide to "cosmic energy" — and culminated at a cabin and its fenced courtyard. There, visitors' sense of balance is disrupted; a tennis ball inspected for magnets rolled uphill on a ramp, and poured water flowed upward before spilling off the side. Inside the cabin's two rooms, guardrails line the walls, a hanging bar pulls occupants toward the cabin's front rather than straight down, and further ramps make balls appear to roll upward.

## Explanation

The article treats the effects as illusions rather than anomalies, calling the site "fake but still fascinating." It attributes them to an obstructed horizon combined with a deliberately skewed physical structure and a narrative of "cosmic energy" and anti-gravity delivered repeatedly by an authoritative guide. The essay cites a 2003 scientific study that recreated such sites and concluded that "antigravity-hill effects follow from a misperception of the eye level relative to gravity, caused by the presence of either contextual inclines or a false horizon line," and links the phenomenon to the aviation concept of [[spatial-disorientation|spatial disorientation]].

For the author, Cosmos functions as a model of narrative deception in the [[information-war|information war]]: a genuine scientific premise — real magnetic anomalies such as the South Atlantic Anomaly — is used to license a false claim, which the body then involuntarily confirms.
