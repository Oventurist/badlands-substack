# Alexander II

Alexander II was the Russian Czar who took the throne in the aftermath of the [[crimean-war|Crimean War]] and, in Matthew Ehret's account, presided over a decisive period of Russian-American strategic cooperation in the 1860s.[1] Ehret describes him as an "enlightened Czar" who ended the Crimean War and elevated a new generation of statesmen — [[grand-duke-constantine|Grand Duke Constantine]], [[nikolai-muraviev|General Nikolai Muraviev]], Foreign Minister [[alexander-gorchakov|Gorchakov]] and Ambassador [[eduard-de-stoeckl|Eduard de Stoeckl]] — around a new foreign policy doctrine that recognized the destructive role of the British Empire's global strategy and the value of America as a partner.[1]

## The Great Liberator

In 1861 Alexander II moved against endemic corruption and freed the 25 million Russian serfs who, in Ehret's words, "had lived for generations as human cattle," earning him the name "the Great Liberator."[1] This occurred only months before [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] earned the same title through the Emancipation Proclamation.[1] The two men never met in person, yet the article argues that the US-Russia alliance they established "changed the world in ways that have still not come to be realized 170 years later."[1]

## Intervention in the American Civil War

In 1863 Alexander II deployed the Russian navy to the Pacific and Atlantic coasts of the United States, a move Ehret credits with preventing British and French forces from entering the [[american-civil-war|Civil War]] openly on the Confederate side.[1] Russian sailors were celebrated by crowds of thousands in San Francisco and New York.[1] The Czar later explained that when France and Great Britain proposed joint recognition of Confederate independence in the autumn of 1862, he answered that he would treat such recognition as a *casus belli* for Russia, and dispatched the fleets so that the threat would not be taken as idle.[1] He added that he acted out of love for Russia, understanding that Russia would face a far more serious task if the American Republic were broken up and Great Britain left in control of modern industrial development.[1]

## Sale of Alaska and legacy

In 1866 Alexander II recalled Stoeckl to Russia and, after a lengthy meeting, approved the initiation of the [[alaska-purchase|sale of Alaska]] to the United States, consummated in March 1867 for $7.2 million.[1] Alexander II and his successor Alexander III applied the [[american-system-of-political-economy|American System]] model to the modernization of Russia, vectored around the [[trans-siberian-railway|Trans-Siberian Railway]].[1] Alexander II was assassinated in 1881, sixteen years after Lincoln's assassination.[1]
