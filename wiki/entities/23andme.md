# 23andMe

23andMe is a consumer genomics company founded and led by [[anne-wojcicki|Anne Wojcicki]].[1] The firm collects and analyzes customer DNA samples, giving it custody of large volumes of personal genetic information.

Badlands Media referenced 23andMe in its August 12, 2024 news brief while examining the Wojcicki family network following the death of [[youtube|YouTube]] chief executive [[susan-wojcicki|Susan Wojcicki]].[1] The brief noted that Anne Wojcicki, Susan's sister, runs the genomics company and married [[google|Google]] co-founder [[sergey-brin|Sergey Brin]], describing her as the sibling holding "everyone's genetic material" and framing the combination of Google's search infrastructure with mass genetic data collection as a component of an emergent shadow-government apparatus.[1] The brief also raised the question of why Susan Wojcicki, with immediate family members in "cure-adjacent fields" and effectively unlimited resources, nonetheless died of lung cancer at 56.[1]

## Bankruptcy and CEO departure (March 2025)

In March 2025 23andMe — the company whose mail-in self-testing kits became synonymous with DNA testing — filed for bankruptcy amid slowing sales, four years after going public.[2] Co-founder [[anne-wojcicki|Anne Wojcicki]], who started the company in 2006, stepped down as chief executive as the firm sought a buyer.[2] In January the company had said it was exploring options for a sale amid slowing demand and the fallout of a major 2023 data breach; in 2024 it agreed to a financial settlement for the breach, which affected 6.9 million users, and announced layoffs of about 40% of its workforce in late 2024.[2] Its stock had recently dipped below a dollar, risking delisting from the NASDAQ.[2] In a note to customers, the company said nothing was changing about how it stores, manages, or protects customer data, that it remained open for business selling DNA kits, and that it would "seek to find a partner who shares our commitment to customer data privacy."[2]

## Genetic data concerns

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] used the bankruptcy to raise the question of what happens to the accumulated genetic material and the derived insights, noting that such data is uniquely identifying and could be used to track or impersonate individuals or commit fraud in their names.[2] She argued a far worse prospect is that genetic data could be used to develop targeted bioweapons — pathogens engineered to harm individuals or demographics based on genetic markers — invoking the gain-of-function debate and Anthony Fauci's congressional admission that he had changed the definition of "gain of function."[2]

Citing Pew Research, the commentary noted that as of 2019 roughly 15% of U.S. adults reported using mail-in DNA testing services such as 23andMe or AncestryDNA — about 37.5 million Americans out of a ~250 million adult population — while 23andMe's 2023 investor report claimed over 14 million genotyped customers worldwide as of February 2024.[2] With bankruptcy, those assets including genetic data could be sold on the open market, and while 23andMe allegedly offers a data-deletion option, users cannot confirm deletion.[2] The piece concluded that "data is the new oil," that Americans' genetic data could plausibly end up with foreign adversaries simply because a company goes out of business, and referenced a U.S. Treasury policy advisor telling an undercover journalist that 23andMe shares consumer data with pharmaceutical companies including the "Ministry of Defense of Russia."[2]
