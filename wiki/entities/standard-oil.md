# Standard Oil

**Standard Oil** was the Rockefeller oil monopoly broken up in 1911 after the U.S. Supreme Court ruled it in violation of federal antitrust laws, splitting it into 34 separate entities.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## Relevance to modern concentration

In *America Has a CIA-Run Economy*, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] invokes the Standard Oil breakup as a precedent when asking whether antitrust action is the path forward for limiting the power and influence that the top 1% of publicly traded American companies now exert over the economy and society.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy) He expects that going forward some mega-corporations will indeed be broken up into smaller companies.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## Cartel with IG Farben

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] writes that Rockefeller's Standard Oil created a new international cartel alongside [[ig-farben|IG Farben]] — then the fourth largest company in the world — in 1929 under the [[young-plan|Young Plan]].[2] The largest of these cartels saw Henry Ford's German operations merging with IG Farben, Dupont industries, Britain's Shell and Standard Oil.[2]

The associated 1928 cartel agreement made it possible for Standard Oil to pass all patents and technologies for the creation of synthetic gasoline from coal to IG Farben, allowing Germany to rise from producing merely 300,000 tons of natural petroleum in 1934 to 6.5 million tons — 85% of its total supply — during the Second World War.[2] Ehret argues that without this transfer the mechanized warfare that characterized [[world-war-ii|World War II]] could never have occurred.[2]

## Partnership with Citibank

Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] writes that [[james-stillman|James Stillman]], who took the reins of the National City Bank in 1891, partnered with the Rockefeller family and Standard Oil as a Robber Baron promoting monopoly power in the United States.[3] Under Stillman's leadership [[citibank|Citibank]] won Standard Oil and the U.S. government as clients and became the first American bank to reach $1 billion in assets.[3]

## Founding, monopoly and dissolution

"The Men Behind the Curtain – Part 5" dates the company's origin to 1863, when [[john-d-rockefeller|John D. Rockefeller]] and several associates built a refinery in Cleveland, Ohio, followed by the incorporation of the Standard Oil Company of Ohio in 1870.[4] Financing came from the National City Bank of Cleveland, described in congressional hearings and by [[eustace-mullins|Eustace Mullins]] as a [[rothschild-family|Rothschild]] bank, while railroad rebates arranged through the holding company South Improvement Co. let Rockefeller undersell rivals; by 1880 the firm controlled 95 percent of U.S. oil production.[4]

Rockefeller created the Standard Oil Trust in 1882, a structure the *New Encyclopaedia Britannica* called "a maze of legal structures, making its workings virtually impervious to public investigation and understanding."[4] After the Ohio Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved in 1892, headquarters moved to New York City and all assets were transferred in 1899 to the Standard Oil Company of New Jersey.[4] The federal government charged the company under the [[sherman-antitrust-act|Sherman Anti-Trust Act]] in 1906, and on May 15, 1911 the Supreme Court declared that "seven men and a corporate machine have conspired against their fellow citizens" and ordered the conspiracy ended by November 15.[4] Journalist [[ida-tarbell|Ida Tarbell]]'s 1902 *History of Standard Oil Company* had helped precipitate this action.[4]

## Successor companies

Eight of the companies formed after dissolution retained "Standard Oil" in their names, though these were soon altered to project diversity.[4] Standard Oil Company of New York merged with Vacuum Oil to form Socony-Vacuum, which became Mobil Oil Corporation in 1966; Standard Oil of Indiana combined with the Nebraska and Kansas companies and became Amoco Corporation by 1985; Standard Oil of California and Standard Oil of Kentucky became [[chevron|Chevron Corporation]] in 1984; and Standard Oil of New Jersey became Exxon Corporation in 1972.[4] Other former Standard companies include Atlantic Richfield, Buckeye Pipeline, Pennzoil and Union Tank Car Company.[4] The breakup left Rockefeller with a quarter share in thirty-three successor firms, and he became the world's first confirmed U.S. dollar billionaire in 1916.[4]
