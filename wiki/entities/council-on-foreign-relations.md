# Council on Foreign Relations

The Council on Foreign Relations (CFR) is an American foreign-policy think tank described by Badlands Media as one of the "elitist policy think tanks" where the ownership class of the mainstream media convenes.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm)

The article states that representatives of the largest institutional investors — [[blackrock|BlackRock]], [[vanguard-group|the Vanguard Group]], [[state-street|State Street]] and [[berkshire-hathaway|Berkshire Hathaway]] — which mutually own the major media conglomerates, "remain regular fixtures" at the CFR and the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm) This overlap underpins the claim that the "big six" media companies function as the propaganda arm of a single Western establishment, "the system of systems."1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm)

By contrast, Badlands defines the [[decentralized-media|decentralized media]] as journalism produced where "there are no emissaries of the think tank class telling journalists what to write and how to frame it."1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm)

## The Iran Deal network

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] counts the Council on Foreign Relations among the think tanks of the old-guard [[foreign-policy-establishment|foreign policy establishment]] that shaped Western policy toward [[iran|Iran]].[2] [[william-burns|William Burns]], lead negotiator of the [[joint-comprehensive-plan-of-action|Iran Deal]], is listed as a CFR member, and [[tom-donilon|Tom Donilon]] as a regular fixture there alongside [[bilderberg|Bilderberg]], the [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]] and the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]].[2]

## Global health funding retrenchment (2025)

In October 2025, [[thomas-bollyky|Thomas Bollyky]], director of the Council's global health program, was quoted in [[politico|Politico]] reporting on the collapse of global health funding, saying that little in the geopolitical picture would give people confidence that this was a great moment for cooperation or significant reinvestment.[3] The article documented cuts by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] since his return to the White House in January, along with aid reductions by nine European countries including the biggest donors, driving overall global health funding to a 15-year low in 2025.[3]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the Council was not truly lamenting the death of health cooperation but the death of globalism, upon which its existence depends, and noted that the experts providing analysis in the article were themselves impacted entities — evidence, in her framing, that the [[invisible-enemy|invisible enemy]] is becoming visible in time for observers to watch its demise.[3]

"The Harvard Control Grid" describes the CFR as "not just a think tank" but "the preeminent American foreign policy syndicate," with a membership spanning ex-presidents, CIA directors, media moguls and multinational CEOs.[7] It names Harvard-linked participants including [[michael-chae|Michael Chae]], [[larry-summers|Larry Summers]] and [[glenn-hutchins|Glenn Hutchins]], who are said to help shape the white papers and task forces driving bipartisan U.S. foreign engagement.[7] The article notes that the Rockefeller family helped found the CFR along with the [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]], and treats CFR membership among [[harvard-management-company|Harvard Management Company]] figures as part of the "shadow scaffolding" through which elite consensus is manufactured rather than merely reflected.[7]

## Origin of the Trilateral Commission

Badlands Media's "The Men Behind the Curtain – Part 2" recaps the CFR as a shadowy think tank created shortly after the First World War and initially funded by early 20th-century "cabal" players including [[john-d-rockefeller|John D. The article argues that for a long period the CFR was the only real ruling power in the United States — until the creation of the [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]].[10] By the late 1960s, CFR chairman [[david-rockefeller|David Rockefeller]] reportedly judged that the council had become too well known, with an unacceptable share of the American public aware of it and conspiracy-minded reporters having a field day; to deflect attention he instigated a slightly more public offshoot that would bring Japan into the globalist fold.[10] The Trilateral Commission is accordingly described as the CFR's spiritual successor, and both are held out by researchers as the epitome of covert organizations guiding public policy against the public's interest and wishes.[10]

Cross-membership between the two bodies is a recurring theme: [[paul-volcker|Paul Volcker]] and [[alan-greenspan|Alan Greenspan]] belonged to the CFR, the Trilateral Commission and the [[bilderberg-group|Bilderberg group]] alike, and many of [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]]'s Trilateral appointees also held CFR membership.[10] The American Legion's 1980 Resolution 773 and a subsequent Veterans of Foreign Wars resolution both called for a congressional investigation of the Trilateral Commission and its predecessor the CFR; Congressman [[larry-mcdonald|Larry McDonald]] introduced them in the House, where they died.[10] Jim Marrs counted twenty-eight CFR members on [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]]'s fifty-nine-member transition team, and Reagan appointed CFR members Alexander Haig, Casper Weinberger and Donald Regan as secretaries of State, Defense and the Treasury.[10] The article closes by noting the claim that the CFR was itself merely a continuation of goals laid down two centuries earlier by the Bavarian Illuminati, carried forward through the Trilateral Commission and now championed by the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]].[10]

## Origins and founding

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]]'s December 2022 essay "The Men Behind the Curtain - Part 1" treats the Council as the central case study in modern secret societies, alongside the [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]], the [[bilderberg-group|Bilderberg Group]] and Klaus Schwab's [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]].[9] It traces the Council's origin to a series of meetings held during the First World War, when [[woodrow-wilson|Woodrow Wilson]]'s confidential advisor [[edward-mandell-house|Edward Mandell House]] gathered roughly 100 of the country's most prominent men — a group that named itself [[the-inquiry|the Inquiry]] — to plan the postwar world.[9] The Inquiry helped produce Wilson's [[fourteen-points|Fourteen Points]], presented to Congress in January 1918, which DeLarme reads as a globalist wish list.[9]

After the U.S. Senate declined to ratify the covenant of the [[league-of-nations|League of Nations]], House met British and American delegates at Paris's Majestic Hotel on 30 May 1919 during the [[paris-peace-conference|Paris Peace Conference]] and resolved to found an "Institute of International Affairs" with branches on both sides of the Atlantic.[9] The English branch became the Royal Institute of International Affairs, today [[chatham-house|Chatham House]]; the American branch was incorporated on 21 July as the Council on Foreign Relations.[9]

## Founding funders and officers

The article states that the Council's primary funders included [[john-d-rockefeller|John D. Rockefeller]], who is alleged to have helped fund the organization, along with international bankers and financiers [[paul-warburg|Paul Warburg]], [[jacob-schiff|Jacob Schiff]], [[otto-kahn|Otto Kahn]] and representatives of the family of J.P. Morgan.[9] DeLarme notes that these same funders were intimately involved in both the [[panic-of-1907|Panic of 1907]] and the ensuing creation of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]].[9]

The CFR's founding president was [[john-w-davis|John W. Davis]], personal attorney to [[jp-morgan-jr|J.P. Morgan Jr.]]; vice president [[paul-cravath|Paul Cravath]] also represented Morgan properties; and the first chairman was [[russell-leffingwell|Russell Leffingwell]], a Morgan partner and trustee of the Carnegie Corporation.[9] Because most early members had Morgan connections, the Council has been described as heavily influenced by Morgan interests.[9]

## Influence and criticism

Journalist [[j-anthony-lucas|J. Anthony Lucas]] wrote that "from 1945 well into the sixties, Council members were in the forefront of America's globalist activism."[9] The CFR's own mission statement describes it as an independent, nonpartisan membership organization, think tank and publisher dedicated to helping members, officials, executives, journalists, educators and citizens better understand the world and the foreign policy choices facing the United States.[9] Critics dispute that framing, arguing the Council has had a hand in every major twentieth-century conflict and is set on world domination through multinational business, international treaties and world government.[9]

Admiral [[chester-ward|Chester Ward]], retired judge advocate general of the U.S. Navy and a longtime member, said the Council's main purpose was "promoting the disarmament of U.S. sovereignty and national independence and submergence into an all-powerful, one-world government," and warned that a second clique of international members comprised Wall Street bankers seeking a world banking monopoly.[9] In the 1975 book [[kissinger-on-the-couch|Kissinger on the Couch]], coauthored with [[phyllis-schlafly|Phyllis Schlafly]], Ward described how once ruling members decide the U.S. government should adopt a policy, CFR research facilities are used to develop intellectual and emotional arguments for it and to discredit opposition.[9]

The Council publishes the journal [[foreign-affairs|Foreign Affairs]], which some believe signals desired policy to those in power; Encyclopaedia Britannica has acknowledged that ideas floated tentatively in the journal often reappear as U.S. government policy or legislation, while those poorly received usually disappear.[9] DeLarme concludes that laws and policies are in effect written not by the people but by the CFR for the benefit of the ruling class.[9]

## Membership and government penetration

Every U.S. administration since the Council's inception has been staffed with CFR members, with the Clinton administration alone reportedly seating more than 100, and even [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s administration was not free of CFR influence.[9] [[gary-allen|Gary Allen]], author of [[none-dare-call-it-conspiracy|None Dare Call It Conspiracy]], remarked before the 1972 elections that voters were offered a choice between "CFR world government advocate Nixon and CFR world government advocate Humphrey," with only the rhetoric changed, and urged both parties to break "Insider control."[9]

The article asserts that almost every CIA director since [[allen-dulles|Allen Dulles]] has been a CFR member, that the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] functions as a security force for friends, relatives and fraternity brothers of the Council, and that the CFR has in turn long been the Agency's principal constituency in the American public.[9] Article II of the CFR bylaws permits dropping any member who reveals details of meetings, which DeLarme argues qualifies the Council as a [[secret-society|secret society]] and has made it the subject of numerous conspiracy theories.[9]

Membership, originally capped at 1,600, now exceeds 5,000 leaders in finance, commerce, communications and academia, admitted through a grueling process requiring proposal by a member, a second, committee approval, professional staff screening and final approval by the Board of Directors.[9] Lucas noted in 1971 that analysts of the Soviet press said the Council appeared more regularly in Pravda and Izvestia than in the New York Times.[9]

Board members cited in the article include [[blackrock|BlackRock]] CEO [[larry-fink|Larry Fink]], Emerson Collective founder [[laurene-powell-jobs|Laurene Powell Jobs]] and Alphabet and Google CFO [[ruth-porat|Ruth Porat]].[9] General membership spans Wall Street figures, international bankers, foundation executives, think tank members, ambassadors, past and present presidents, secretaries of state, lobbyists, media conglomerate owners, university presidents and professors, federal and Supreme Court judges, and military figures from both NATO and the Pentagon.[9] A corporate membership program provides subscribing companies with twice-yearly dinner briefings by officials such as the Treasury Secretary or CIA director; listed corporate members include Apple, Bank of America, BlackRock, Chevron, Citi, ExxonMobil, Goldman Sachs, Google, Meta, PepsiCo, Nasdaq and Morgan Stanley.[9]
