# Hugo Chávez

Hugo Chávez was the president of Venezuela whose 2004 recall referendum provided [[smartmatic|Smartmatic]] with its first national election contract, a fact central to Badlands Media's account of the origins of modern voting technology.[1]

According to the June 9, 2026 *Badlands Brief*, Smartmatic gained its first Venezuelan contract in 2004 ahead of the Chávez recall election, months after receiving investments from the Venezuelan government.[1] The Brief cites *New York Times* reporting that a Venezuelan government financing agency invested over $200,000 in [[bitza|Bitza]], a smaller company sharing owners with Smartmatic, taking a 28 percent stake and a board seat filled by a senior official who had previously advised Chávez on elections technology.[1]

Chávez's name recurs in the later American controversy through a letter from [[john-c-bonifaz|John C. Bonifaz]] to the National Institute of Standards and Technology, which warned that [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]] "depends upon secret source code created and owned by Smartmatic, a foreign controlled company with ties to The Venezuelan Government Led by Hugo Chavez."[1]

## Rise to power

Chávez was a Venezuelan politician, military officer, and revolutionary who served as president of [[venezuela|Venezuela]] from 1999 until his death in 2013, except for a period of roughly forty-seven hours in 2002.[2] His ascent began with the [[caracazo|Caracazo]], the wave of protests, riots, looting and massacres that began on 27 February 1989 in Caracas after neoliberal economic reforms sharply raised gasoline and transportation costs; security forces killed an estimated 2,000–3,000 people over nine days.[2] The public outrage paved the way for Chávez's failed 1992 coup attempt, which landed him in prison but transformed him into a national hero among the impoverished.[2]

Released from prison in 1994, Chávez swept to the presidency in a 1998 landslide.[2] He had not yet publicly declared himself a socialist, but Venezuela's entrenched rulers viewed his embrace of *Bolivarianismo* — invoking [[simon-bolivar|Simón Bolívar]] to champion domestic reform and anti-imperialism — as a mortal threat, and the civil service, judiciary, bureaucracy, state oil company and parts of the military remained hostile strongholds.[2]

## Presidency and confrontation with Washington

For his first five years Chávez was forced into a defensive posture.[2] In April 2002 he survived a [[2002-venezuelan-coup-attempt|U.S.-backed coup]] and was restored after 48 hours by mass loyalist protests; months later the business elite launched an "owners' strike" that paralyzed petroleum production and cut GDP by an estimated 27 percent before collapsing in early 2003.[2] The opposition's final serious constitutional attempt to remove him, the August 2004 recall referendum, he won with 58 percent of the vote; his coalition then took 20 of 22 governorships and 270 of 337 municipalities, and he was re-elected in 2006 with more than 62 percent, carrying every state.[2]

Beneath this surface story, Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] describes a quieter war pitting Chávez against the U.S. foreign policy machine of intelligence agencies, think tanks, NGOs and corporate contractors.[2] [[john-perkins|John Perkins]], the self-described "economic hit man," named Chávez in his 2004 memoir as a president who refused to play the game: "He nationalized his country's oil, used the revenues to help the poor, and told us to go to hell. So we labeled him a dictator and set out to overthrow him."[2] Knowing that [[pdvsa|PDVSA]] — then among the world's top five crude exporters at 3.5 million barrels a day — was the ultimate prize, Chávez fired 19,000 PDVSA managers in 2002–2003, replaced them with loyalists, and redirected petrodollars into social programs.[2]

Washington's response was swift: the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] quadrupled its Venezuela budget, funneling millions to opposition NGOs including [[sumate|Súmate]] and [[primero-justicia|Primero Justicia]], while U.S. military advisors quietly revived the [[plan-avila|Plan Ávila]] counterinsurgency blueprint.[2] When Chávez began trading oil for Cuban doctors, Chinese rail stock and Russian Sukhois — bypassing the dollar and traditional middlemen — the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] began drafting the sanctions that would later become the maximum-pressure campaign.[2] DeLarme concludes that Chávez's sin was not incompetence but defiance: he proved a country could nationalize its crown jewel, fund social programs, and still pay its debts.[2]

On 20 September 2006, Chávez delivered a speech to the UN General Assembly describing [[george-w-bush|George W. Bush]]'s address of the previous day as "The Devil's Recipe," remarking: "Yesterday the devil came here, in this very place, it still smells of sulphur."[2]

## Death and poisoning claims

Chávez reportedly died of a heart attack in 2013 while recovering from prolonged respiratory and other complications following multiple surgeries and chemotherapy for cancerous tumors in Cuba.[2] Suspicions of assassination were intensified by the unclear details surrounding his illness and treatment.[2] In 2022, [[igor-kirillov|Igor Kirillov]], commander of the radiological, chemical and biological defense forces of the Russian Armed Forces, claimed Chávez had been poisoned by his nurse from 2003 to 2011, Claudia Díaz, who allegedly collaborated with U.S. special services and later moved to the United States; Kirillov said Russian doctors and forensic examination found an atypical course to the illness.[2] Kirillov was himself assassinated in 2024.[2]

By the time of Chávez's death the quieter war had become an open one of asset freezes, secondary sanctions, recognition of parallel governments, and what DeLarme describes as a refugee exodus engineered to bleed [[chavismo|the revolution]] dry.[2] His hand-picked successor was [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]].[2]

## Solidarity Center funding of opposition unions

"Two Wings, One Ugly Bird" reports that the [[solidarity-center|Solidarity Center]] poured cash into Venezuelan labour groups opposing Chávez in the early 2000s, at the same time as the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] was plotting his ouster.[3] Declassified cables from 2002 are cited as showing the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] funnelling $154,000 through the Center to anti-Chávez unions, synchronised with a failed coup attempt.[3]
