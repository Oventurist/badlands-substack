# China

China is the world's largest importer of crude oil and, in Badlands Media's framing, one of the three pillars of the emerging [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] alongside the United States and [[russia|Russia]].[1]

## Role in the 2026 energy shock

During the [[iran-war|Iran conflict]] of 2026, China sharply reduced its crude oil imports, a move that analysis published by [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] argued may have prevented a far more severe global energy crisis. Because China is the largest importer, its unexpected demand drop freed millions of barrels per day for other countries and helped keep oil prices well below the $150–$200 per barrel levels many analysts had feared after disruptions in the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]].[1]

The exact reason for the import cuts remains debated. Analysts have pointed to a combination of large strategic oil stockpiles, lower refinery activity, growing electric vehicle adoption, and years of investment in alternative energy that reduced dependence on imported crude. Beijing has released little official data, leaving outside observers uncertain how long the strategy can be sustained. Some analysts caution the reduction may be temporary: if China resumes normal purchasing while Middle East disruptions persist, global oil markets could tighten rapidly and prices rise significantly.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized The Atlantic's treatment as "rudderless analysis" — an earnest attempt to reconcile an irreconcilable set of facts while avoiding the explanation staring the authors in the face. The Atlantic speculated that China was using its blunting of the energy crisis as a form of soft power projection, and that Beijing might not want the world to know it needs foreign economies stable so its exports can continue. Burning Bright argued this reading ignores that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], by reducing tariffs and sanctions on Russian oil bound for India and China during the height of the conflict, mirrored [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]]'s silent "move of no move."[1]

The Atlantic also raised, as an aside, the possibility that Russia was supplying China under the table — a scenario Burning Bright judged not only possible but highly probable. In his view this closes the Sovereign Alliance loop: all three pillars appear to have helped one another withstand a shock that should have followed Trump's Iran war, a war China and Russia both publicly opposed while evidently possessing foreknowledge that it would occur. He concluded that Sovereign Alliance coordination is the only thing keeping the globalist economy from shattering overnight as [[sovereign-disentanglement|disentanglement]] proceeds.[1]

## Economic pressure on rivals

China's competitiveness is also cited as a driver of industrial decline elsewhere. The [[federation-of-german-industries|Federation of German Industries]] lists increased competition from China among the causes of Germany's manufacturing crisis, and weaker sales in the Chinese market are among the pressures behind restructuring at [[bmw|BMW]] and other German automakers.[1]

## Consecutive American and Russian visits (May 2026)

In May 2026 China received U.S. President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] for a state visit covering trade, Taiwan and the [[iran-war|Iran conflict]], and days later hosted Russian President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] in Beijing on 19–20 May for talks with [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] marking the 25th anniversary of the 2001 Sino-Russian Treaty of Friendship.[2] Chinese state media called the sequence unusual and indicative of Beijing's growing diplomatic influence, and quoted Xi describing China–Russia cooperation as having "continuously deepened and solidified."[2] Bilateral political and economic ties expanded markedly after [[russian-invasion-of-ukraine|Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine]] increased Moscow's dependence on Chinese trade and energy purchases.[2]

## March 2026: proposed Ethnic Unity Law

In March 2026, China's legislature was expected to advance a proposed [[ethnic-unity-law|Ethnic Unity Law]] aimed at strengthening what the government calls national cohesion among the country's 56 officially recognized ethnic groups.[3] The proposal, under consideration by the [[national-peoples-congress|National People's Congress]], would formalize policies promoting ethnic integration and unity nationwide.[3]

Critics argued the legislation could codify pressure on minority groups to assimilate into Han Chinese cultural and political norms, particularly in regions with large minority populations, and human rights organizations warned it could expand existing programs affecting communities such as the [[uyghurs|Uyghurs]] and Tibetans.[3] Chinese officials said the law was intended to promote unity, stability, and development in minority regions, which Beijing has long described as a national priority.[3]

## AI infrastructure mobilization

In June 2026 China was reported to be preparing a roughly 2 trillion yuan ($295 billion) investment over five years to build a nationwide network of interconnected data centers and computing hubs, an effort designed to scale domestic AI infrastructure and position the country to lead in a technology seen as strategically decisive through the 2030s.[4] The [[china-ai-data-center-buildout|buildout]] prioritizes Chinese suppliers such as [[huawei|Huawei]] for at least 80 percent of core technology, aiming to displace [[nvidia|Nvidia]] and [[amd|AMD]], and forms a central element of Beijing's [[six-networks|Six Networks]] mobilization integrating compute with power, water and logistics.[4]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued the resulting "AI arms race" framing with Washington in fact ratifies the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] between [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]], each leaning into the rivalry to supply the other with [[narrative-shielding|Narrative Shielding]] for a shared energy-and-compute buildout.[4]

## Nord Stream probe and the new world order (2023)

In March 2023 China's foreign ministry publicly backed Russia's draft [[united-nations-security-council|UN Security Council]] resolution calling for an international independent investigation commission into the [[nord-stream-sabotage|Nord Stream pipeline sabotage]], with state-run Xinhua reporting the spokesperson's remarks at a regular press briefing.[5] Beijing and Moscow treated the refusal of the United States and other Western Council members to allow such a commission as an admission of guilt and as evidence that Western allies were obfuscating the investigation.[5]

The same month, following the Moscow summit at which [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] told [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] that "change is coming that hasn't happened in 100 years and we are driving this change together" — to which Putin replied "I agree" — Beijing offered the United States advice on the war in Ukraine.[5] Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the episode alongside a strengthened Russian ruble, a Western banking crisis cascade and looming European strikes as evidence that the [[multipolar-world|multipolar world order]] was consolidating, summarizing the moment as "Power. Is. Power."[5]

## 2024 ban on Intel and AMD chips in government computers

In March 2024, China introduced new procurement guidelines phasing US chips from [[intel|Intel]] and [[amd|AMD]] out of government PCs and servers, part of a campaign to replace foreign technology with homegrown solutions, as reported by the Financial Times.[6] The move was retaliation after the United States acted to prevent Chinese companies from acquiring the latest Nvidia AI chips and blocked ASML from shipping advanced chipmaking machines to Beijing — restrictions that had produced a one-time surge of Chinese orders ahead of the ban.[6] The stricter guidance also sought to sideline Microsoft's Windows operating system and foreign-made database software in favour of domestic options, and ran alongside a parallel localization drive within state-owned enterprises.[6] The FT described the purchasing rules as China's most significant step yet toward building domestic substitutes for foreign technology, echoing moves in the United States as tensions between the two countries increased.[6]


## Response to US secondary tariffs over Russian oil (September 2025)

In September 2025 China condemned U.S. efforts to pressure [[g7|G7]] and [[nato|NATO]] countries into imposing tariffs on Beijing over its purchases of Russian oil, warning it would retaliate if its interests were harmed.[7] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had repeatedly called for pressure on [[russia|Russia]]'s trading partners to stop buying its oil, arguing this could help end the Ukraine conflict, and the Financial Times reported that Washington had asked G7 finance ministers to consider secondary tariffs of 50–100% on Chinese and Indian imports tied to Russian oil.[7] Trump also urged the [[european-union|EU]] to impose tariffs of up to 100% on goods from Beijing and New Delhi.[7]

Foreign Ministry spokesperson [[lin-jian|Lin Jian]] said it was "fully legitimate and lawful" for Beijing to maintain normal economic, trade and energy cooperation with all countries including Russia, and described Washington's request as "a typical move of unilateralism, bullying, and economic coercion."[7] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the exchange as "Wrestlemania-styled rhetoric among the Big Three," masking a real economic decoupling between East and West that is making Asia more self-reliant and collapsing the globalized supply chain.[7] Separately, Trump invoked China in arguing that U.S. firms should abandon quarterly reporting, though Chinese companies do file quarterly reports, with only Hong Kong-listed firms reporting semiannually.[7]

## Surveillance balloon over Montana (2023)

On February 2, 2023, the [[department-of-defense|Department of Defense]] confirmed that an object that had prompted a two-hour shutdown of [[billings-logan-international-airport|Billings Logan International Airport]] the previous day was an intelligence-gathering balloon "most certainly launched by the People's Republic of China."[8] Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that China's interest in Montana lay in the state's US intercontinental ballistic missile facilities rather than its agriculture, writing that "they ain't looking for more farmland."[8] The episode is treated at [[chinese-spy-balloon-incident-2023|Chinese spy balloon incident (2023)]].[8]

## US munitions buildup, September 2025

The *Wall Street Journal* reported on 29 September 2025 that the United States was working to ramp up missile production in preparation for a potential conflict with China, with the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] pressing defense contractors to double or quadruple output amid concern over insufficient weapons stockpiles.[9] The effort, launched in June 2025 and known as the [[munitions-acceleration-council|Munitions Acceleration Council]], focuses on twelve weapons the Pentagon wants on hand for such a conflict.[9] [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the report as the start of a hype cycle for a long-anticipated conflict over Taiwan, while questioning how defense-industry collaboration with senior Pentagon officials differed from business as usual.[9]

## Support for Venezuela and the EUV breakthrough (December 2025)

China joined [[russia|Russia]] in expressing support for [[venezuela|Venezuela]] in December 2025 as it confronted the US [[2025-venezuela-oil-tanker-blockade|blockade]] of sanctioned oil tankers amid President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s pressure campaign against [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolas Maduro]].[10]

Reuters reported the same month that Chinese scientists in a high-security Shenzhen laboratory had built a prototype extreme ultraviolet lithography machine - described as China's "Manhattan Project" to rival the West in AI chips - by reverse-engineering the systems of the Dutch giant [[asml|ASML]]; the [[china-euv-lithography-program|program]]'s prototype was operational and generating EUV light but had not yet produced working chips.[10]

## Shrinking share of the global economy (2022–2023)

In November 2023 it was reported that China's rise as an economic superpower had gone into reverse, with its share of the global economy shrinking at the fastest pace since the [[mao-zedong|Mao]] era.[11] After stagnating in the 1960s and 1970s, China opened to the world in the 1980s and its share of world output rose nearly tenfold, from below 2 per cent in 1990 to 18.4 per cent in 2021, a rise without historical precedent in speed or scale.[11] The share then shrank slightly in 2022 and more significantly in 2023, to about 17 per cent — a two-year drop of 1.4 percentage points, the largest since the 1960s.[11]

The figures were expressed in nominal dollar terms, unadjusted for inflation, the measure said to most accurately capture relative economic strength, and were drawn from China's own official GDP data rather than foreign or independent sources, meaning the reversal was recorded "by Beijing's own account."[11] Commentary noted that China's ambition to reclaim the imperial economic status it held from the 16th to the early 19th centuries, when its share of world output peaked at roughly one-third, might be slipping out of reach.[11]

Since the 1990s China's gains had come mainly at the expense of Europe and Japan, whose shares held roughly steady over the two-year period, while the gap left by China was filled by the United States and other emerging nations.[11] Of the roughly $8 trillion of expected world economic growth in 2022 and 2023 — bringing global output to $105 trillion — China was projected to account for none, the US for 45 per cent, and other emerging nations for 50 per cent, with half the emerging-market gain coming from India, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil and Poland.[11]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] read the news "bicamerally," arguing that the diversion of China's economic domination plan toward interior stability and job creation — rather than sustaining production for what he called the American Oligarchy — was simultaneously good for American nationalism and production and for Chinese nationalism and production, and a sign of rising sovereignty and future trade agreements.[11]

## Geneva tariff truce (May 2025)

On Monday, May 12, 2025, the United States and China agreed in Geneva to lower tariffs on each other's imports for 90 days, an attempt to cool an escalating trade war between the world's two biggest economies.[12] Under the [[2025-us-china-geneva-tariff-agreement|joint statement]], US tariffs on Chinese goods fell from 145% to 30% and Chinese duties on US imports fell from 125% to 10%, effective 14 May.[12] Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] said neither delegation wanted a decoupling and that the prior levels amounted to "the equivalent of an embargo."[12] Chinese equities were upgraded to a "tactical overweight" by Nomura analysts even as Hong Kong's Hang Seng Index fell 1.74% and the mainland CSI 300 was flat.[12]

Badlands commentary treated the episode skeptically, with [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] arguing that "China isn't the enemy, the people in Geneva are," and noting the near-identical set of China-related deals Trump had secured in 2020 and 2025 — a trade deal, an agreement with [[xi-jinping|Xi]] to stop the fentanyl trade, and a plan for TikTok.[12]

## Accusations of aiding Russia's war machine (September 2024)

On Tuesday, September 10, 2024, U.S. Deputy Secretary of State [[kurt-campbell|Kurt Campbell]] said after meetings with European Union and NATO counterparts in Brussels that Beijing is giving Moscow "very substantial" help to beef up its war machine, and that in return [[russia|Russia]] is handing over closely guarded military technology on submarines and missiles.[13] Washington had previously focused on Beijing's supply of dual-use technologies applicable to military or civilian purposes, but now stated unambiguously that China is aiding the Russian military, which needs technology to sustain production under international sanctions in its war on [[ukraine|Ukraine]].[13] "These are not dual-use capabilities," Campbell said. "These are basically being applied directly to the Russian war machine."[13]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the Media Industrial Complex would use such statements to cast an emergent Russia–China–Iran military triumvirate as a "New Axis" posing an existential threat to the Globalist West, noting that Russia had announced its "Ocean 2024" global military exercises which China would join, while both backed Iran publicly amid spiraling Israel chaos.[13] He predicted [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] would allow this narrative before codifying peace and trade deals already settled.[13]
