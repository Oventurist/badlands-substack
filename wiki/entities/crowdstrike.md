# CrowdStrike

CrowdStrike is an American cybersecurity company whose endpoint-protection software is deployed across business, healthcare, technology and government networks worldwide. On Friday, July 19, 2024, a defective CrowdStrike software update triggered a global outage that brought many of those systems "to a screeching halt in under 12 hours," disrupting 911 emergency call centers, transportation services, global banks, airlines, hospitals and motor vehicle agencies.[1]

## The July 2024 global outage

According to a CNN timeline of the event, the disruption began late Thursday night and early Friday with failures at 911 centers and transportation services; between 6 and 7 a.m. Eastern the [[white-house|White House]] began investigating and global banks reported issues; between 8 and 9 a.m. some flights resumed while airports braced for a heavy day; between 9 a.m. and noon federal agencies became involved and President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] was briefed; late morning through early afternoon brought canceled doctor and DMV appointments; the late afternoon saw further medical disruptions; and Saturday morning brought massive flight delays.[1] The incident is treated in more depth at [[2024-crowdstrike-global-outage]].

## Reception in the Truth Community

Badlands Media commentators rejected the framing of the outage as an accident. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that it was "hard to believe that this Crowdstrike thing is an organic occurrence," describing it instead as "a massive red pill" on two fronts: the fragility of smart devices and data — citing social-media reports of internet-connected refrigerators locking shut during the outage — and the vulnerability of election infrastructure, since electronic voting equipment is connected to the internet, a claim long advanced on the Badlands program [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]] against assurances about "air gapped machines."[1] She framed the episode as a demonstration of the trade-offs of smart thermostats, doorbells and voice assistants, invoking government-mandated thermostat caps and social credit scoring as the logical endpoints.[1]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] added that the company "is infamous in the Truth Community for the role it did or did not play in the 2020 election," noted that the dominant worldwide question was whether the outage resulted from a cyber attack (denied in mainstream reporting), and linked the episode to the mystery of Department of Defense IP address space transferred to an obscure Florida company as [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] left office in January 2021, to [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s [[starlink|Starlink]], and to the resilience of the [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] network, which was unaffected while banking networks failed.[1] Both commentators tied the outage to expectations of a false-flag cyber event ahead of the November 2024 election.[1]

## First congressional hearing on the outage (September 2024)

A House Homeland Security subcommittee announced that it would hold the first congressional hearing on the global CrowdStrike outage on September 24, 2024, a proceeding described as a bellwether for how much trust the company had lost both in Washington and among its own customers.[2] Adam Meyers, senior vice president of counter adversary operations at CrowdStrike, was named as the witness before the cybersecurity subcommittee.[2] Rep. [[mark-green|Mark Green]] (R-Tenn.), the committee chair, said that "while we can be thankful that this wasn't a cyberattack, this incident demonstrates the urgency of promoting cyber hygiene and resiliency amid increased threats."[2] CrowdStrike spokesperson Kevin Benacci said the company continued to "actively and collaboratively work with relevant Congressional committees."[2] Lawmakers had originally called on CEO George Kurtz to testify following the outage, which affected roughly 8.5 million Windows devices.[2]

## Delta's threatened lawsuit (July 2024)

On Wednesday, July 31, 2024, [[delta-air-lines|Delta Air Lines]] CEO [[ed-bastian|Ed Bastian]] announced that the global IT outage sparked by CrowdStrike had cost the airline $500 million, and said the carrier would seek damages, telling CNBC's "Squawk Box": "We have no choice."[3] Bastian said that a vendor with "priority access to the Delta ecosystem" had an obligation to test its software and could not "come into a mission critical 24/7 operation and tell us we have a bug."[3] The faulty update had caused Delta to cancel more than 4,000 flights that month.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the most notable implication of the CrowdStrike failure was its bearing on US critical infrastructure — weapons systems, water and utilities, and elections — and that the airline storyline, while the least interesting, kept the company in the headlines and created an opening to educate the public.[3] She argued that the pending litigation threatened to bankrupt CrowdStrike, an entity providing technical services across many industry sectors and geographies, which in turn implied significant vulnerabilities across the company's client ecosystem.[3] The suit was also framed as a test of the contractual terms of public-private partnerships in critical infrastructure and a concrete challenge to what she called the regime's ubiquitous plausible deniability.[3]
