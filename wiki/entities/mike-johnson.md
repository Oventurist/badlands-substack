# Mike Johnson

Mike Johnson is the Speaker of the United States House of Representatives. In "A Glaring Double Standard," Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] notes that Johnson facilitated the House press conference at which [[eyal-yakoby|Eyal Yakoby]] spoke, held before the presidents of the [[university-of-pennsylvania|University of Pennsylvania]], Harvard, and MIT testified before [[congress|Congress]] on campus antisemitism.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

Yakoby praised Johnson for showing "moral clarity" on the issue of antisemitism.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) Johnson's role is presented as part of the institutional apparatus through which post–October 7 campus speech grievances were channeled into congressional action, a trajectory DeLarme links to the [[antisemitism-awareness-act|Antisemitism Awareness Act]] and broader [[government-sponsored-censorship|government-sponsored censorship]] efforts.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Support for Howard Lutnick (2026)

During the February 2026 fallout from the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]]'s release of [[jeffrey-epstein|Epstein]] documents, House Speaker Johnson joined the [[white-house|White House]] in continuing to express support for Commerce Secretary [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]], dismissing demands for his resignation as absurd.[2]

## House Code of Official Conduct expansion (2026)

In July 2026 Johnson endorsed expanding the House Code of Official Conduct to ban lawmakers from sexual relationships with any congressional staff, not only subordinates.[3] The existing rule, adopted in 2018, prohibits intimate relationships with subordinates in personal offices or on committees, with an exception for married staffers; the proposed change would remove the subordinate limitation.[3]

The move came alongside [[kat-cammack|Kat Cammack]]'s push for a vote on [[h-res-500|H.Res. 500]] requiring staff background checks, and drew the same reaction from Badlands commentary: [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the proposal backfired because the public was astonished such a rule did not already exist, citing the Senate room sex tape and other scandals as proof that [[congress|Congress]] is unfit to police itself.[3]

## 2026 State of the Union seating

Ahead of the February 2026 [[state-of-the-union|State of the Union address]], Johnson told reporters that Capitol leadership was working to accommodate the US men's Olympic hockey team, whom [[donald-trump|President Trump]] had invited following their gold-medal win, given the size of the group and the space limitations in the House chamber.[4] "There's no way to have special guests on the floor because it's a literal session of Congress," Johnson said. "But we're going to work and do what we can to accommodate."[4]

## Pausing the Clinton contempt resolutions (2026)

On February 3, 2026, Johnson announced that criminal contempt of Congress efforts against [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] were "on pause" after House Republicans finalized a deal for the couple to testify under oath in the [[house-committee-on-oversight-and-accountability|House Oversight Committee]]'s [[jeffrey-epstein|Epstein]] investigation.[5] The contempt resolutions, which carried potential fines or imprisonment, had drawn some bipartisan support, including nine Democratic votes against Bill Clinton and three against Hillary Clinton.[5]

## Statements on the 2026 Iran operation

In March 2026 House Speaker Mike Johnson and other Republican lawmakers said in congressional briefings that [[israel|Israel]] was prepared to attack [[iran|Iran]] "with or without American support," and that US involvement in the joint strike campaign reflected planning to protect American forces from anticipated Iranian retaliation.[6] The briefings accompanied a closed-door session led by Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] on the operation.[6]

## February 2026 shutdown vote

During the partial federal government shutdown that began on January 31, 2026, the Senate passed a bipartisan spending package funding most agencies through the end of the fiscal year while extending [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] funding short-term, returning the legislation to the House where Speaker Johnson faced divisions within his Republican conference.[7] A bloc of House Republicans sought to attach the [[save-act|SAVE Act]] to the spending bill, an approach opposed by Democrats and absent from the Senate package, while President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] urged House Republicans to pass the Senate measure as written.[7] Johnson's leadership indicated a vote to reopen the government was expected once members returned to Washington, with negotiations focused on whether the package would proceed cleanly or carry additional policy conditions; see [[january-2026-government-shutdown-standoff|the January 2026 government shutdown standoff]].[7]

## June 2026 floor freeze

In late June 2026 Johnson lost control of the House floor when a small group of Republican hard-liners defeated the chamber's procedural rule 224-198, with 14 Republicans joining Democrats, extending a week-long impasse over the stalled [[save-america-act|SAVE America Act]].[8] Rep. [[chip-roy|Chip Roy]] cited Johnson's broken promise to hold a vote on the House-passed immigration bill HR 2 before recess as his principal reason for opposing the rule.[8] Johnson and Majority Leader [[steve-scalise|Steve Scalise]] had planned to resolve the disputes over the following day-and-a-half but instead cancelled votes and began the July 4 recess early, shelving the annual [[pentagon|Pentagon]] policy bill and the fiscal 2027 [[state-department|State Department]] spending measure; see [[june-2026-house-rule-failure|the rule failure]].[8]

## Conference discontent after the MTG retirement (November 2025)

Following Rep. [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]]'s November 2025 announcement that she would leave [[congress|Congress]] within 42 days, ZeroHedge reported — citing Punchbowl News — that other House Republicans were weighing early exits and blamed both the [[white-house|White House]] and Johnson.[9] One member said the White House team "has treated ALL members like garbage" and that "Mike Johnson has let it happen because he wanted it to happen," describing the sentiment as near-universal among appropriators, authorizers, hawks, doves and rank-and-file members alike.[9] The same member predicted that Johnson "will be stripped of his gavel and they will lose the majority before this term is out," and characterized the conference as "a tinder box" with morale at a historic low.[9]

## Foreign aid bills (April 2024)

On April 17, 2024, House Republicans released the text of separate [[ukraine|Ukraine]], [[israel|Israel]] and [[taiwan|Taiwan]] aid bills after months of delay, with spending levels roughly in line with the $95 billion foreign aid package the Senate had passed in a broad bipartisan vote that February.[10] The three measures included roughly $26 billion for Israel, $61 billion for Ukraine, and $8.12 billion for Taiwan and the Indo-Pacific.[10] The bills met immediate repudiation from Johnson's right flank, creating obstacles to passage and making it almost certain that he would need Democratic help to bring all three to the House floor and pass them.[10]

## Democratic protection against the motion to vacate (2024)

On April 30, 2024, House Democratic leadership confirmed long-rumored plans to save Johnson's speakership: if Rep. [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]] (R-Ga.) introduced a motion to oust him, they would help kill it.[11] Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] and his deputies, Reps. Katherine Clark (D-Mass.) and Pete Aguilar (D-Calif.), said in a statement, "We will vote to table Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene's Motion to Vacate the Chair," adding that "if she invokes the motion, it will not succeed."[11] The Democratic leaders pointed to the foreign aid bill that had passed earlier that month, declaring that "upon completion of our national security work, the time has come to turn the page on this chapter of Pro-Putin Republican obstruction."[11] Only two right-wing House Republicans had signed onto Greene's motion, enough to remove Johnson only if virtually every Democrat voted with them.[11]

## Election as Speaker (October 2023)

Johnson was elected Speaker of the House in late October 2023, ending a three-week leadership void created by the ouster of Rep. [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] (R-Calif.).[12] The GOP conference chose him as its speaker nominee and he won 220 votes in the first floor ballot on a Wednesday.[12] He had been runner-up to Rep. [[tom-emmer|Tom Emmer]] (R-Minn.), the third speaker nominee, who withdrew his name hours after receiving the conference nomination; Reps. [[steve-scalise|Steve Scalise]] (R-La.) and [[jim-jordan|Jim Jordan]] (R-Ohio) had failed earlier to garner the needed support.[12]

Rep. [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]] (R-Fla.), who led the motion to vacate against McCarthy, presented Johnson's victory as proof of the strength of the "MAGA movement" and the waning power of the "swamp," telling [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]]'s "War Room" podcast that "moving from Kevin McCarthy to MAGA Mike Johnson shows the ascendance of this movement and where the power in the Republican Party truly lies."[12] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] admitted he had not foreseen Johnson's rise but agreed with Gaetz's framing, describing the elevation as a proxy for the [[america-first-movement|America First]] and [[donald-trump|Trump]]-inspired movement and a key indicator of where real power in the nation lies regardless of Johnson's eventual legislative record.[12] See [[2023-speaker-of-the-house-election]].

## Surviving the May 2024 motion to vacate

On 8 May 2024 the [[house-of-representatives|House]] overwhelmingly voted to block a [[may-2024-motion-to-vacate-against-speaker-mike-johnson|motion to vacate]] brought by Representative [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]] to strip Johnson of the gavel.[13] Greene forced the vote after meeting with the Speaker twice that week to discuss her grievances; Majority Whip [[steve-scalise|Steve Scalise]] then moved to table her resolution, and Democrats joined Republicans to shelve it 359 to 43, with 11 Republicans voting to advance the ouster.[13] House Democratic leaders had pledged beforehand to protect Johnson, citing his help in passing $95 billion in foreign aid for [[ukraine|Ukraine]], [[israel|Israel]] and the Indo-Pacific.[13] Johnson had previously denounced Greene's effort as a "dangerous gambit," and stated that his meeting with her had not been a negotiation.[13]

## September 2024 stopgap spending deal

In September 2024, congressional leaders announced agreement on a short-term spending bill funding federal agencies for about three months, averting a possible partial government shutdown at the start of the new budget year on October 1 and pushing final decisions past the November election.[14] At the urging of the most conservative members of his conference, Johnson had initially linked temporary funding to a mandate compelling states to require proof of citizenship for voter registration, but he abandoned that approach to reach a deal — even as [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] insisted there should be no stop-gap measure without the voting requirement.[14] The bill funded agencies at current levels while adding $231 million to bolster the [[us-secret-service|Secret Service]] after two assassination attempts against Trump, plus additional money for the presidential transition.[14]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode as one of a series of "Kobayashi Maru" no-win scenarios confronting the Deep State, arguing that the "adults in the room" in [[congress|Congress]] spent more time capitulating to the systemic status quo than acting — which he took to be the point, all setting the stage for Trump's return.[14]

## Deference to Trump (2025 reporting)

An October 2025 Wall Street Journal report, summarized by The Independent, cited Johnson's attendance at the 40th birthday party of Deputy White House Chief of Staff [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] at Ned's Club in Washington, D.C. on August 23 — where he paid tribute to the official — as an example of congressional Republicans bending to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s will.[15] The same report quoted [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] likening the Republican-controlled [[congress|Congress]] to Russia's largely ceremonial Duma, while senior administration advisers joked about ruling Capitol Hill with an "iron fist."[15]

## Planned rescheduling executive order (December 2025)

In December 2025 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was reported to be preparing to push the federal government to dramatically loosen restrictions on marijuana, reducing oversight of the plant and its derivatives to the same level as some common prescription painkillers and other drugs, according to six people familiar with the discussions.[16] Trump discussed the plan with House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] during a Wednesday phone call from the Oval Office, and was expected to ease access through an executive order directing federal agencies to pursue reclassification.[16]

The move would not legalize or decriminalize marijuana, but it would ease barriers to research and improve the finances of legal cannabis businesses.[16] Badlands contributor [[jordan-sather|Jordan Sather]] characterized the plan as a shift from Schedule I to Schedule III and predicted it would "blow so many left wing AND right wing minds."[16]

## November 2023 shutdown deadline

As Speaker of the House, Johnson faced a government funding deadline of November 17, 2023, with House Republicans showing no sign on November 7 of coalescing around a plan to keep federal agencies open.[17] Republican lawmakers, holding a slim 221-212 majority, met behind closed doors to debate how to structure a [[continuing-resolution|continuing resolution]].[17] Johnson described the closed-door debate as "positive," telling a news conference: "We certainly want to avoid a government shutdown. It's a dangerous time around the world right now. We recognize that," and adding, "We'll be revealing what our plan is in short order."[17]

Republicans, who said they feared being forced to back a rival funding measure from the Democratic-led [[us-senate|Senate]], expected Johnson to unveil a stopgap within days and to hold a House vote early the following week; Representative Dusty Johnson predicted the speaker would "make a play call" that the overwhelming majority of the conference would back.[17]

## December 2025 health care vote

As Speaker of the House, Johnson barreled toward a Wednesday vote in mid-December 2025 on a health care bill that he and other Republican leaders presented as an alternative to the Obamacare tax credits set to expire at the end of the month, with no plans to allow a vote before then on extending the subsidies.[18] A band of vulnerable House Republicans who had long warned about the expiration of the subsidies signaled they would fall in line, among them Rep. [[brian-fitzpatrick|Brian Fitzpatrick]] of Pennsylvania, who said he had not seen anything objectionable in the plan and would not vote against it in protest.[18]

## Epstein controversy and the 2026 midterms

In July 2025 Fox News host [[mark-levin|Mark Levin]] urged Johnson to move on from the controversy over the Trump administration's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein case, calling it a "waste of time."[19] Levin's remarks followed Johnson's own emphasis on the stakes of the 2026 midterms: "everything is on the line in the midterm election in 2026," Johnson said, arguing that if "the radical, woke, progressive, Marxist Democrats" retook the House majority they would impeach President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] on the first day of that Congress in early January 2027 and the country "would descend again into chaos" through renewed lawfare.[19] Badlands commentator Chris Paul rejected the framing outright, holding that elections are fake and midterm leverage arguments unserious.[19]

## Opposition to the 2024 Border-Ukraine Deal

In late January 2024, as House Speaker, Johnson responded to President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s call for [[congress|Congress]] to pass a bipartisan Senate border-and-Ukraine package by suggesting the compromise on border and immigration policy could be "dead on arrival" in his chamber.[20] In a letter addressing the negotiations he wrote that "rather than accept accountability, President Biden is now trying to blame Congress for what HE himself intentionally created."[20] Johnson's resistance, alongside public opposition from [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], contributed to the collapse of Senate Republican support for the deal, with potentially far-reaching consequences for U.S. allies — Ukraine in particular — that depended on the attached $110 billion emergency funding request.[20]

## Killing the 2024 border bill

On Sunday, February 4, 2024, Johnson announced on X that the Senate's [[bipartisan-border-bill-2024|bipartisan border bill]] was dead on arrival in the House of Representatives.[21] Many House Republicans reacted with open anger to the package negotiated between Senate leaders [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] and [[mitch-mcconnell|Mitch McConnell]], which critics said contained instant amnesty provisions and a daily migrant threshold of 8,500 while directing more money overseas than to the border itself.[21]

## Election as Speaker (October 2023)

Johnson was elected Speaker on Wednesday, October 25, 2023, after three weeks in which the House had no leader, winning 220 votes to 209 for Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] without losing a single Republican vote.[22] Coverage noted his standing as a [[donald-trump|Trump]] ally and his role as a key congressional figure in the failed effort to overturn the 2020 election, including a 2020 email sent from a personal account to every House Republican soliciting signatures for an amicus brief in the Texas lawsuit seeking to invalidate electoral college votes from multiple states.[22] He inherited a looming shutdown deadline of November 17 that would require cooperation with the Democratic-led Senate, along with pending decisions on aid to [[israel|Israel]] and [[ukraine|Ukraine]].[22] Pledging an "aggressive schedule," he presided over a first vote in which the House passed a bipartisan resolution supporting Israel.[22]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described Johnson as strong in committee and among the most conservative members of the House, but called his election anticlimactic in an era of collapsed institutional trust.[22] She criticized his gracious, bipartisan-toned first address as feeling "almost like a betrayal" amid national division, and faulted his stated priority order — Israel first, then the US border, then Ukraine — as out of step with the American people's priorities, concluding that she was "less than optimistic" that he would prove the fighter needed to restore [[america-first-movement|America First]] principles.[22]

## Laddered continuing resolution and impeachment posture (November 2023)

On Saturday, November 11, 2023, just two and a half weeks into the job, Johnson unveiled a stopgap funding bill to avert a government shutdown set to begin the following weekend, with only five legislative days remaining before the deadline.[23] Rather than a typical [[continuing-resolution|continuing resolution]] covering the entire federal government, he opted for an untested two-step CR — dubbed a "laddered CR" by Johnson and others, and likened by critics to a step stool — aimed at appeasing far-right members of his conference who oppose CRs.[23] Under the plan, several spending bills would be extended until January 19 while the remaining bills would run on a CR until February 2; the package contained no budget cuts and no aid for [[israel|Israel]], and the House was expected to vote as early as Tuesday after giving members 72 hours to read the text.[23]

Over the same weekend, Johnson made clear that impeachment of [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] was not a priority for the Republican-led House, a stance [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] criticized as irresponsible given the accumulated evidence from the [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] investigations.[23] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that he did not have high hopes for Johnson beyond the view that the Speaker was, wittingly or unwittingly, continuing "a portion of the biggest mass disclosure operation ever implemented on the American people," and that even if Johnson pushed an America First agenda, the apparatus around him appeared broken to the public — which in his framing supplies the mandate to fix it.[23]

## Retaining the gavel in the 119th Congress (January 2025)

Johnson entered the January 3, 2025 vote to seat the Speaker of the 119th Congress facing an uncertain outcome and a razor-thin Republican majority.[24] He drew public backing from Senate Republicans, including Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.), who said Johnson had "done an incredible job in the House," and Sen. [[bill-cassidy|Bill Cassidy]] (R-La.), who wrote, "I agree with President Trump that [Johnson] is the right man to lead. He's a committed conservative and a man of integrity," referencing President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s endorsement.[24] During the December 2024 government-spending fight most Republican senators declined to call for his replacement, though Sens. [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] and [[mike-lee|Mike Lee]] suggested someone else would do a better job, with Paul noting that House rules arguably do not require the Speaker to be a member of the chamber.[24]

The Democratic whip's office signaled that all Democrats would be present, which mattered because Rep. [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] (R-Ky.) had already vowed to vote for someone other than Johnson: with full Democratic attendance behind Rep. [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]], a single additional GOP defection would deny Johnson a simple majority, while one Democratic absence would let him absorb two.[24] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] predicted a first-ballot, party-line win, arguing there was no coordinated opposition comparable to the Matt Gaetz-led effort against Kevin McCarthy in 2023, and that Johnson, as Trump's stated choice, would likely keep the gavel; see [[2025-speaker-of-the-house-election|2025 Speaker of the House election]].[24]

## The December 2024 schism with Trump

In December 2024 Johnson's relationship with President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] fractured publicly during the [[2024-december-continuing-resolution-fight|continuing resolution fight]].[25] Trump had long supported Johnson — hosting him on election night, bringing him to the Army-Navy college football game, and backing him privately despite conservative complaints about the House's actions — which is why it stunned many Republicans when [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]], with Trump's go-ahead, helped tank Johnson's short-term funding deal on a Wednesday afternoon with a barrage of social media posts calling the deal "criminal."[25] Trump then threatened to oppose in a 2026 primary any Republican who voted for it and demanded that the [[debt-ceiling|debt ceiling]] be lifted or eliminated before he took office, pushing the government to the brink of a shutdown.[25]

Republican lawmakers were left wondering why the schism surfaced only at the final moment, with one GOP lawmaker telling CNN, "It's all very strange. This was completely avoidable."[25] By Thursday evening Trump was backing Johnson again as he advanced a replacement plan extending funding three months, lifting the debt ceiling until 2027, extending the farm bill and providing $110 billion in disaster relief; it failed with 38 Republicans opposed, a whiplash that underscored both Johnson's weakness and Musk's opening with Trump.[25] Democrats, who had helped Johnson save his job the previous spring, said they were through helping the Louisiana Republican manage his unruly conference.[25] Sen. [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] and Rep. [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]] responded by floating Musk as a replacement Speaker, noting that the office need not be held by a member of Congress.[25]

## Trans Day of Visibility dispute and Ukraine aid timing (April 2024)

In early April 2024 President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] denied having issued the Trans Day of Visibility proclamation and, told that Johnson said he had, replied that the Speaker was "thoroughly uninformed."[26]

Separately, Johnson suggested the House of Representatives would act in the coming weeks to send more funding to [[ukraine|Ukraine]].[26] "When we return after this period, we'll be moving a product. But it's going to, I think, have some important innovations," he told Fox News' "Sunday Night in America with Trey Gowdy."[26] Both the House and Senate were then in the second week of a two-week recess, with the House due back on Tuesday, April 9.[26] Johnson said he had been "working to build consensus" on a supplemental national security and foreign aid package that would look different from prior attempts, and that the House would "be moving it right after the district work period."[26]

## Medicaid warning on the 2025 megabill

In late June 2025, as President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s July 4 deadline approached and Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] said the Senate was "on a path" to begin voting on the reconciliation megabill that Friday, Johnson warned privately that Senate Republicans could cost House Republicans their majority in 2026 if they pushed through the deep [[medicaid|Medicaid]] cuts in the Senate version, according to three people granted anonymity by [[politico|Politico]].[27] Thune was simultaneously under pressure to water down Medicaid provisions the Senate GOP was counting on for hundreds of billions of dollars in savings.[27] Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] responded that the package was, after all, Johnson's own.[27] Separately, commentary on the Supreme Court's pending Louisiana redistricting case noted that oral argument had touched on drawing districts to protect Johnson's seat.[27]

## Buck resignation and the shrinking majority (March 2024)

Asked in March 2024 whether he had advance notice of Rep. [[ken-buck|Ken Buck]]'s resignation, Johnson replied simply, "I didn't know," and said he was surprised by the announcement.[28] Buck's office said the Colorado congressman had called Johnson to inform him but reached no answer and left a voicemail.[28] The resignation left the House at 218 Republicans to 213 Democrats, meaning Johnson could afford to lose only two members on any party-line vote and making legislation harder to pass without Democratic help.[28] Badlands analyst [[just-human|Just Human]] framed the departure as possible [[uniparty]] maneuvering to return the House to Democratic control before the 2024 election.[28]

## Ukraine funding shift (March 2024)

Johnson told Republican senators in March 2024 that the House would send over new Ukraine funding legislation, though it would "look substantially different" from the $95 billion foreign aid package the Senate had passed the previous month.[28] He floated structuring the assistance as a loan or lend-lease program — an idea [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had raised in February — and suggested including a mechanism resembling the [[repo-for-ukrainians-act|REPO for Ukrainians Act]] sponsored by Rep. [[michael-mccaul|Michael McCaul]], which would confiscate and liquidate Russian sovereign assets for a Ukraine support fund.[28] Absent from the discussion was any mention of U.S. border reforms such as "Remain in Mexico" provisions.[28] Speaking separately to the New York Post, Johnson said Ukraine funding would take a back seat to funding the federal government for the rest of the fiscal year, prompting criticism that he was telling different audiences different things.[28]

## Refusal to negotiate during the October 2025 shutdown

On Thursday, October 2, 2025, House Speaker Johnson made clear that House Republicans would not budge amid the standoff over government funding, as Democrats continued to insist on healthcare concessions tied to Obamacare.[29] "Don't ask the Republicans what we should be doing or what we should be negotiating. I don't have anything to negotiate. I sent them, in good faith, exactly what they voted for before," Johnson told reporters at a press conference.[29] He added, "We did not put any Republican provisions in that, and we tried to make this very simple, in good faith, so the appropriations process of the people can continue."[29]

Badlands contributor Chris Paul dismissed the standoff as "Uniparty jousting," writing that Republicans had "accomplished nothing since 2020," had not even attempted to fix elections, and were "not better than the Democrats" but rather "the Uniparty Right and nothing more."[29]

## Border Security Added to the $95.3 Billion Foreign Aid Package (April 2024)

On Wednesday, April 17, 2024, Johnson attached a border security measure to the stalled foreign aid package in an attempt to appease conservatives, telling lawmakers the House would vote on the package Saturday night and delaying a week-long recess set to begin that Friday.[30] The $95.3 billion package comprised three separate aid bills covering Ukraine, Israel and Taiwan, with the Ukraine portion structured as a loan that carried provisions allowing cancellation.[30] Facing a growing removal threat from his right flank, Johnson brushed off the specter of a motion to vacate, arguing Ukraine was at a tipping point and that an "axis of evil" of Russia, China and Iran was coordinating to push the conflict deep into Europe and to the front yard of NATO allies.[30] "To put it bluntly, I would rather send bullets to Ukraine than American boys," the Louisiana Republican said.[30]

Badlands commentator Burning Bright read the episode through his "War of Stories"/5GW frame, calling Johnson's joint appearance with Donald Trump and his self-description as a "wartime Speaker" seismic developments, and suggesting that both Johnson and his America First opponents in Congress — Marjorie Taylor Greene, Matt Gaetz and Lauren Boebert — were advancing the MAGA agenda while absorbing psychological costs, with Johnson having damaged the Uniparty "wittingly or unwittingly."[30]

## Formalizing the Biden impeachment inquiry

As Speaker of the House, Mike Johnson presided over the December 13, 2023 floor vote formalizing the impeachment inquiry into President Joe Biden, while publicly resisting the suggestion that impeachment itself was inevitable.[31] "We're not going to prejudge the outcome of this because we can't," he told reporters the day before the vote, adding, "It's not a political calculation. We're following the law and we are the rule of law team and I'm going to hold to that."[31]

## February 2024: threat warning and the foreign aid supplemental

Responding to House Intelligence Committee chairman [[mike-turner|Mike Turner]]'s February 14, 2024 warning of a "serious national security threat," Johnson said in a statement that there was "no need for public alarm," adding that he would press the Biden administration to take "appropriate action."[32] "Steady hands are at the wheel, we're working on it, there's no need for alarm," he cautioned.[32]

The same week, after the Senate passed a $95 billion foreign aid package, Johnson refused to hold a House vote on it until Congress first secured the southern border, saying: "We really do owe it to the American people to get control of that border."[32] House Democrats responded by preparing a [[discharge-petition|discharge petition]] to force a vote over his objection.[32]

## Support for Texas defiance of the border ruling (January 2024)

In January 2024, Johnson threw his support behind Texas Governor [[greg-abbott|Greg Abbott]] as the governor continued to defy the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration following a [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruling that granted the federal government authority to remove swaths of razor wire installed at the southern border by the state.[33] Rolling Stone grouped him among the high-profile Republicans pushing Texas to defy the 5-4 decision.[33] Democratic Representatives [[joaquin-castro|Joaquin Castro]] and [[greg-casar|Greg Casar]] took the opposite position, calling on Biden to federalize the [[texas-national-guard|Texas National Guard]].[33]

## February 2024 Oval Office meeting with Biden

In a February 2024 one-on-one conversation in the Oval Office, Johnson raised an administration policy change that jeopardized some large energy projects, and President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] told him it was "just a study," according to six people told at the time about Johnson's account.[34] Johnson worried afterward that the president's memory of the details of his own policy had slipped, an episode later reported by The Wall Street Journal as part of a wider account of Biden's private decline.[34]

## January 2024 topline spending deal

On Sunday, January 7, 2024, Johnson and Senate Majority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] announced a fiscal year 2024 spending framework as the first step toward averting a government shutdown, setting a topline of $1.59 trillion with $886 billion for defense and roughly $704 billion in non-defense spending, plus a $69 billion side deal in adjustments that pushed non-defense spending to nearly $773 billion and the overall figure to about $1.66 trillion.[35] The agreement reduced the chances of a shutdown but left funding bills or a stopgap resolution still to be passed ahead of the January 19 and February 2 deadlines, with conservative demands to attach stringent immigration and border security measures unresolved.[35]

The [[house-freedom-caucus|House Freedom Caucus]] denounced the deal as a "total failure," a significant problem for Johnson given his extremely narrow majority.[35] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the bipartisan alignment of Johnson, Schumer and the [[white-house|White House]] showed the uniparty uniting against the America First majority, and connected the outcome to Johnson's unanimous election as Speaker by his caucus, which she said was becoming easier to understand in retrospect.[35]

## Defense of the Santos commutation (October 2025)

On Sunday, October 19, 2025, Johnson defended President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s decision to grant clemency to former New York Rep. [[george-santos|George Santos]], who was serving a seven-year sentence for wire fraud and identity theft.[36] "The president has the right in the Constitution for pardon and commutation, of course," the Louisiana Republican told Jonathan Karl on ABC, adding, "We believe in redemption. This is a personal belief of mine. And I hope Mr. Santos makes the most of his second chance."[36] Johnson said Trump had not acted because of Santos's political affiliation and argued that the president was being "transparent."[36] Santos had served less than three months of the sentence when Trump announced the commutation in a social media post.[36]

## First floor action as Speaker

The first bill brought to the House floor after Johnson's election as Speaker was [[house-resolution-771-standing-with-israel|House Resolution 771]], a resolution expressing strong support for [[israel]] following the October 7 [[hamas]] attack, which passed on October 25, 2023 by a vote of 412–10.[37] The measure reaffirmed U.S. military aid to Israel, pledged that [[congress]] would pass more, called for continued enforcement of sanctions on [[iran]], and stated that the House "stands ready to assist Israel with emergency resupply and other security, diplomatic, and intelligence support."[37]

## Greene's "not our Speaker" charge (December 2025)

In a New York Times profile published December 29, 2025, Rep. [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]] said Johnson was "100 percent" under direct orders from the [[white-house|White House]] and was surrendering the institutional power of [[congress|Congress]] as a result.[38] "I want you to know that Johnson is not our Speaker. He is not our leader," Greene told the paper, adding that many Republicans were furious about the arrangement "but they're cowards."[38] The criticism arrived amid wider House complaints about the legislative branch's deference to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], and shortly before Greene's resignation from Congress took effect on January 5, 2026.[38]

## Withdrawal of the December 2024 stopgap bill

On December 18, 2024, Johnson withdrew the stopgap spending bill intended to fund the government into spring after mounting opposition from fellow Republicans, including President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and Vice President-elect [[jd-vance|JD Vance]], who objected to billions in added spending.[39] House Majority Leader [[steve-scalise|Steve Scalise]] confirmed the measure's demise, saying "There's no new agreement right now," as lawmakers faced a Friday shutdown deadline in the [[2024-december-continuing-resolution-fight|December 2024 continuing resolution fight]].[39] Critics of the roughly 1,500-page package objected to provisions said to shield [[congress|Congress]] from sunshine laws and subpoenas, continued funding for the [[global-engagement-center|Global Engagement Center]], and a 40% congressional pay raise; the bill reportedly died Wednesday night.[39]

## Response to the 2025 off-year elections

Following the Republican losses in the [[2025-off-year-elections|2025 off-year elections]], Johnson used a news conference to cast [[zohran-mamdani|Zohran Mamdani]], the mayor-elect of New York City and a self-described democratic socialist, as the new leader of the [[democratic-party|Democratic Party]].[40] His framing was part of a broader Republican search for culprits that blamed weak candidates, the [[october-2025-government-shutdown|government shutdown]], a focus on transgender issues, and a weak economic message — while conspicuously avoiding any criticism of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[40]

## Refusal to recall the House for disaster aid (October 2024)

As [[hurricane-milton|Hurricane Milton]] approached Florida in October 2024 and federal disaster programs — including [[fema|FEMA]]'s disaster fund and flood insurance program and [[small-business-administration|Small Business Administration]] loans — were reported to be weeks from exhaustion, lawmakers of both parties called for Congress to return to Washington before the November election to approve additional funding.[41] Fiscal conservatives in the House balked at the idea, and Johnson said that weekend that he had no plans to bring his members back.[41]

## House member security pilot program (2025)

In July 2025, as Speaker of the [[us-house-of-representatives|House of Representatives]], Johnson announced an increase in security measures for House members structured as a two-month pilot program, prompted by rising political violence and threats as lawmakers prepared to leave Washington for the August recess.[42] Among the changes was a doubling of the funds members may use to install security equipment at their personal residences, from $10,000 to $20,000.[42] Johnson said leaders would in a few months "evaluate all the data points [of the pilot program], see how effective it was, how it was utilized, and then make decisions going forward."[42] See [[2025-house-member-security-pilot-program|2025 House member security pilot program]].[42]

## FY2026 NDAA floor strategy (December 2025)

In December 2025, Johnson and House GOP leaders planned to pass the annual defense policy bill late on Wednesday afternoon, having unveiled the 3,086-page text on Sunday evening and immediately drawing public pushback from hard-line conservatives over Ukraine funding and the omission of a [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] digital currency ban.[43] While the Speaker could likely count on some Democratic support on final passage, he first needed to hold his own party together on a procedural vote earlier that afternoon, and leadership promoted conservative wins in the final package — including provisions taking aim at diversity and equity programs in the ranks — to build unity.[43] Badlands criticism of the resulting bill centered on the inclusion of the [[road-to-housing-act|ROAD to Housing Act]] inside the [[national-defense-authorization-act|NDAA]].[43]

## Republican retention of the House (November 2024)

On Monday, November 11, 2024, Republicans were projected to keep control of the [[us-house-of-representatives|House of Representatives]], handing the party total control of Washington with President-elect [[donald-trump|Trump]] returning to the White House in January.[44] [[decision-desk-hq|Decision Desk HQ]] projected the GOP would hold the chamber upon winning its 218th seat.[44] The Hill described the result as a major win for Johnson, who had skyrocketed from obscurity to lead the House GOP both legislatively and in a large role in its campaign infrastructure.[44]

Republicans saved vulnerable incumbents such as Representatives Don Bacon of Nebraska and David Valadao of California while defeating several vulnerable Democrats: state Representative Ryan Mackenzie unseated Representative Susan Wild of Pennsylvania, and businessman Rob Bresnahan defeated Representative Matt Cartwright of Pennsylvania.[44] Those gains offset GOP losses, including three first-term New York Republicans — Representatives Anthony D'Esposito, Marc Molinaro and Brandon Williams — and Representative Lori Chavez-DeRemer of Oregon, all of whom lost their reelection bids.[44]
