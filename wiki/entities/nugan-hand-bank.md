# Nugan Hand Bank

The Nugan Hand Bank was a Sydney-based merchant bank founded in 1973 by the American [[michael-jon-hand]] and the Australian lawyer [[frank-nugan]], and widely alleged to have been a [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] vehicle for arms smuggling, drug-money laundering and the financing of covert operations before its 1980 collapse.[1] Joe Lange describes it flatly as "a CIA money laundering operation from its inception," noting that nobody was prosecuted for any of it.[1]

## Founding and fraud

Hand had been a Green Beret in Vietnam and a CIA operative in Laos before moving to Australia, where he and Nugan — described by ProPublica as a wealthy playboy — established the bank with $80.[1] As Alfred W. McCoy recounts in "The Politics of Heroin: CIA Complicity in the Global Drug Trade," Nugan wrote the bank a check for $980,000 and then covered it by writing a bank check to himself for the same amount; through this elementary accounting fraud he could claim the company's paid-up capital was a million dollars.[1] Lange remarks on how easily a bank could be created from such beginnings.[1]

From those origins the bank grew into a global operation, attracting investors with promises of 16 percent interest on offshore deposits. By 1979 Nugan Hand had 13 branches around the world, and according to Australian investigators many of its depositors were drug traffickers.[1]

## Branch network

Branches were placed in strategic locations. The bank was registered in the Cayman Islands to avoid taxes, and opened an office in Chiang Mai, Thailand — the epicenter of the heroin trade in the [[golden-triangle]] — located in the same building as the local DEA office, yet never placed under suspicion of laundering drug money.[1] A key branch was opened in Hong Kong, tied directly to the bank's Saudi Arabian operations run by [[bernie-houghton]].[1]

## Saudi Arabia and the Hong Kong scheme

The New York Times reported in November 1982 that Australian detectives were preparing to travel to the United States and Southeast Asia to determine whether the failed bank was involved in heroin trafficking and CIA covert activities, and that the Australian government had uncovered evidence of a link between the CIA and Nugan Hand in a heavily censored police report.[1] The report noted that Hand, born in New York City, and Houghton, a 62-year-old Texan who was the bank's representative in Saudi Arabia, both had backgrounds in American intelligence before arriving in Sydney in 1967.[1] Lange notes that 1967 was also the year [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]] and his Zapata Offshore partner traveled to Vietnam.[1]

Almost 40 pages of the 240-page report were devoted to Houghton; it stated that when he ran the Saudi Arabian office the bank transferred millions of dollars out of Saudi Arabia, money that has yet to be traced.[1] The censored portion is believed to discuss links between Nugan Hand and organized crime, establishing that the bank was involved in heroin smuggling.[1] WikiSpooks records the bank's "most brazen fraud" as possibly the theft of at least $5 million, and maybe more than $10 million, from American civilian and military personnel in Saudi Arabia.[1]

According to Joel Bainerman's 1992 book "The Crimes Of A President," the bank sent "investment counselors" to installations where Americans worked in Saudi Arabia and told them to invest their salaries in Nugan Hand's Hong Kong branch in secured government bonds; the depositors lost everything and none of the money has been traced or recovered.[1]

## Collapse and investigations

Nugan was found dead in his Mercedes-Benz in 1980, his left hand holding the barrel of a .30-calibre rifle a few inches from his head and his right hand near the trigger; an inquest ruled the death a suicide, a finding many Australians found dubious.[1] Documents bearing the name of former CIA director [[william-colby]] — the bank's legal adviser — were found on Nugan's body.[1] During the inquest Hand testified that the bank was insolvent, owing investors some $50 million, then fled Australia in disguise on a phony passport.[1]

The Australian government's investigations found that millions of dollars were missing and unaccounted for, that the bank's main depositors were connected with the narcotics trade in the Middle East and Asia, that the CIA was using Nugan Hand to finance a variety of covert operations, and that the bank was linked to at least 26 separate individuals or groups known to be associated with drug trafficking.[1] In 1983 the Australian Joint Task Force on Drug Trafficking reported to Parliament that [[ted-shackley]], [[richard-secord]], [[thomas-clines]], [[rafael-quintero]] and [[edwin-wilson]] had backgrounds "relevant to a proper understanding" of the group's activities — names that never appeared in media coverage of the collapse.[1]

Lange links the bank back to [[paul-helliwell]], the CIA lawyer-banker who helped set it up and whose earlier [[castle-bank-and-trust]] in the Bahamas served as its model.[1]
