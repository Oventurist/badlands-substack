# Charles A. Lindbergh Sr.

Charles A. Lindbergh Sr. was a Minnesota legislator and the father of the aviator Charles Lindbergh. He is remembered in Badlands Media reporting as one of the most prominent opponents of the creation of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] in 1913.[1]

## Opposition to the Federal Reserve Act

Badlands describes Lindbergh as a Minnesotan senator who opposed the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act]] when it passed in 1913, and attributes to him the claim that the legislation created "the most gigantic trust on Earth."[1] The article treats that phrase as a technical rather than rhetorical statement, arguing that Lindbergh was pointing to the fact that the full faith and credit of the American people had been entrusted to a private corporation acting as trustee.[1]

## Interpretation in trust terms

Using the framework of [[trust-law|trust law]], the Badlands essay reads Lindbergh's warning as identifying an arrangement in which the American people are simultaneously trustor and nominal beneficiary while the Federal Reserve serves as trustee over the nation's credit.[1] The author asserts that this was the largest trust ever proposed in history at the time, surpassed only in later years by the [[international-monetary-fund|International Monetary Fund]].[1]

## Opposition to the Federal Reserve

Charles A. Lindbergh Sr., a Republican member of Congress, was among the most outspoken opponents of the [[aldrich-plan|Aldrich plan]] and its successor.[2] During debate he charged that the real purpose of the monetary commission was to frame currency and banking amendments "which would suit the [[money-trust|Money Trust]]," that the interests were busy everywhere educating the people in favor of the Aldrich plan with a large sum raised for the purpose, and that "Wall Street speculation brought on the [[panic-of-1907|Panic of 1907]]" because depositors' funds had been loaned to gamblers and to whomever the Money Trust wished to favor.[2] He further stated that "the Aldrich plan is the Wall Street Plan. It means another panic, if necessary, to intimidate the people. Aldrich, paid by the government to represent the people, proposes a plan for the trusts instead."[2]

On the passage of the Glass-Owen Bill, Lindbergh declared: "This Act establishes the most gigantic trust on earth. When the President signs this bill, the invisible government of the monetary power will be legalized. The people may not know it immediately, but the day of reckoning is only a few years removed ... The worst legislative crime of the ages is perpetrated by this banking and currency bill."[2]

## Analysis of the Business Cycle

One year after passage of the Federal Reserve Bill, Lindbergh outlined how the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] created the "business cycle" and manipulated it for advantage: lowering the rediscount rate would expand credit and lift the stock market, and once businessmen adjusted, prosperity could be checked in mid-career by arbitrarily raising interest rates.[2] He argued the Board could make the market swing gently or violently at will while possessing inside information and advance knowledge of coming changes — "the strongest, most dangerous advantage ever placed in the hands of a special privilege class by any Government that ever existed."[2] The system, he concluded, was private and conducted solely to extract the greatest possible profits from other people's money, with its operators knowing in advance when to create and when to stop panics.[2]
