# Iain McGilchrist

**Iain McGilchrist** is a neuroscientist and author whose books examine the distinct ways in which the two hemispheres of the human brain perceive and interact with the world.[1] He is cited at length in the Badlands Media essay "Expand Your Thinking" as the principal authority for the claim that [[hemispheric-specialization|hemispheric specialization]] is a demonstrable fact of neurology rather than a metaphor.[1]

## Works cited

The essay notes that McGilchrist's most recent work at the time of writing was a two-volume set, *The Matter with Things*, each volume running to roughly 1,500 pages, and cautions that any short summary of his argument is necessarily an oversimplification.[1] It also quotes his earlier book *The Divided Brain and the Search for Meaning*, in which he writes that "The left hemisphere's take on things comes from assessing thousands of points of information in turn and trying to reach a conclusion about the whole picture that way … [and] the right hemisphere … sees things as a whole, never as isolated particles independent of a context."[1]

## Argument as presented

On the reading advanced in the article, McGilchrist holds that the left hemisphere reduces reality into static, manipulable parts and does not experience reality directly, instead rendering experience into a map, while the right hemisphere apprehends wholes in context.[1] The article attributes to him the further finding that the left hemisphere, deprived of input from the right, becomes "demonstrably self-deceiving, and confabulates—it makes up a story, when it cannot understand something, and tells it with conviction."[1]

A longer quotation given in the essay states that, unlike the right hemisphere, which tends toward self-doubt, the left hemisphere "takes a distinctly flattering view of its own capabilities … It is *not* reasonable. It is angry when challenged, dismisses evidence it doesn't like or can't understand, and is unreasonably sure of its own rightness … Its attention is narrow, its vision myopic, and it can't see how the parts fit together. It's good for only one thing: manipulating the world. Its world is a representation, a virtual world, only … it prioritizes the procedure, without grasp of its meaning or purpose. And it requires certainty where none can be found."[1]

## Reception in the article

The author, writing under the pen name [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]], uses McGilchrist's framework to argue that mainstream science and academia display the hallmarks of left-brain imbalance, being held hostage by their own maps and presuppositions about the nature of reality.[1] The essay observes that many neuroscientists have long treated the hemispheres as functionally redundant because both are involved in language, information acquisition, emotion and spatial orientation, and calls that assumption itself "a very left-brained way of looking at things."[1] It counters that the hemispheres' distinct characters reveal themselves in cases of one-sided brain injury, that the pattern is consistent across patients, and that hemispheric specialization is observable in animals as well as humans.[1]
