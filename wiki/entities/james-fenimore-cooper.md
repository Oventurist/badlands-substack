# James Fenimore Cooper

James Fenimore Cooper was an American novelist and, in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]], one of the leading members of the American artistic movement and intelligentsia of the early nineteenth century as well as a political organiser within the republican networks around the [[marquis-de-lafayette|Marquis de Lafayette]].[1]

## Literary work

Cooper innovated an entire new literary school of the written word, encompassing not only *The Last of the Mohicans* and the *Leatherstocking Tales* but also fiction that infused insights into psychological warfare techniques and occult intelligence operations threatening the United States.[1] This is described as especially clear in *The Bravo*, which explores the nature of Venice and the structure of its political systems, contrasting them with the republican cultures and political systems of the West.[1]

## Patron and organiser

Cooper was a sponsor of [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]] the painter and one of the catalysts who brought him to France at a strategic moment in world history, and he acquired for Morse the contract to paint Lafayette's portrait, into which several ideas were embedded as visual ironies.[1] He was a member of the [[society-of-the-cincinnati|Society of the Cincinnatus]], as was [[edgar-allan-poe|Edgar Allan Poe]], and by 1829 was travelling with Lafayette alongside other American literary figures coordinating a republican movement, including Washington Irving, then strategically positioned as US ambassador to Spain.[1] Cooper, Morse and Lafayette lived several doors from one another in Paris, spending evenings working, planning and playing music together, Cooper at the piano.[1] Cooper was among the crowd shouting for Lafayette to declare himself president of a new French republic on the day the Marquis instead endorsed the Duke of Orleans as king.[1]

## In the Gallery of the Louvre

Cooper appears in Morse's *Gallery of the Louvre* with his wife and their daughter Susan Cooper, herself a renowned painter, who had died two years before the work was completed — one of several figures in the tableau who could not have been present simultaneously, echoing the treatment of time in Raphael's *School of Athens*.[1]
