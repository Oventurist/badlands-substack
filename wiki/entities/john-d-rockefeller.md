# John D. Rockefeller

**John D. Rockefeller** is quoted in Part 4 of the [[structures-of-control-part-1-series|Structures of Control]] series as expressing the intent behind the reshaping of American education.[1]

## The quotation and its use

The article attributes to Rockefeller the line "I don't want a nation of thinkers. I want a nation of workers," presenting it as the animating purpose of the early-twentieth-century funding the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]] poured into American education.[1] That funding is said to have shifted curricula away from classical models of philosophy, logic, and literature toward utilitarian obedience training.[1]

## Wider claim

The essay identifies the educational system as "ground zero" for what it calls fourth-dimensional control — control exercised through time rather than space, aimed at writing history, shaping memory, and installing long-term belief systems.[1] It argues that [[unesco|UNESCO]] and the global education reform movement later scaled the framework worldwide under banners of human rights and development, training generations to comply institutionally rather than think critically, and that the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] and [[world-bank|World Bank]] continue the lineage through "future skills" agendas rooted in AI symbiosis and behavioral economics rather than sovereignty.[1] An earlier section makes the parallel claim that the Rockefeller Foundation's reformation of American schooling deliberately removed classical education, critical thinking, and spiritual philosophy in favor of standardized industrial training.[1]

## Pivot from oil to medicine

At the beginning of the twentieth century John D. Rockefeller had become the richest man in America through his monopolization of the oil industry, and Badlands Media commentary argues that when the Model T Ford — first produced in Detroit in 1908 — made automobiles and thus gasoline demand accessible to the masses, he transitioned into the medical industry.[2]

Rockefeller is credited with moving medicine from mostly homeopathic and natural treatment to an allopathic, petrochemical model, so that remedies drawn for centuries from natural sources would instead come from chemicals.[2] He bought shares in the German pharmaceutical company [[ig-farben|I.G. Farben]], later one of Nazi Germany's largest firms, and mounted a smear campaign portraying natural healing modalities as quackery.[2] With [[andrew-carnegie|Andrew Carnegie]] he founded medical schools teaching only allopathic medicine, and the [[american-medical-association|American Medical Association]] was formed to recognize only chemical-based practitioners.[2] The [[rockefeller-institute-hospital|Rockefeller Institute Hospital]] opened in 1910 as the first center for clinical research and, in the article's phrase, the birthplace of virology and of [[big-pharma|Big Pharma]].[2] After the Spanish Flu subsided, Rockefeller-run pharmaceutical companies rolled out vaccines and claimed credit for ending the outbreak.[2]

## Capture of medical education and the eugenics movement

In "The Inversion of Health," Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argues that when the [[american-medical-association|American Medical Association]] ran out of money in 1910 while ranking the nation's medical schools, the Rockefeller and Carnegie foundations created the education fund that completed the project, producing the [[flexner-report|Flexner Report]].[3] [[simon-flexner|Simon Flexner]] of the [[rockefeller-institute|Rockefeller Institute]] proposed his brother [[abraham-flexner|Abraham Flexner]] for the job, and schools that failed to conform to the resulting standard were denied funds and prestige and forced out of business.[3] DeLarme contends that Rockefeller's motives were twofold: a pharmaceutical industry developed largely from petroleum, and the advancement of [[eugenics]].[3]

After creating the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]] in 1913, Rockefeller took charge of the [[bureau-of-social-hygiene|Bureau of Social Hygiene]], and his wife Cettie gave it $25,000 the same year to promote social hygiene instruction for female students nationwide.[3] Through the foundation's medical division, the National Research Council, he funded the sex research of [[alfred-kinsey|Alfred Kinsey]], and the Bureau in turn funded [[margaret-sanger|Margaret Sanger]]'s [[american-birth-control-league|American Birth Control League]].[3] DeLarme rejects the conventional portrait of Rockefeller as the architect of modern philanthropy and champion of humanity that men like [[bill-gates|Bill Gates]] and [[george-soros|George Soros]] later emulated, arguing instead that his family's record points the opposite way.[3]

## The rise of Standard Oil

Badlands Media's "The Men Behind the Curtain – Part 5" describes John Davison Rockefeller as the original American robber baron and the most recognized — and perhaps most despised — billionaire ever to have lived, despite having died in 1937.[4] At the start of the American Civil War he was a young agricultural commodities broker in Cleveland, Ohio; recognizing the potential of the fledgling petroleum industry, he and several associates built a refinery in 1863 and he incorporated the [[standard-oil|Standard Oil Company of Ohio]] in 1870.[4] Author [[eustace-mullins|Eustace Mullins]] and the investigative video *The Money Masters* both allege that his takeover of competitors was financed by the National City Bank of Cleveland, identified in congressional hearings as one of three [[rothschild-family|Rothschild]] banks in the United States, and that rebates arranged with J.P. Morgan and Kuhn, Loeb — which controlled 95% of U.S. railway mileage — through his holding company South Improvement Co. allowed him to undersell and ruin rivals.[4]

Rockefeller is quoted as declaring that "competition is a sin," and he eliminated competitors by merger, outright purchase, or price-cutting until they were forced to sell.[4] By 1880 he owned or controlled 95 percent of all oil produced in the United States.[4] A widely reprinted rural Texas newspaper item of the period calculated that his fortune grew by $17,705 each night he slept and $50,000 between each evening's turn at the violin.[4] The article recounts an anecdote told by [[nelson-rockefeller|Nelson Rockefeller]] about the origin of his grandfather's philosophy: his father, William "Big Bill" Rockefeller, who sold cancer "cures" from a medicine wagon, once let the boy fall while jumping into his arms and told him, "Remember, never trust anyone completely—not even me."[4]

## Trusts, prosecution and dissolution

Journalist [[ida-tarbell|Ida Minerva Tarbell]], daughter of a Pennsylvania oil producer ruined by Rockefeller, published her *History of Standard Oil Company* series in 1902 after five years of research, triggering court and government action.[4] Rockefeller had, however, already created the Standard Oil Trust in 1882, a structure the *New Encyclopaedia Britannica* described as "a maze of legal structures, making its workings virtually impervious to public investigation and understanding."[4] When the Ohio Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved in 1892 he simply moved headquarters to New York City, transferring all assets in 1899 to the Standard Oil Company of New Jersey.[4] The federal government charged Standard with violating the [[sherman-antitrust-act|Sherman Anti-Trust Act]] in 1906, and the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against it on May 15, 1911.[4] The breakup only increased Rockefeller's wealth, leaving him with a quarter share of thirty-three successor companies, and he became the world's first confirmed U.S. dollar billionaire in 1916.[4]

## Later life and institutions

By his death in 1937, Rockefeller and his son [[john-d-rockefeller-jr|John D. Rockefeller Jr.]] had built not only an oil empire but institutions including the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research (1901), the General Education Board (1903), the University of Chicago (1889), the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]] (1913), the Lincoln School (1917), and [[rockefeller-university|Rockefeller University]].[4] Beginning in 1917 and over the following five years he handed his fortune to his only son "with no strings attached," according to biographer Alvin Moscow.[4] His grandson [[david-rockefeller|David Rockefeller Sr.]] died in 2017 at age 101 worth $3.3 billion by Forbes' reckoning, though Badlands Media argues true power is measured in influence and connections rather than declared dollars.[4]
