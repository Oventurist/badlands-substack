# School of the Americas

The School of the Americas (SOA) was the US military training institution whose Latin American graduates included [[manuel-noriega]], the Panamanian general who became an installed CIA dictator and a paid asset of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]].[1]

In Joe Lange's account the school functions as one node of a wider apparatus that produced compliant strongmen across Central and South America: Noriega, an SOA graduate, went on to run Panama's army and police, to facilitate cocaine trafficking from Colombia into the United States on behalf of the [[medellin-cartel]], and to provide [[ramon-milian-rodriguez]] with the use of Panama's airports, banking system and security officers for laundering drug proceeds.[1] The school is treated in the series as part of the institutional infrastructure of what Lange calls the [[shadow-government]].[1]

## Founding, curriculum and Project X

The institute was founded in 1946, and by 2000 more than 60,000 Latin American military, law enforcement, and security personnel had attended the school.[2] It was located in the Panama Canal Zone until its expulsion in 1984, and is strongly associated with the dirty wars carried out by U.S.-supported military juntas in South America, particularly in the 1970s and 1980s; many of its alumni served in repressive, undemocratic governments and engaged in human rights abuses such as torture and enforced disappearances.[2]

The counterinsurgency manuals used for instruction were produced during the Army's Project X, established under the Foreign Intelligence Assistance Program in 1965–66, which relied on knowledge produced during the CIA's [[phoenix-program|Phoenix Program]] in Vietnam.[2] Major Joseph Blair, a former instructor at the school, said the author of the SOA and CIA torture manuals "drew from intelligence materials used during the Vietnam War that advocated assassination, torture, extortion, and other 'techniques'."[2] The scholar J. Patrice McSherry argued that the manuals' authors believed oversight regulations and prohibitions applied only to U.S. personnel, not to foreign officers.[2] Use of the manuals was suspended under President [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]] over concerns about their correlation to human rights abuses, then re-introduced into the curriculum under the [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]] administration in 1982.[2]

## Alumni across the cocaine corridor

Joe Lange's follow-up article argues that every country linking Colombia to the United States — Panama, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala and Mexico — had rulers, military officers or police trained at the SOA, and that the CIA controlled them while doing nothing to stop, and in fact promoting, the flow of cocaine northward.[2] Named graduates cited include [[manuel-noriega|Manuel Noriega]] of Panama, Honduran general Humberto Ragalado Hernández and at least 19 members of [[battalion-3-16|Battalion 3-16]], Salvadoran death squad leader [[roberto-daubuisson|Roberto D'Aubuisson]], and Guatemalan dictator [[efrain-rios-montt|Efraín Ríos Montt]] alongside intelligence chief Manuel Antonio Callejas y Callejas.[2] In El Salvador, 19 of the 27 officers who took part in the 1989 execution of six Jesuit priests at Central America University were SOA-trained, as were three-quarters of the officers implicated in the assassination of Archbishop Óscar Romero.[2]
