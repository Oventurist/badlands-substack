# William Dunlap

William Dunlap (1766–1839) was an American painter, playwright, actor and historian of the arts in the early United States, and a central figure in the republican cultural movement that opposed the Anglophile [[american-academy-of-fine-arts|American Academy of Fine Arts]].[1] The Badlands Media article by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] renders his surname "Dunlop."[1]

## Career

In 1783, at the age of seventeen, Dunlap painted a portrait of [[george-washington|George Washington]], who then sponsored the young man's journey to England, where he studied under the American painter [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] before returning to the United States to launch a new movement in theatre and design.[1] Of his historical writings he is best known for the three-volume *The History of the Rise and Progress of the Arts of Design in the United States*, published in 1834.[1]

## National Academy of Design

Dunlap was a co-founder and vice president of the [[national-academy-of-design|National Academy of the Arts of Design]], established in New York by [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]].[1] Together with Morse he opened a direct war on [[john-trumbull|John Trumbull]]'s academy, describing it as a "joint stock company," composed of persons of every trade and profession who thought the privilege of visiting the exhibition an equivalent for their subscription, in which "artists could only share those privileges by purchasing stocks, and might be controlled in everything respecting their profession by those who were ignorant of the arts."[1] Dunlap's history is the source used for the surviving texts of the Trumbull–Morse public debates.[1]
