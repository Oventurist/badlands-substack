# British Crown

The British Crown is the sovereign institution of the United Kingdom, and in Badlands Media's legal history of American money it is cast as the principal foreign power whose influence over American law and finance was never fully severed by the Revolutionary War.[1]

## Treatment of the American colonies

A February 16, 2023 Badlands Media essay argues that during the eighteenth century the American colonies were treated, more or less, like slave states by the Crown, with colonists legally and economically disempowered by numerous statutes of Westminster — British law — that gave egregiously unfair advantages to Crown agents as well as to wealthy Dutch merchants in every manner of trade and commerce in America.[1] These parties, the essay contends, saw America as their own private cash crop and sought a monopoly in every conceivable sphere of economic activity, with the [[dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] responsible for the lion's share of the slave trade.[1]

The article holds that the Crown and the Dutch merchants had fomented economic and "legal" suppression against the early colonists to such a degree that many colonists rightly saw it as necessary to declare independence forever or succumb to an impenetrable tyranny, quoting the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]] on the right and duty of a people to throw off a government pursuing a design of absolute despotism.[1]

## Motive for continued interference

From the point of view of the Crown and the Dutch merchants, the essay argues, losing control of the colonies meant the loss of some of their most precious investments and real estate — the equivalent of investing trillions of dollars of capital in today's money into a new series of colonies only for those colonies to declare independence, precluding any return.[1] In the eyes of the Crown and the Dutch merchants, the article states, the colonies existed only to enrich them, noble appeals to equality and freedom on the part of the citizenry be damned.[1]

## Legal footholds after independence

The essay's central claim is that separation from England was never as complete as Americans have been told, because Crown legal institutions retained a foothold in the new republic.[1] Many jurists and attorneys working in early America were trained by the [[middle-temple|Middle Temple of London]], a prestigious "inn" of the Crown, and the [[inns-of-court|inns of court]] were responsible for creating much of what is recognized today as [[equity-jurisprudence|equity jurisprudence]], including the formation of chancery courts.[1] The [[delaware-court-of-chancery|Delaware Court of Chancery]] is offered as a surviving example of eighteenth-century British equity law operating on American soil.[1] [[thomas-jefferson|Thomas Jefferson]] is quoted warning as early as 1797 that a party had arisen seeking to unite American destinies with those of Great Britain and to assimilate the American government to Britain's.[1]
