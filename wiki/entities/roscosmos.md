# Roscosmos

Roscosmos is the Russian state space corporation and the successor institution to the Soviet space program. In Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s July 2024 essay "Time to Revive the Spirit of JFK," Roscosmos appears as a would-be partner of the United States in a revived program of great-power cooperation in space, and later as an agency driven into the arms of China by Washington's abandonment of that cooperation.[1]

## Cooperation with NASA

On September 27, 2017, Roscosmos and [[nasa|NASA]] signed a joint statement on researching and exploring deep space, declaring that both agencies "see the gateway as a strategic component of human space exploration architecture that warrants additional study," and noting that NASA had already engaged industry partners in gateway concept studies while Roscosmos and other space station partner agencies prepared to do the same.[1] Russia subsequently participated in the [[lunar-gateway-project|Lunar Gateway Project]], the planned first lunar-orbiting space station to be co-created by Japan, Europe, the United States, and Russia.[1]

Roscosmos also expressed willingness to negotiate directly with NASA over lunar exploration. One day after the May 15, 2020 announcement of the [[artemis-accords|Artemis Accords]], the outlet *Russia Business Today* published an article reporting that Roscosmos was ready for negotiations with NASA on Moon exploration projects; Sergei Savalyev, deputy director general for Roscosmos on international cooperation, stated that "ambitious projects connected to Moon colonization could become a serious factor for the interaction of the two countries in difficult times."[1] Roscosmos was then headed by [[dmitry-rogozin|Dmitry Rogozin]], who was photographed with NASA administrator [[jim-bridenstine|Jim Bridenstine]] during this period of outreach.[1]

## Material dependence of the U.S. program

Ehret notes that Russia supplied the United States with RD-180 and RD-181 rocket engines for American launch vehicles, and that for years Russian vehicles ferried American astronauts to the [[international-space-station|International Space Station]] after [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] ended the manned shuttle program in 2011.[1]

## Break with the United States and pivot to China

According to Ehret, "within seconds of [[joe-biden|Biden]]'s entry into the White House," Russia left the Lunar Gateway Project, which he describes as thereafter sitting stagnant on a drawing board.[1] Russia instead accelerated its partnership with China's lunar program, having signed a joint lunar cooperation treaty with China's space agency in September 2019 and, on June 16, 2024, ratified treaties with China for the joint construction and operation of a permanent scientific base on the Moon with a goal of full industrialization and mining operations by 2040.[1]

## Soviet heritage

Ehret situates the modern agency within a longer Soviet record of planetary exploration: although less fortunate than the United States on the Moon, Russia landed ten probes on the surface of Venus during the 1961–1984 Venera program and pioneered the application of nuclear reactor technology to spacecraft, sending out thirty nuclear reactor units in thirty years.[1] The 1975 Apollo–Soyuz mission between the U.S. and Russian space agencies is presented as the high-water mark of an earlier cooperative era that failed to become the foundation of lasting space diplomacy.[1]
