# JPMorgan Chase

JPMorgan Chase is the largest bank in the United States. In January 2026 it was reported to be the defendant in a lawsuit brought by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] over alleged post-January 6 debanking.[1]

## Response to Trump's lawsuit

In a statement, the bank said: "While we regret President Trump has sued us, we believe the suit has no merit," adding, "We respect the president's right to sue us and our right to defend ourselves — that's what courts are for."[1] The dispute arose from Trump's claim that the bank improperly cut off accounts in the aftermath of January 6, 2021.[1]

## $5 billion Trump lawsuit (January 2026)

On Thursday, January 22, 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] filed a $5 billion lawsuit against JPMorgan Chase and CEO [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]], accusing the bank of cutting off his accounts for political reasons after decades-long business ties.[2] The suit was filed in Florida state court in Miami by Trump attorney Alejandro Brito.[2]

The complaint alleges JPMorgan abruptly terminated multiple accounts belonging to Trump and his hospitality companies in early 2021 despite the accounts being in good standing and having processed hundreds of millions of dollars in transactions, and that the bank provided no warning, appeal process, or alternative banking arrangements.[2] Trump's legal team argues the closures were driven by political and social considerations rather than legitimate financial or regulatory concerns.[2] The suit further alleges the bank and Dimon caused Trump, the Trump Organization, affiliated entities, and Trump family members to be placed on an internal banking blacklist shared across federally regulated institutions — characterized in the complaint as false, malicious, and designed to prevent other banks from doing business with them.[2]

JPMorgan Chase responded: "While we regret President Trump has sued us, we believe the suit has no merit. We respect the president's right to sue us and our right to defend ourselves — that's what courts are for."[2]

## US Virgin Islands Epstein litigation (2023)

In 2023, JPMorgan Chase was the defendant in a civil case brought by the [[us-virgin-islands|US Virgin Islands]] alleging that the bank facilitated [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]]'s sex-trafficking ring by handling his payments to recruiters and victims.[3] Discovery in the case sought depositions from prominent figures, including the bank's chief executive and Google co-founder [[larry-page|Larry Page]].[3] In May 2023, court filings showed prosecutors could not physically locate Page to serve him and asked to summon him through [[alphabet|Alphabet]] instead.[3]

### Jes Staley emails (February 2023)

Emails disclosed in the Virgin Islands suit and reported by [[bloomberg|Bloomberg]] showed former JPMorgan executive [[jes-staley|Jes Staley]] exchanging messages with [[jeffrey-epstein|Epstein]] in July 2010 that appeared to use Disney princess names as code for women, including Staley's line "Say hi to Snow White" and his request for "Beauty and the Beast."[8] The filing also alleged Epstein emailed Staley photographs of young women in seductive poses.[8] The government alleged that JPMorgan "continued to facilitate Epstein's ongoing trafficking" and that the bank's facilitation "made the sex-trafficking venture possible and allowed ongoing and future sex-trafficking."[8] Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] noted that despite redactions the filing left ample visible evidence, and that separate passages described a production company supplying [[harvey-weinstein|Harvey Weinstein]] with "medications and other paraphernalia to perform sex acts."[8]

## First Republic acquisition (2023)

In late April 2023, the Wall Street Journal reported that JPMorgan and [[pnc-financial-services-group|PNC]] were set to buy [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]], not through a private market-arranged deal but in a transaction following a government seizure of the troubled lender.[4] The seizure and sale, expected as soon as that weekend, would wipe out First Republic's equity and potentially impose losses on creditors.[4] ZeroHedge observed that JPMorgan, already the largest U.S. bank, was about to get even bigger by scooping up the good First Republic assets while leaving U.S. taxpayers holding the toxic ones.[4]

It was not immediately clear whether the $30 billion in deposits that JPMorgan and other banks had funneled into First Republic would be treated as insured funds, nor how a wipeout of that capital could avoid sparking a systemic crisis while the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] pursued what ZeroHedge called "monetary tightening through bank collapse."[4] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] compared the auction to the government-brokered [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]]–[[ubs|UBS]] deal and suggested large banks might be serving as custodians for assets shed by failing peers.[4]

## SEC fine over deleted emails (2023)

In June 2023 the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission]] fined JPMorgan Chase $4 million after roughly 47 million emails belonging to its retail banking group were mistakenly and permanently deleted.[5] The emails dated from January 1 to April 23, 2018 and were deleted in June 2019 from about 8,700 mailboxes, including those of as many as 7,500 employees who regularly worked with customers; many were business records the bank was required under SEC rules to retain for three years.[5]

According to a cease-and-desist order, the deletions occurred after JPMorgan's corporate compliance technology department, having tried unsuccessfully to delete communications from the 1970s and 1980s, sought help from an outside vendor managing the bank's email storage; the vendor failed to apply the three-year retention setting to "Chase" emails from early 2018.[5] The SEC said JPMorgan had been unable in at least twelve civil securities-related regulatory probes to comply with subpoenas and document requests for communications that had been permanently deleted.[5]

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] compared the episode to [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s email practices and called the penalty "a slap in the face of justice," noting that JPMorgan Chase manages more than $3 trillion in assets.[5] She also argued that chief executive [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] effectively sets the investigative priorities of the SEC and the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]], citing his suggestion that authorities crack down on investors shorting bank stocks, which the DOJ announced as a priority two weeks later.[5]

## 2019 internal Epstein report

In June 2023 the [[wall-street-journal|Wall Street Journal]] obtained an internal JPMorgan report, compiled in 2019, documenting the bank's ties to [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]].[6] The report showed that Epstein had been intimately involved in the bank's business deals and in meetings with government officials, and that he orchestrated meetings between JPMorgan executives and officials of the [[united-arab-emirates|United Arab Emirates]] and United Kingdom governments to facilitate deals and the bank's international expansion.[6] The Daily Caller characterized the relationship as one of "regular communication" between Epstein and the bank.[6]

## Epstein-linked Google and Brin accounts

Emails unsealed in August 2023 in the [[us-virgin-islands|U.S. Virgin Islands]] suit against the bank showed senior JPMorgan bankers accommodating [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] as he helped line up lucrative clients, including [[google|Google]] and co-founder [[sergey-brin|Sergey Brin]].[7] [[mary-erdoes|Mary Erdoes]], now CEO of the bank's asset and wealth management division, wrote in 2006 that the account team should be based in New York "solely because of our need to have a NY team cover a NY person, Jeffrey Epstein (as the advisor to the partners)."[7] Ann Borowiec, then a managing director at JPMorgan's New Jersey Private Bank, told colleagues a New York team had been chosen "to work with Jeffrey Epstein (advisor) on business oppy associated with GRATS that are terminating for Google founders."[7]

## US Virgin Islands damages demand (2023)

In a Manhattan federal court filing in July 2023, the [[us-virgin-islands|United States Virgin Islands]] said it was seeking at least $190 million from JPMorgan Chase in its lawsuit accusing the bank of enabling [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]]'s sex trafficking, and also demanded that the bank commit to reforms protecting against human trafficking.[9] Epstein, who maintained a private retreat in the territory and brought many of his victims there before his death in 2019, was a JPMorgan client from 1998 to 2013.[9]

USVI Attorney General [[ariel-smith|Ariel Smith]] said she was gratified that victims had received some measure of compensation but that "more needs to be done to hold JPMorgan Chase accountable."[9] The USVI suit was one of two filed against the bank in late 2022; JPMorgan settled the other, a class action on behalf of Epstein victims, for $290 million without admitting liability.[9] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] called the developments seismic, noting the direct accusation by a sitting territorial attorney general and the parallel allegation that former USVI First Lady [[cecile-de-jongh|Cecile de Jongh]] covered for Epstein in a quid pro quo scheme.[9]

## Strategic mineral reserve proposal (2025)

Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] said in an October 15, 2025 CNBC interview that the United States needs to establish a strategic mineral reserve and that JPMorgan Chase is interested in working with the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] to create one.[10] The proposal accompanied the administration's announcement of [[price-floors-industrial-policy-2025|price floors across a range of industries]] intended to counter Chinese price suppression in rare earths and other strategic sectors.[10]

## Rakoff ruling on Epstein liability (May 2023)

On May 1, 2023, U.S. District Judge [[jed-rakoff|Jed Rakoff]] rejected motions to dismiss the civil cases brought against JPMorgan Chase, longtime executive [[jes-staley|Jes Staley]], and [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]], finding that plaintiffs had pled enough to proceed on claims that the institutions could be liable for [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]]'s sex trafficking.[11] In a 54-page decision, Rakoff wrote that plaintiffs had "pled sufficient facts to support their allegations that JP Morgan had … knowledge of Jeffrey Epstein's sex-trafficking venture, either directly or by recklessly disregarding what was plainly to be seen."[11] The ruling came in cases brought by several alleged Epstein victims and by the [[us-virgin-islands|United States Virgin Islands]] government, and also covered a claim JPMorgan itself had filed against Staley.[11]

## January 2023 victim lawsuit

A lawsuit filed by one of [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]]'s victims in January 2023 named JP Morgan alongside [[jes-staley|Jes Staley]], the former [[barclays|Barclays]] chief accused in court documents of having "personally observ[ed] the sexual abuse of young women" by Epstein.[12] Staley had worked at JP Morgan in 2013, when Epstein was a private client of the firm.[12]

## Bitcoin access for clients (2025)

At the bank's annual investor day on May 19, 2025, chief executive [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] announced that JPMorgan Chase — the largest US bank — would soon allow clients to buy [[bitcoin|bitcoin]], while stopping short of offering custody.[13] "We are going to allow you to buy it," Dimon said. "We're not going to custody it. We're going to put it in statements for clients."[13] The move marked a sharp departure from Dimon's long-running public skepticism toward the cryptocurrency and from his earlier threat to fire JPMorgan employees who traded it.[13]

## Dimon deposition (March 2023)

In March 2023 Reuters reported that chief executive [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] would be deposed under oath about the bank's relationship with [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]], a former client, with the interview expected in early May.[14] The disclosure came from [[brad-edwards|Brad Edwards]], an attorney for women who allege Epstein abused them and who are suing the largest U.S. bank for allegedly enabling his sex trafficking.[14] [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary paired the JPMorgan case with the parallel [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]] litigation, arguing that the mere association of major banks with Epstein victims in headlines during a financial crisis carried more public weight than the underlying legal record.[14]

## Bitcoin call (May 2025)

In May 2025 JPMorgan analysts flipped from gold to [[bitcoin|bitcoin]], predicting that bitcoin would outperform gold over the remainder of 2025, a call reported as the bitcoin price reclaimed $100,000 and rose to $106,000.[15]

## USVI disclosure of $1 billion in suspicious Epstein transfers (September 2023)

In September 2023 the [[us-virgin-islands|U.S. Virgin Islands]] told a federal court in Manhattan that JPMorgan had flagged more than $1 billion in suspicious transactions linked to [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] and reported them to the U.S. government, a disclosure made in a 2019 filing to the U.S. Department of the Treasury after Epstein died in a Manhattan jail cell a month following his sex-trafficking arrest.[16] Bloomberg noted it was the first time the sheer volume of Epstein's financial activity at the bank over a 16-year period had been disclosed; Epstein had banked there from the late 1990s until 2013, when the firm cut ties.[16] USVI attorney Mimi Liu said "JPMorgan was a full-service bank for Jeffrey Epstein's sex trafficking."[16] The bank denied letting Epstein's activities slide and said it reported around 150 cash transactions to a federal regulator between 2002 and 2013; the territory had told the judge a month earlier that the bank facilitated over $1.1 million in payments from Epstein to "girls or women," many with Eastern European surnames.[16] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] observed that the name most associated with elite child trafficking could not surface without a major financial institution attached to it, adding: "Drip. Drip. Flood."[16]

## Russian court seizure of $439.5 million (2024)

In April 2024 a Russian arbitration court in St Petersburg ordered the seizure of JPMorgan Chase funds totalling $439.5 million, a week after the Kremlin-run lender [[vtb-bank|VTB]] launched legal action against the largest US bank to recoup money stuck under Washington's sanctions regime.[17] The seizure order, published in the Russian court register, targeted funds in JPMorgan's accounts as well as shares in its Russian subsidiaries, assets that had already been frozen by Russian authorities in the wake of Western sanctions.[17] The underlying dispute concerned $439 million that VTB had held in a JPMorgan account in the United States; when Washington sanctioned VTB, JPMorgan moved the funds to a separate escrow account, leaving neither party able to access them.[17] The Financial Times framed the episode as evidence of the fallout Western companies face from punitive measures against Moscow and of the difficulty Western lenders have had closing their Russia operations since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.[17]

Badlands contributor GhostofBasedPatrickHenry linked the seizure to [[executive-order-13818|Executive Order 13818]], the December 2017 order signed by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] authorising seizure of property belonging to individuals and companies involved in serious human rights abuse and trafficking, noting JPMorgan's exposure through [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] and former executive [[jes-staley|Jes Staley]], and remarking that "the only narrative better than the United States government seizing JP Morgan assets is the Russian government seizing JP Morgan assets."[17] He read the move as a sign that "the East" would increasingly throw its weight around as a multipolar world emerged.[17]

## State objections to the Epstein settlement (October 2023)

JPMorgan Chase's $290 million class-action settlement with accusers of [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] drew an objection from many U.S. states in October 2023, on the grounds that the accord could limit their ability to seek compensation for sexual abuse victims.[18] In a letter made public on Monday, October 23 in Manhattan federal court, the attorneys general of 16 states and Washington, D.C. complained about settlement language preventing "any sovereign or government" from seeking damages arising from sex trafficking by Epstein and his associates.[18]

The attorneys general said that including such language without their consent would deter them from seeking damages for sex trafficking victims — not only Epstein's — under the federal [[trafficking-victims-protection-act|Trafficking Victims Protection Act]].[18] They also observed that [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]]'s similar $75 million agreement with Epstein's accusers contained no such language.[18] New Mexico Attorney General [[raul-torrez|Raul Torrez]] wrote that Epstein's surviving victims "should be fully compensated for the profound harm they have suffered," but that the agreement "improperly seeks to release (the states') claims for victim-specific relief."[18] Signatories included Arizona, California, Connecticut, Delaware, the District of Columbia, Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, Mississippi, New York, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Utah and Vermont.[18]

## Rakoff ruling allowing the Epstein suits to proceed (March 2023)

On March 20, 2023, U.S. District Judge [[jed-rakoff|Jed Rakoff]] ruled that JPMorgan Chase & Co. and [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]] would have to face lawsuits claiming they enabled [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] to traffic his victims.[19] Two women identified as "Jane Doe" had filed federal class-action suits against the banks in November 2022, and the [[us-virgin-islands|U.S. Virgin Islands]] filed its own suit against JPMorgan in December.[19] In a four-page order, Rakoff held that the women could pursue claims that the defendants "knowingly benefited from participating in a sex-trafficking venture," "obstructed enforcement of the [[trafficking-victims-protection-act|Trafficking Victims Protection Act]]," and "negligently failed to exercise reasonable care to prevent physical harm," as well as a claim that the banks negligently failed to exercise reasonable care as institutions providing non-routine banking; all other claims were dismissed.[19] With respect to the U.S. Virgin Islands suit against JPMorgan, Rakoff allowed the claim that the bank "knowingly benefited from participating in a sex-trafficking venture" to proceed.[19]

## Russian seizure of JPMorgan accounts in the VTB dispute (April 2024)

A Russian court ordered the seizure of funds in JPMorgan Chase bank accounts inside Russia, according to court filings reported on Wednesday, April 24, 2024, in a lawsuit brought by the state-owned [[vtb-bank|VTB]] as it sought to regain funds blocked abroad.[20] The ruling by the Arbitration Court of St Petersburg and the Leningrad Region was dated April 22.[20]

The order followed JPMorgan Chase's own suit against VTB, filed in New York the previous week, seeking to halt VTB's efforts to recover $439.5 million from an account frozen after Russia sent its army into Ukraine in 2022 and VTB was placed under sanctions.[20] In its complaint in federal court in Manhattan, JPMorgan described VTB's attempt to recover the money inside Russia as a "blatant breach" of the parties' agreement to have disputes addressed in New York.[20]

The Russian court said it had ordered the seizure of all funds in JPMorgan bank accounts in Russia, including correspondent accounts and those opened in the name of a subsidiary, while stating that it had not seized securities and property held by JPMorgan funds, nor the jpmorgan.ru domain.[20] JPMorgan Chase declined to comment on the interim measures, and VTB did not immediately comment, having previously declined to discuss its legal disputes with the bank.[20]

## Role in the 2023 banking crisis and acquisition of First Republic

A May 31, 2023 Badlands essay by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described JPMorgan Chase as the largest bank in the United States and the third largest in the world, behind only Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and China Construction Bank.[22] Citing the firm's own media boilerplate, the piece noted that JPMorgan Chase held $3.7 trillion in assets and $303 billion in stockholders' equity as of March 31, 2023.[22]

The article drew a parallel between the bank's conduct in the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 financial crisis]] and in the [[2023-banking-crisis|2023 banking crisis]]. In 2008 JPMorgan Chase purchased [[washington-mutual|Washington Mutual]], the largest U.S. savings and loan association and a heavy participant in risky mortgages, for roughly $1.9 billion after it was seized by the government, and acquired the investment bank [[bear-stearns|Bear Stearns]] for about $1.2 billion in a transaction facilitated by the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] to prevent its collapse.[22] Other crisis-era purchases included Bank of New York Mellon's shareholder services business for $16.5 billion, RBS Sempra Commodities' global oil, gas, metals and agricultural trading operations for $1.7 billion, and MF Global's stake in the London Metal Exchange for $39.1 million.[22]

In May 2023 the bank acquired [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]]. Per the firm's press release the deal included approximately $173 billion of loans and $30 billion of securities, the assumption of about $92 billion of deposits (including $30 billion of large bank deposits to be repaid post-close or eliminated in consolidation), and [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] loss-share agreements covering acquired single-family residential and commercial loans plus $50 billion of five-year fixed-rate term financing; JPMorgan did not assume First Republic's corporate debt or preferred stock.[22] [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] said the government "invited us and others to step up, and we did," and that the bank's financial strength allowed it to structure a bid minimising costs to the Deposit Insurance Fund.[22]

Ashe in America noted that the company describes itself as "built on the foundation of more than 1,200 predecessor institutions," and questioned Dimon's claim that the integration burden precluded further acquisitions, suggesting instead that the [[banking-cartel|Banking Cartel]] consistently makes moves that consolidate and centralise its power.[22]
