# Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) is the U.S. federal agency that insures deposits at private banks. Badlands Media characterizes federal deposit insurance not as a consumer protection but as a "cabal trick" that destroyed the public banking option and entrenched private banking control of the financial system.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Role in ending postal banking

During the post-World War II economic boom, interest rates at private banks rose, and after the FDIC offered depositors at private banks a guarantee, usage of the [[postal-savings-system|Postal Savings System]] declined until the program was discontinued in 1967.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) The article argues that private banks had historically been risky and that postal savings accounts held a competitive advantage precisely because they were safe; by removing that risk through insurance, the government erased the advantage, allowing private banks to offer higher interest through riskier investments while depositors were protected with taxpayer money.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Private bankers, unable to compete for depositors in hard times, are said to have lobbied Congress to create federal deposit insurance for exactly this purpose.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Criticism advanced in the article

Badlands Media disputes the claim that the FDIC is funded by the banks themselves, pointing to decades of what it calls back-door bailouts using money from the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], characterized as taxpayer money handed to Wall Street banks so they can profit by pumping up the stock market.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) The [[american-bankers-association|American Bankers Association]] states that FDIC-insured depositors have never lost a penny of an insured deposit; the article calls this a lie on the grounds that taxpayers have paid for all bank bailouts for generations.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) It also emphasizes the $250,000 insurance cap, arguing that a public option would remove any limit on protecting the American people's savings.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Data on financial exclusion

FDIC household survey data cited in the article found that 6.5 percent of U.S. households (8.4 million) were "unbanked," meaning they had no bank account, and 18.7 percent (24.2 million) were "underbanked," holding a savings or checking account but still going outside the banking system for some financial services.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Relationship to FedAccounts

Because deposits at a Federal Reserve Bank cannot be defaulted on, the [[fedaccounts|FedAccounts]] proposal would lessen or eliminate the need for widespread deposit insurance altogether.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Badlands Media argues that Wall Street banks fear losing FDIC protection because without it the majority of Americans would choose the public option, ending private banking's control over government and eliminating taxpayer bailouts.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## First Republic seizure (2023)

In late April 2023, reporting indicated that [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]] would be sold to [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan]] and [[pnc-financial-services-group|PNC]] in a transaction following a government seizure rather than a private, market-arranged deal.[2] The seizure was expected to wipe out First Republic's equity and potentially impose losses on creditors, with acquiring banks taking the sound assets while the toxic remainder fell to the deposit insurance system and ultimately to taxpayers.[2] It was not immediately clear whether the $30 billion in deposits earlier funneled into First Republic by large banks would be treated as insured funds.[2]

## March 2023 emergency backstop

On the night of March 12–13, 2023, the FDIC acted jointly with the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the Treasury to backstop the banking system after the failures of [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]] and [[signature-bank|Signature Bank]].[3] Market commentary noted that although the three agencies had "saved the world" again, regional bank shares nonetheless fell dramatically the next session amid multiple trading halts, and credit-default-swap spreads widened across the global banking majors — raising the question of why systemic signals kept worsening if the official backstop was as robust as advertised.[3]

## Trump transition proposals to abolish the agency (December 2024)

In December 2024 the [[trump-transition|Trump transition team]] began exploring pathways to dramatically shrink, consolidate or even eliminate Washington's top bank watchdogs, and in interviews with potential nominees to lead bank regulatory agencies, Trump advisers and officials from the newly formed [[doge|Department of Government Efficiency]] asked whether the president-elect could abolish the FDIC outright.[4] Advisers also asked nominees under consideration for the FDIC and the [[office-of-the-comptroller-of-the-currency|Office of the Comptroller of the Currency]] whether deposit insurance could be absorbed into the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]].[4]

Any proposal to eliminate the FDIC or another agency would require congressional action; Washington has never shut down a major cabinet-level agency and has rarely closed non-cabinet bodies of the FDIC's kind.[4] While bank executives were optimistic that the incoming administration would ease rules on capital cushions, consumer protections and merger scrutiny, FDIC deposit insurance was described as near sacred, since any move undermining even the perception of coverage could ripple through the banking system and compound customer fears in a crisis.[4] The concern was grounded in recent experience: after several bank failures the previous year, customers panicked about the safety of deposits at smaller institutions and fled to the largest banks perceived as too important to fail, prompting smaller banks to call for wider deposit insurance protections to stay competitive.[4]

## March 2023 blanket deposit guarantee study

In March 2023 Bloomberg reported that US officials were studying ways to temporarily expand FDIC coverage to all deposits — roughly $18 trillion — a step sought by a coalition of banks arguing it was needed to head off a potential financial crisis.[5] Mid-sized banks had demanded a two-year total deposit insurance scheme from the agency, warning of further forced mergers otherwise, while [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] staff reviewed whether regulators held enough emergency authority to insure deposits above the $250,000 cap without action by [[us-congress|Congress]].[5] The FDIC had already joined the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the Treasury in shoring up the banking sector with special loans and guarantees of uninsured deposits at the failed banks.[5] The episode is treated at [[2023-proposal-to-guarantee-all-us-bank-deposits|the 2023 proposal to guarantee all US bank deposits]].[5]

## Role in the First Republic resolution (May 2023)

In the [[2023-banking-crisis|2023 banking crisis]] the FDIC's failed bank list recorded three failures — [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]], [[signature-bank|Signature Bank]] and [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]] — which together held $532 billion in assets, exceeding in inflation-adjusted terms the $526 billion held by the 25 banks that collapsed in 2008.[6] In the First Republic resolution the FDIC provided [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]] with loss-share agreements covering acquired single-family residential mortgage loans and commercial loans, together with $50 billion of five-year, fixed-rate term financing.[6] [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] said JPMorgan's bid had been structured to minimise costs to the agency's Deposit Insurance Fund.[6]

## Creation in 1933

The FDIC was created in [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]]'s first hundred days to protect citizens' savings from future crises, one of the reforms made politically possible by the [[pecora-commission|Pecora Commission]]'s exposure of Wall Street fraud.[7] It was enacted alongside [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]] banking separation, the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]] and the [[reconstruction-finance-corporation|Reconstruction Finance Corporation]] as part of the [[new-deal|New Deal]].[7]
