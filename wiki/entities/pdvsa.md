# PDVSA

PDVSA (Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A.) is Venezuela's state-owned oil company and the source of the funds at the center of the money-laundering cases that Badlands writers connect to the January 2026 confrontation with the global banking system.[1]

Billions of dollars embezzled from PDVSA were laundered by banker [[mathias-krull|Mathias Krull]], whose 2018 arrest by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s Department of Justice and subsequent cooperation led to large asset forfeitures at banks including [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]], with the money owed to the government of Venezuela.[1] Venezuelan oil revenue also formed the subject of Trump's January 9, 2026 executive order shielding those funds, held in U.S. Treasury accounts, from seizure or judicial attachment.[1]

## The 2015 "Investigation Case" disclosure

On December 31, 2015 PDVSA published its Consolidated Financial Statement, in which note 32 on pages 78–79, entitled "Investigation Case," referred to a U.S. criminal indictment against multiple PDVSA representatives, all of whom pleaded guilty.[2] PDVSA stated that the company itself was a victim of the fraud, vowed to overhaul its operations, and announced a deep internal investigation that it expected could lead to criminal indictments in Venezuela.[2]

Badlands commentator [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] treated the disclosure as the origin point of [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]]'s corruption purges, of the lawsuits Maduro filed against 42 corrupt oil executives in U.S. court, and of [[alex-saab|Alex Saab]]'s cooperation with U.S. authorities during the first Trump administration.[2] On this reading it was the arrest of corrupt officials arising from the investigation that prompted legacy media to brand Maduro a ruthless dictator, when he was cleaning up corruption inside his own government tied to the Bush dynasty and its allies in Big Oil.[2]


## The 2018 litigation and its revival

After [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]] was given a laptop in March 2017 containing evidence of large-scale embezzlement, attorney [[david-boies|David Boies]] filed civil lawsuits a year later in the District of Southern Florida against 42 oil-industry executives, and also in Geneva, Switzerland, where the companies involved maintained offices.[3] Swiss authorities raided the [[helsinge|Helsinge]] office in Geneva and seized computer servers, launching additional investigations and arrests.[3] Three months later Helsinge's Miami office was raided and [[matthias-krull|Matthias Krull]] was arrested for laundering $1.2 billion stolen from PDVSA, ultimately cooperating and mapping the network that moved embezzled funds from Venezuela to the U.S. and Europe.[3]

[[executive-order-13850|Executive Order 13850]], issued in November 2018, targeted entities doing business with PDVSA by freezing bank wires and driving Suspicious Activity Reports; defense attorneys used it to argue that discovery would violate national security, and the Florida lawsuits were dismissed.[3] Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] contends the case was paused rather than closed, and that lifting sanctions would revive discovery while newly lawful PDVSA bank wires would expose discrepancies to regulators and trigger fresh investigations.[3] Some PDVSA officials are said to have ties to traditional cartels that the Trump administration has designated terrorist organizations.[3]

## Reconstruction and the reparations reading (January 2026)

After [[operation-absolute-resolve|Operation Absolute Resolve]], President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said American oil majors would "go in, spend billions of dollars, fix the badly broken infrastructure, the oil infrastructure, and start making money for the country," framing the arrangement as recovery of assets the "socialist regime stole from us."[4] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] suggested that this promised expenditure by oil companies could in fact represent reparations legitimately owed under the lawsuits PDVSA filed in 2018 against dozens of the world's most powerful oil executives, accusing them of corruption and of stealing Venezuela's wealth.[4]

## Output cutbacks (January 2026)

In early January 2026, PDVSA asked some joint-venture partners to reduce oil output, according to a Reuters report citing anonymous sources.[5] The request was attributed to rising onshore inventories and operational constraints, including shortages of the diluents needed to process Venezuela's heavy crude.[5]

The cutbacks came as energy markets weighed whether sanctions relief following [[operation-absolute-resolve|Operation Absolute Resolve]] could allow increased Venezuelan output.[5] Morningstar analyst Joshua Aguilar said the firm's oil price forecasts would not change, since Venezuela requires years of large capital investment to modernize its oil infrastructure, adding that "regime changes rarely lead to rapid investment and stabilizing supply, particularly as opposing factions vie for power."[5]

## Citgo ownership and the December 2025 forced sale

Badlands reporting in December 2025 described [[citgo|Citgo]] — the holder of the Venezuelan oil rights at issue in Washington's confrontation with Caracas — as owned by PDVSA, the Venezuelan state oil company.[6] That ownership was disrupted when US judges ordered [[venezuela|Venezuela]] to sell Citgo to satisfy owed debts, a court order issued on December 2, 2025.[6]

An exemption granted by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to Citgo from the 2019 Venezuela sanctions forbids the company from paying taxes or fees to the Venezuelan government for the oil, but does permit Citgo to give PDVSA crude oil, which PDVSA can then sell for money — an arrangement Badlands contributors flagged as evidence that the public narrative around the standoff is incomplete.[6]

## The Chávez purge and the long decline

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] describes PDVSA as "the ultimate prize" in Washington's decades-long contest with [[venezuela|Venezuela]], noting that under [[hugo-chavez|Hugo Chávez]] it ranked among the world's top five crude exporters, pumping 3.5 million barrels a day.[7] Chávez believed the old elite inside PDVSA had spent decades siphoning profits into Miami condominiums and Zurich accounts while the slums of Caracas festered.[7] In 2002–2003, after surviving the [[2002-venezuelan-coup-attempt|April 2002 coup]] and the business elite's "owners' strike" that paralyzed petroleum production and cut GDP by an estimated 27 percent, Chávez fired 19,000 PDVSA managers, replaced them with loyalists, and redirected the resulting petrodollars into health, education, and housing programs.[7]

Under sanctions, Venezuelan oil production fell from 3.5 million barrels per day under Chávez to a low of 300,000 in 2020; PDVSA's rigs rusted and skilled engineers fled to Guyana and Trinidad.[7] The [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]]'s freezing of $7 billion in [[citgo|Citgo]] assets in 2019 and the transfer of control to [[juan-guaido|Juan Guaidó]]'s parallel government intensified the cash-flow squeeze.[7]

## Chinese and Russian lifelines

[[china|China]] restructured $60 billion in loans across 2016–2019, converting maturing obligations into long-term, oil-backed commitments under which Venezuela pledged future crude shipments, often at below-market rates, to service the debt.[7] By 2020 Chinese firms such as CNPC were operating joint ventures in the [[orinoco-mining-arc|Orinoco Belt]], extracting oil that doubled as debt repayment, and when sanctions choked PDVSA's cash flow Beijing's state banks quietly extended grace periods and rolled over principal to prevent default.[7] [[rosneft|Rosneft]], the Kremlin-linked oil giant, prepaid for Venezuelan crude and injected billions into PDVSA's coffers, while [[russia|Russia]] refinanced $17 billion in military contracts, accepting oil and gold as payment.[7]
