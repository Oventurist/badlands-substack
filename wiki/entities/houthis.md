# Houthis

The Houthis are a Yemeni armed movement widely described as an [[iran|Iranian]] proxy force. In late 2023 the group launched what observers characterized as a full-blown war on commercial shipping in the [[red-sea-shipping-crisis|Red Sea]], attacking container vessels with drones and missiles on a daily basis.[1]

## Attacks on shipping and U.S. warships

By late December 2023, Houthi attacks had grown frequent enough to trigger delays and rising prices on goods as major shipping liners avoided Red Sea transit altogether.[1] American warships operating in waters off Yemen also came under direct attack.[1]

U.S. defense officials expressed frustration that the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] was being held back from responding forcefully against Houthi positions in Yemen by the [[joe-biden|Biden]] [[white-house|White House]].[1] Reporting in *Politico* cited current and former military officials frustrated by the administration's initial response to the Houthis' attacks, while top military brass pushed for a more forceful posture lest the Iranian proxies grow more emboldened.[1] The *Wall Street Journal* ran the headline "U.S. Naval Deterrence Is Going, Going, Maybe Even Gone."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] wrote that he had assigned [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] to the story ahead of a coming Power Hour because it had "the gravitational effects of [[devolution|Devolution]] written ALL over it."[1] He argued that whatever was actually happening in the Red Sea was so far failing to trigger a U.S. military response under the framing of National Essential Functions, and that Biden either had no desire to interfere with the regional chaos or, more likely in his view, no ability to do so.[1]

## January 2024 attacks and safe-passage offer

On January 18, 2024, Houthi fighters unsuccessfully targeted the U.S.-owned tanker M/V Chem Ranger, a Marshall Islands-flagged and Greek-operated vessel, firing two anti-ship ballistic missiles that fell into the water without causing damage or injury, according to [[centcom|U.S. Central Command]].[2] By then the group had launched more than thirty attacks on commercial shipping from Yemen since late November 2023, and the United States had conducted five rounds of retaliatory strikes since an initial salvo the previous week.[2] The [[pentagon|Pentagon]] said the strikes were damaging Houthi infrastructure and forcing the Iranian-backed militia to scale down its operations, though the group remained resilient and the hostilities pushed major shipping companies onto the long route around Africa.[2]

Senior Houthi official [[mohammed-al-bukhaiti|Mohammed al-Bukhaiti]] told the Russian outlet Izvestia that Russian and Chinese vessels transiting the Red Sea would be granted safe passage, saying that "as for all other countries, including Russia and China, their shipping in the region is not threatened" and that the movement was ready to ensure their safe passage because free navigation played a significant role for Yemen.[2] He added that attacks on vessels "in any way connected with [[israel|Israel]]" would continue, although reporting noted that Houthi strikes had hit ships with no apparent Israeli connection.[2]


## 2025 ceasefire announcement

In May 2025 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said the United States would stop bombing the Houthis in Yemen after claiming the Iran-aligned group had agreed to stop interrupting important shipping lanes in the Middle East.[3] "They said please don't bomb us anymore and we're not going to attack your ships," Trump said during an Oval Office meeting with Canadian Prime Minister [[mark-carney|Mark Carney]]; there was no immediate response from the Houthis.[3] Israel was not informed in advance of the [[us-houthi-ceasefire-announcement-2025|announcement]], and Trump treated the Houthi conflict with Israel as a separate matter, saying "I don't know anything about that, but they want nothing to do with us."[3]

## U.S. strikes and Trump's warning to Iran (March 2025)

On Monday, March 17, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] explicitly linked the actions of Yemen's Houthi rebels to their main benefactor, [[iran|Iran]], warning Tehran it would "suffer the consequences" for further attacks by the group.[4] The remarks, posted to Truth Social, escalated his administration's new campaign of airstrikes against the rebels, which killed at least 53 people over the preceding weekend.[4] U.S. officials, speaking anonymously, said the strikes hit more than 40 targets and that further airstrikes were planned in the coming days.[4]

Houthi supporters rallied in several cities after the strikes, vowing revenge against America and Israel over the blocking of aid to the [[gaza|Gaza]] Strip.[4] The group's al-Masirah satellite news channel broadcast young boys chanting the movement's slogan: "God is the greatest; death to America; death to Israel; curse the Jews; victory to Islam."[4]

Badlands commentary compared warnings of "full scale war with Iran" to first-term predictions of nuclear war with North Korea, noted that Putin and MBS had both offered to mediate peace talks, and argued that in blaming Iran for Houthi attacks Trump was in fact targeting what the writers called rogue elements of a CIA proxy state.[4] Other contributors observed that the Houthis and Iran had become inextricably linked in Washington and Tel Aviv, where some were eager to equate a surgical strike against the militia with a declaration of war on Iran.[4]

## Maersk Hangzhou attack (December 2023)

At the end of December 2023, Houthi militants attacked the [[maersk|Maersk]] container ship Maersk Hangzhou in the Southern Red Sea, prompting the shipping giant to suspend all container sailings in the waterway.[5] [[centcom|US Central Command]] said the vessel was struck by a missile and that the destroyers USS Gravely and USS Laboon responded, with Gravely shooting down two anti-ship ballistic missiles fired from Houthi-controlled areas of [[yemen|Yemen]].[5] CENTCOM described the incident as the 23rd "illegal attack" by Houthi rebels on commercial vessels in the corridor since 19 November.[5]

Houthi fighters in four small boats attempted to board the ship but were repelled by its private security team.[5] Attack helicopters from the carrier USS Dwight D. Eisenhower and from USS Gravely answered the distress call, came under fire from the militants, and in self-defence destroyed three of the boats and killed their crews, while a fourth escaped.[5] Senator [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]] responded to the escalation by urging the Biden administration to strike [[iran|Iran]] directly, saying "Without Iran there are no Houthis."[5]

## Maersk ship attack and US retaliation (December 2023)

On December 31, 2023, Iran-backed Houthi forces attacked a Maersk container ship in the Red Sea, prompting U.S. attack helicopters to destroy three small boats and kill ten rebels.[6] A group spokesperson warned of "consequences and repercussions" for the U.S. action, and military spokesman [[yahya-sarea|Yahya Sarea]] confirmed on Al-Masirah television that "US enemy forces attacked three boats belonging to the Yemeni Naval Forces, which led to the martyrdom and the loss of ten people from the Naval Forces."[6] Sarea characterized the fighters as "performing their humanitarian and moral duty" in deterring Israel-related commercial vessels from transiting the Red Sea "in solidarity and support for the Palestinian people."[6] The [[maersk-container-ship-attack|episode]] contributed to mounting regional instability, with [[iran|Iran]] subsequently dispatching a warship to the Red Sea.[6]

## Red Sea undersea cable denial (February 2024)

In a statement issued Tuesday, February 27, 2024, the Houthi military spokesman explicitly denied any intention to knock out undersea regional internet cables, while reiterating that the Iran-linked group's top goal remained blocking commercial shipping and supplies to [[israel|Israel]].[7] The full statement, carried by Al Mayadeen, read: "We are keen to spare all cables and their services from any risks and to provide the necessary facilities for their maintenance. The decision to prevent the passage of Israeli ships does not include ships belonging to international companies licensed to carry out marine cable work."[7]

The denial followed widespread reports that up to four undersea telecoms cables in the Red Sea between Jeddah and Djibouti had been damaged; the operator Seacom reported connectivity problems after reports originating in Israeli media, which Sky News Arabia picked up on Monday.[7] Analysts noted that although Red Sea waters had been the scene of daily Houthi attacks on shipping and Western coalition warships, a fiber-optic sabotage campaign would be difficult to execute because it would likely require submarine or deep-water equipment the Houthis probably lack.[7]

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] argued that the episode's ingredients — an unfamiliar radical group, an ambiguous denial, damage reported far away, and a vague multi-week repair timeline — would be exactly what a "raging globalist" would assemble to justify a sudden internet blackout in North America.[7]

## Russian and Chinese shipping arrangement

In March 2024, the Yemen-based Houthis told China and Russia that their ships could sail through the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden without being attacked; in exchange, the two countries were reported as potentially providing political support to the group in bodies such as the United Nations Security Council, according to people with knowledge of the militant group's discussions.[8] Later that month, Russian warships from the Pacific Fleet — the missile cruiser Varyag and the frigate Marshal Shaposhnikov — crossed the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait and entered the Red Sea, a maritime region plagued by Houthi attacks and crowded with naval vessels.[8] The Houthis had for months attacked vessels in the Red Sea in retaliation for Israel's military actions in Gaza, forcing many ships to redirect their journeys.[8]

## MSC United strike (December 2023)

On 26 December 2023 the Houthis claimed a missile attack on the container ship MSC UNITED, declaring that "the naval forces of the Yemeni Armed Forces carried out a targeting operation against the commercial ship 'MSC UNITED' with appropriate naval missiles."[9] The vessel was reportedly en route to Pakistan; no crew injuries were reported and the Houthis said the ship had ignored three warning calls before being fired upon.[9] The incident coincided with a fresh Houthi missile attack on [[israel|Israel]] and unfolded as the [[joe-biden|Biden]] White House struggled to stand up its multinational naval coalition, with commentators describing the group as acting "with impunity."[9]

## Eternity C attack (July 2025)

On Monday, July 7, 2025, at least two crew members of a Greek-owned vessel were wounded and two were missing in the Red Sea, according to Greek government officials and the vessel's owner.[10] In the second comparable attack in the Red Sea within 24 hours, the Liberian-flagged cargo ship Eternity C was targeted by sea drones and skiffs off Hodeidah, 50 nautical miles west of the Yemeni capital Sanaa, which is controlled by the Iran-aligned Houthi rebels.[10] No party claimed responsibility for the strikes.[10]

## Red Sea attacks and the US naval response (December 2023)

By mid-December 2023 Iran-backed Houthi attacks on commercial vessels transiting the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab Strait had become a daily occurrence, and the group had also launched rockets and drones into southern Israel, triggering US warship intercepts.[11] In response the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] announced [[operation-prosperity-guardian|Operation Prosperity Guardian]], a multinational task force established under the [[combined-maritime-forces|Combined Maritime Forces]] umbrella and its Task Force 153 to protect shipping in the corridor.[11]
