# Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) is a United States federal regulatory agency overseeing consumer finance. Its unusual funding structure — drawing money outside the ordinary congressional appropriations process — became the subject of a major constitutional challenge in the early 2020s.[1]

## Fifth Circuit funding ruling and Supreme Court review

The [[fifth-circuit-court-of-appeals|Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals]] declared the CFPB's funding system unconstitutional, and the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] agreed to review that decision for its October 2023 term.[1] The case was one of several Fifth Circuit rulings on federal regulatory power headed to the high court that term, alongside a decision rejecting the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]]'s use of in-house administrative judges.[1] Bloomberg framed the cluster as a test of how far the Supreme Court's conservative majority would go in remaking the legal landscape governing the administrative state.[1]

## Firing of Director Rohit Chopra (2025)

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] fired CFPB director [[rohit-chopra|Rohit Chopra]] in early February 2025, in what was described as the latest purge of a Biden administration holdover.[2] Chopra had been one of the more important regulators from the previous Democratic administration still serving after Trump took office on January 20, and his tenure saw the removal of medical debt from credit reports and limits on overdraft penalties — measures many in the financial industry viewed as regulatory overreach.[2] In a social media post about his departure, Chopra thanked Americans who had shared their ideas and experiences with the bureau.[2]

Commentary in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] noted that the CFPB was the brainchild of [[elizabeth-warren|Elizabeth Warren]] and is funded not by Congress but by the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]].[2]

## Andreessen criticism and the DOGE debate (December 2024)

In December 2024, an MSNBC opinion piece cited [[marc-andreessen|Marc Andreessen]]'s recent complaints about the CFPB — described as the agency created after the 2008 financial crisis to protect consumers from deceptive financial products — as evidence of hypocrisy, given reporting that Andreessen was helping identify and interview candidates for President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s government spending advisory commission, [[doge|DOGE]].[3]

## DOGE AI deregulation (July 2025)

According to internal documents reported in July 2025, the [[doge-ai-deregulation-decision-tool|DOGE AI Deregulation Decision Tool]] had handled 100% of recent deregulation efforts at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, making the agency one of the first testbeds for [[doge|DOGE]]'s automated rule-cutting system.[4]

## Shutdown and contract cancellations (February 2025)

In February 2025 the bureau was effectively frozen under stop-work orders issued by acting Director [[russ-vought|Russell Vought]], who simultaneously headed the [[office-of-management-and-budget|Office of Management and Budget]].[5] On the morning of 11 February 2025 the bureau's supervision director, [[lorelei-salas|Lorelei Salas]], and its enforcement director, [[eric-halperin|Eric Halperin]], both resigned, citing those orders in emails to their teams that were shared with NPR by current and former employees; both had held their posts for over three years.[5] Prior to resigning the two were placed on leave by [[mark-paoletta|Mark Paoletta]], general counsel at OMB, for reasons that were never publicly established.[5] Salas wrote that the bureau had been "instructed to stand down" and that she did not believe halting all supervisory activities and examinations was appropriate or lawful.[5]

At the same time the CFPB moved to cancel more than $100 million in vendor contracts as a cost-cutting measure, a step reported to put its cybersecurity efforts at risk because cybersecurity and internal management systems were absent from a list of "essential" contracts obtained by Bloomberg Law.[5] The cuts unfolded while [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s [[department-of-government-efficiency|Department of Government Efficiency]] examined the bureau's internal finance and procurement data, and while Musk was developing his X platform into a payments platform.[5] An OMB spokesperson said Vought was "wasting no time getting to the bottom of the waste, fraud, and lavish spending at this rogue and weaponized agency and in bringing it to heel," confirming the cancellation of "hundreds of wasteful and unnecessary contracts worth over $100 million."[5] Badlands commentary framed the episode as one stop in a rapid sequence of agency confrontations — following [[usaid|USAID]] and preceding the Department of Education, IRS, EPA and NED — moving too fast for opponents to organise sustained protest.[5]

## Week-long shutdown and funding cutoff (February 2025)

In February 2025 multiple outlets reported that the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau would be shut down for a week.[6] Office of Management and Budget Director [[russ-vought|Russell Vought]] had been moving to curtail the bureau's activities, announcing in a late-Saturday social media post that he had notified the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] that the CFPB "will not be taking its next draw of unappropriated funding because it is not 'reasonably necessary' to carry out its duties."[6] Vought added that the bureau's current balance of $711.6 million was "in fact excessive in the current fiscal environment," and that "this spigot, long contributing to CFPB's unaccountability, is now being turned off."[6] NBC News reported that Vought also emailed employees a series of directives for the bureau that would take effect immediately.[6]
