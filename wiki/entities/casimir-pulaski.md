# Casimir Pulaski

Count Casimir Pulaski was a Polish cavalry commander who became a general in the Continental Army and is remembered as the "Father of the American Cavalry."[1]

## Recruitment by Franklin

After the Partition of Poland began in 1772, Pulaski led the [[winged-hussars|Hussars]], who were by then without a country and had stopped wearing their iconic wings.[1] [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], who spent most of the period between 1750 and 1780 in Europe engaged in diplomacy, approached Pulaski and convinced him to bring his horsemen to America to fight the British Empire — roughly 90 years after the Hussars had saved Vienna from the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] at the [[battle-of-vienna|Battle of Vienna]].[1]

## Service in the American Revolution

Upon arriving in the colonies in July 1777, Pulaski wrote to [[george-washington|George Washington]]: "I came here, where freedom is being defended, to serve it, and to live or die for it."[1] Washington had little experience employing cavalry as a battlefield set piece, as most of his military background came from the French and Indian War fought in the mountains of the northern colonies and Canada.[1] Pulaski and fellow horsemen such as Hungarian Colonel [[michael-kovats|Michael Kovats]] supplied the expertise needed to counter the British dragoons commanded by [[banastre-tarleton|Banastre Tarleton]].[1]

After a demonstration of riding stunts before Washington and his officers, Pulaski argued for the superiority of cavalry over infantry and was granted an officer's commission as "Commander of Horse" in time for the Battle of Brandywine in September 1777.[1] There he turned the tide as the Continental Army began to yield, averting a disastrous defeat with a cavalry charge that saved Washington's life and earned Pulaski legendary status among the colonists.[1]

## Death and legacy

Pulaski was killed by grapeshot while leading a charge at the Battle of Savannah in 1779.[1] He is venerated as a hero in both America and Poland, and a statue of him stands in Freedom Plaza next to the [[white-house|White House]].[1] Badlands framed the American Revolution as "the last ride of the [Winged] Hussars," a force that effectively became the U.S. Cavalry.[1]
