# Blockchain Voting Companies

**Blockchain Voting Companies** (BVCs) is the collective term used by [[holly-at-altitude|Holly at Altitude]] in the Badlands Media article "Blockchain, Good. Blockchain Voting, Bad." for the for-profit firms developing and marketing [[blockchain-voting|blockchain voting]] systems to United States election officials.[1] The article reports that beta testing programs have started in counties across the U.S. and that BVCs are actively pitching election officials on the benefits of their new systems.[1]

## Product offerings

According to the article, BVCs focus their pitches on the individual voter experience and present step-by-step "blockchain voter user journeys."[1] Their solutions are described as predictably similar regardless of vendor — "a stew of many convoluted steps" comprising smartphone app downloads, QR codes, bar codes, public and private keys, verification steps, relays of encrypted and non-encrypted information, serialized ballots, scanning, tear-off paper ballot receipts, and digital wallets.[1] Whatever the packaging, BVCs promise the same three outcomes: ballots uploaded to a blockchain to create an unchangeable record, the ability for voters to track their own and others' ballots, and assurance that ballots will be counted quickly and accurately.[1]

Sales messaging targets two audiences. Voters are promised elections that are more convenient, secure, transparent, and trackable; election officials, characterised in the article as tech-illiterate, are assured their administrative work will become much easier and more convenient, as well as more cost-effective and efficient.[1] When the technical discussion deepens, advocates emphasise authentication features such as unique QR codes, private-key confirmation, and identity verification through selfie video and photo uploads.[1]

## Criticism: ownership, patents, and governance

The article's central charge is that BVCs reproduce the problems of the incumbent electronic voting vendors.[1] BVCs are said to be unclear about which chain their products use, leaving it unknown whether they are building proprietary blockchains or using open ones.[1] A private chain built by a BVC would place the entire blockchain infrastructure under a single company's control, while a government-built chain would leave elections state-owned but privately run, with little chance of public accountability.[1] If BVCs patent their technology, public and official access to critical systems for evaluation, security, investigation, monitoring, and auditing would be blocked — mirroring the existing situation with private voting equipment vendors.[1]

Even open-sourcing the code and claiming decentralized operation, the article argues, does not resolve the issue: at some point the BVC or the blockchain's creator had full control of the technology, and there is no telling how that genesis affects the system's integrity.[1] Open questions raised include who funded the BVC, who developed the technology, whether partnership or integration deals exist with other companies or the government, and what privacy and security measures the company is responsible for and how these are guaranteed, tested, and verified.[1] On governance, the article notes that BVCs and advocates have not explained a governance plan or reward system for running an assumed decentralized, open chain, raising the question of whether the election system would be monetized — including whether there would be an initial coin offering, who sets initial valuation, how many tokens would be issued, and what the plan is for burning, mining difficulty, and forking.[1]

Because BVCs must in any case build all the apps, interfaces, connections, equipment, APIs, and software needed to make their solutions usable, the article asks whether they will own and control that infrastructure and its data, and how ownership will work for the components integrating with legacy government election equipment and databases.[1]

## Criticism: profitability and corruption risk

Fundamentally, the article states, a BVC is a for-profit company, so whichever firm develops the blockchain and its supporting infrastructure controls and inevitably profits from that work.[1] With money at stake in a multi-billion-dollar elections industry where power, influence, and wealth are in play, BVCs are held to be at risk of corruption, and the author questions whether the mere involvement of blockchain makes them any different from other electronic voting companies.[1]

Implementation would also require extensive integration with government, existing equipment and processes, and other private companies, meaning BVCs would inevitably work with the electronic voting companies that created and control what the article calls a corrupted, broken system — so blockchain voting "starts off corrupted to some degree."[1] Even with a proliferation of BVCs competing for business over time, the article concludes there would never be enough companies to decentralize control over the entire election system.[1]

## Technical shortcomings attributed to BVCs

The article holds that BVCs have misdiagnosed the problem, since any blockchain component must integrate into an already-broken system and cures none of its systemic failings.[1] It notes that BVCs fail to address persistent election integrity issues arising from state-managed centralized voter databases, universal [[mail-in-voting|mail-in voting]], and electronic voting and counting.[1] Current BVC solutions are said to provide no plan or process guaranteeing that ballots are properly and accurately counted — a separate third-party counting system would be required — or that post-election auditing and ballot reconciliation satisfy the varying laws of each locality and state, which change annually or more frequently.[1]
