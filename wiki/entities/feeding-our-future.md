# Feeding Our Future

Feeding Our Future is a prominent pandemic-era child nutrition scheme in Minnesota, described as a $250 million fraud and treated as a central component of the broader Minnesota Somali welfare fraud scandal.[1]

## Role in the fraud scandal

Prosecutors and investigators estimated that up to half of $18–20 billion in federally funded welfare programs over recent years was lost to fraud in Minnesota, primarily through Medicaid — where 86% of Somalis in the state reportedly receive free healthcare — along with food stamps, housing, childcare and Feeding Our Future.[1] The scheme became a focal point of investigations led by House Oversight Chairman [[james-comer|James Comer]], Senate Homeland Security Chairman [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] and House Majority Whip [[tom-emmer|Tom Emmer]], who described the fraud as a "coordinated effort" to manipulate elections, congressional apportionment and federal funding in blue states.[1]

Deputy Chief of Staff [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] called the broader Somali scandal "one of the greatest financial scandals in American history," and the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] backed the [[scam-act|SCAM Act]] to expand denaturalization powers in response.[1]

## Prosecutions and convictions

Feeding Our Future remains the largest COVID-era fraud scheme prosecuted to date, with at least 78 defendants indicted and Somali-specific indictments filed as recently as November 2025.[2] [[aimee-bock|Aimee Bock]], described by prosecutors as the scheme's mastermind, and co-defendant [[salim-said|Salim Said]] were convicted in March 2025 in connection with the $250 million fraud.[2] Federal prosecutors allege that stolen US government funds — nominally distributed to service an alleged "food desert" — financed luxury beachfront accommodations, high-end vehicles including a Rolls-Royce and a Lamborghini, travel and Minnesota real estate.[2] Prosecutions remain ongoing, and Badlands emphasized that this COVID-era public-benefit fraud predates the separate Somali-linked childcare scandal that later went viral on social media.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the response should be to "follow the money and prosecute the Americans," pointing to Bock's conviction and to the "white American karens" surrounding the Somali figures featured in viral clips, whom she said should be questioned at a minimum; she summarized her position as "Deport the Somalis. Arrest the Americans."[2] [[canncon|CannCon]] noted that many of the "at home childcare" operations under scrutiny were located in subsidized housing, meaning operators paid neither rent nor mortgage, and called for a tax revolt if the practice were not stopped.[2] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] accepted the fraud as real but argued the timing was the tell, saying "a coordinated messaging op is happening right now to tell the country about this type of fraud" and that "the time at which these stories are reported has to be part of the consideration of the story."[2]

## Reference point for the April 2026 Minneapolis raids

The Feeding Our Future case was invoked as the anchor precedent when [[fbi|FBI]] and [[homeland-security-investigations|Homeland Security Investigations]] agents raided more than twenty Minneapolis-area locations on April 28, 2026, primarily targeting Somali-linked businesses in a probe of "rampant fraud of U.S. taxpayers dollars."[3] Coverage described Feeding Our Future as the massive scandal that siphoned over $250 million in federal child nutrition funds, part of a broader Minnesota pattern also encompassing autism services, housing assistance, Medicaid and SNAP schemes.[3] Since January, authorities had issued more than 1,750 subpoenas and 130 warrants in these cases, with a key raid target being the [[quality-learning-center|Quality Learning Center]]; officials called the raids "the tip of a very large iceberg."[3]

## Threats of racism litigation

Feeding Our Future was the largest provider in the pandemic child-nutrition program at the center of the [[minnesota-somali-fraud-scandal|Minnesota Somali fraud scandal]].[4] When state officials began scrutinizing new applications, the nonprofit responded with a warning: in an email it told the state agency that failing to promptly approve new applicants from "minority-owned businesses" would result in a lawsuit featuring accusations of racism that would be "sprawled across the news."[4] That episode was cited in later reporting as an illustration of how accusations of racism deterred regulators from confronting fraud that ultimately exceeded $1 billion across three investigated plots, with 59 people convicted.[4]
