# Millennium Challenge Corporation

The **Millennium Challenge Corporation** (MCC) is a bilateral United States foreign aid agency established by the U.S. Congress in 2004, formally independent of the [[state-department|State Department]] and [[usaid|USAID]].[1] It provides grants to countries judged to have sound economic policies and growth potential, using a qualification process based on third-party scores across twenty different areas; an eligible country must apply for a grant tied to a specific project.[1]

## Moldova compact

On January 22, 2010, MCC chief executive Daniel Yohannes signed his first compact, a five-year, $262 million agreement with the Republic of [[moldova|Moldova]].[1] The signing ceremony was presided over by U.S. Secretary of State [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] and Moldovan Prime Minister [[vlad-filat|Vladimir Filat]].[1] Yohannes praised Moldova's "commitment to poverty reduction and economic development opportunities," and the compact was described as adding "an additional layer of improved access to credit and technical assistance."[1]

The Moldova compact included what analysts at the Center for Global Development described as an "innovative financing facility" — a step beyond earlier financial-access components in the Madagascar, Benin and Morocco compacts — intended to support investment by farmers and entrepreneurs in higher-value agriculture, post-harvest processing, storage and marketing.[1] That package was co-financed by USAID.[1] Moldova had previously received a $24.7 million MCC Threshold program focused on anti-corruption: strengthening the judiciary, increasing monitoring capacity of civil society and the media, and curbing corruption in tax, customs and police services.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands writer Joe Lange argues that the MCC's nominal independence is illusory — that "there is no such thing as an 'independent' agency within our government" — and that the Moldova compact functioned less as poverty relief than as the scaffolding for a money-laundering conduit.[1] He notes that the billion-dollar theft from Moldova's banking system occurred in 2012–2014, roughly two years after the compact and its USAID-co-financed financing facility were established, and that the anti-corruption Threshold money coincided with, rather than prevented, the capture of the Moldovan state.[1] In this reading, the taxpayer grants "were never designed to relieve poverty" but instead "set the table for a massive money laundering operation."[1] These are the author's inferences and are not established fact.[1]
