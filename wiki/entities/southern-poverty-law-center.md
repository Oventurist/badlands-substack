# Southern Poverty Law Center

The Southern Poverty Law Center (SPLC) is an American nonprofit legal advocacy organization best known for its designations of "hate groups" and "extremists," designations that critics in independent media have long argued function as political weapons rather than neutral research. In the reporting covered by [[the-blitz|The Blitz]], the SPLC became the subject of what was described as a "massive indictment," a development treated by Badlands hosts as one of the leading stories of the week and a marker of what they termed the dawning age of American accountability.[1]

## Indictment coverage

Episode 14 of the Badlands Blitz opened its weekly recap with the SPLC indictment, framing it as the first of a series of "white pills" — favorable developments — for the audience.[1] The hosts, [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] and [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], connected the SPLC story to the broader thread of the week, treating the legal action against the organization as continuous with the parallel unraveling of the [[russiagate-hoax|Russiagate Hoax]] rather than as an isolated event.[1]

## Significance in Badlands coverage

Within the Badlands editorial frame, the SPLC has functioned as an institutional node of the censorship and lawfare apparatus, and its indictment is presented as evidence that accountability is beginning to reach institutions previously considered untouchable.[1] The organization's treatment in the Blitz recap is characteristic of the show's format, in which the week's Badlands Brief reporting is dissected and placed in a larger narrative of institutional unraveling.[1]

## Superseding indictment (June 2026)

On Tuesday, June 2, 2026, the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] said it had secured a superseding indictment against the SPLC, adding new allegations about how the organization's tax-exempt donations were used to fund informants inside extremist groups.[2] A grand jury in the Middle District of Alabama alleges the group used roughly $4.1 million to pay individuals embedded in hate organizations, who prosecutors say then took part in activities including recruiting members and obtaining materials linked to cross burnings and Ku Klux Klan regalia.[2]

The updated indictment reportedly removes language referring to "false or misleading" statements to banks, following a Supreme Court ruling in *Thompson v. United States* clarifying that the relevant statute applies only to false statements.[2] The SPLC faces 11 counts of wire fraud, bank fraud and conspiracy to commit money laundering, with prosecutors alleging it defrauded donors and banks by routing funds through shell accounts to individuals inside the groups it publicly opposed.[2] The organization has pleaded not guilty and moved to dismiss the case, calling it a vindictive prosecution.[2]

## Cross-burning allegations and commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] linked the cross-burning element of the indictment to the 2023 Colorado Springs case in which a cross was burned in front of a campaign sign for [[yemi-mobolade|Yemi Mobolade]], who went on to win the mayoralty of one of Colorado's reddest cities; she wrote, "Now I wonder if the SPLC was involved."[2] She argued that the narrative was so important that "when supply for racism outweighed demand, racism had to be manufactured," and said she and her collaborators are tracking the SPLC connections of the people and entities that fabricated claims under the [[ku-klux-klan-act|KKK Act]] against them in 2022.[2] Contending that abusing the legal process for narrative gain should be a crime, she proposed reviving the medieval common-law offense of [[barratry|barratry]], "beginning with the SPLC and their collaborators."[2]

### The "SPLC model" of activist NGOs (2026)

In May 2026 [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] used the SPLC as the template for a class of organizations, writing that [[codepink|CodePink]] "has long operated in the same ecosystem of activist NGOs that parallel the SPLC model — leveraging humanitarian framing and narrative positioning to advance anti-American rhetoric while maintaining operational ties that are now facing scrutiny."[3] He argued that the "anti-SPLC action could mark the beginnings of the dismantling of institutional proxies that manufactured conflict and laundered narrative power through the administrative state," and paired it with the [[office-of-foreign-assets-control|OFAC]] probe of the [[nuestra-america-convoy|Nuestra America Convoy]] as parallel fronts of the same campaign.[3]

## Donor pullback (2026)

In coverage dated May 1, 2026, investment giants [[fidelity-investments|Fidelity Investments]] and [[vanguard-group|Vanguard Group]] were reported to have paused charitable contributions to the SPLC after a federal indictment involving an individual allegedly connected to the organization.[4] The move was described as signaling reputational-risk concerns among major financial institutions amid rising scrutiny of where corporate philanthropy is directed.[4] Although the SPLC itself had not been charged as an organization, the association prompted a reassessment by donors wary of legal and public-relations fallout, with broader implications for asset managers under pressure to justify both political neutrality and charitable affiliations.[4]

## April 2026 indictment details

The [[department-of-justice|U.S. Justice Department]] indicted the SPLC on an 11-count federal indictment in Montgomery, Alabama, charging wire fraud, bank fraud, and conspiracy to commit money laundering.[5] Prosecutors allege the organization defrauded donors by secretly funneling more than $3 million between 2014 and 2023 to paid informants and leaders inside the Ku Klux Klan, neo-Nazi groups, and other extremist organizations it publicly claimed to monitor and oppose.[5] Acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] said the SPLC "was not dismantling these groups. It was instead manufacturing the extremism it purports to oppose by paying sources to stoke racial hatred."[5]

The paid informant program reportedly ran for years, with roots in the 1980s, before being discontinued, and some intelligence gathered was shared with law enforcement.[5] SPLC chief executive [[bryan-fair|Bryan Fair]] confirmed the probe and indictment, describing it as centering on the organization's past use of confidential informants, and vowed that the SPLC would not be "intimidated."[5]

## NGO accountability commentary (April 2026)

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the SPLC as a tax-exempt 501(c)(3) nonprofit and "an excellent example of unaccountable NGOs wielding unchecked influence across the Republic," arguing that in confirming the informant program the organization had effectively admitted the charged conduct, leaving its lawfulness ripe for judicial review.[5] She contended that leftist NGOs act as self-appointed social monitors and "mini attorneys general," policing First Amendment–protected belief and advocacy of political opponents, with "election denialism" by supporters of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] among the SPLC's favorite targets.[5]

She cited the N.Y.U. Review of Law & Social Change article "Voters Strike Back: Litigating Against Modern Voter Intimidation" as a framework for using the judicial system against First Amendment protections, and identified it as the playbook behind the case brought against her and her codefendants.[5] Characterizing the SPLC as "an institutional Jussie Smollet at scale," she noted that the supply of extremism could not meet demand and so was fabricated, remarked on the [[naacp|NAACP]] defending the SPLC even as the SPLC funded the Klan, and referenced claims that the SPLC was behind the "Unite the Right" rally.[5]

## Beirich and the "F-9" informant (June 2026 reporting)

Later reporting on the June 2, 2026 superseding indictment detailed the charges as wire fraud, bank fraud, false statements to a federally insured bank, and conspiracy to commit money laundering.[6] Prosecutors allege that former SPLC Intelligence Project director [[heidi-beirich|Heidi Beirich]], identified in court documents as "Employee-2," directed more than $1.2 million in donor funds over roughly two decades to an informant identified only as "F-9," who was embedded in the neo-Nazi [[national-alliance|National Alliance]].[6] Beirich and F-9 were said to be in a romantic relationship, sharing a residence and joint bank accounts into which approximately $140,000 of SPLC donor money was deposited between 2015 and 2021 for personal expenses.[6] While receiving SPLC payments, F-9 allegedly continued fundraising for and supporting the National Alliance's activities.[6]

The indictment further claims the SPLC paid another informant, Randolph Dilloway, $6,000 to falsely take responsibility for a 2014 burglary at the National Alliance's West Virginia headquarters.[6] Prosecutors contend the organization engaged in a pattern of financially supporting actual extremist groups while publicly denouncing them and designating numerous conservative organizations as "hate groups."[6]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] remarked that calling the 58-year-old Beirich a "fascism expert" was "a bit of a stretch," since "her expertise is based on intimate knowledge of the inner workings of fascism."[6] She called for the entire network to be rolled up rather than ceremonial arrests, argued that NGOs frequently operate as unaccountable arms of government with direct decision rights over citizens, said the SPLC "destroyed lives" through its hate-group list and was cited as an authority by government actors targeting political opponents, and asked where journalists were while the organization allegedly fabricated reality and laundered money.[6]

## Federal fraud prosecution (2026)

The Southern Poverty Law Center entered a not guilty plea in July 2026 to federal fraud charges during a remote arraignment in Montgomery, Alabama, following a superseding indictment that added detail but no new charges to the original case.[7] The [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] alleges the nonprofit misled donors by failing to disclose that millions of dollars in donations were used to pay confidential informants embedded in extremist organizations between 2014 and 2023, including reimbursements for activities tied to those groups.[7] Federal prosecutors contend the conduct constituted wire fraud, false statements to federally insured banks, and conspiracy to commit money laundering, and the case was scheduled for trial in October.[7]

The SPLC denies wrongdoing, arguing that the paid informant program generated intelligence regularly shared with law enforcement and helped prevent violence, and has characterized the prosecution as politically motivated.[7] Badlands commentators questioned whether the organization had effectively functioned as an extrajudicial entrapment operation with less oversight than government handlers, and whether its informants generated intelligence or manufactured the very racism and white-supremacy narratives the group reported.[7] See [[splc-federal-fraud-prosecution|SPLC federal fraud prosecution]].[7]

## Florida civil investigation (May 2026)

On May 5, 2026, Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier announced a civil investigation into the SPLC and issued a subpoena demanding extensive records by May 25, 2026, targeting the organization's charitable fundraising practices and allegations of deceptive and unfair conduct.[8] Uthmeier said the group "appears to be running a deceptive organization that pays informants to manufacture racism on its behalf."[8] The subpoena sought disclosures to Florida donors about informant use, marketing and fundraising materials and their timelines, names and aliases used in solicitations, websites used to solicit donations, annual Florida donation totals, disbursements tied to informants, internal policies governing informant payments, and the share of the SPLC budget allocated to such programs.[8] See [[florida-civil-probe-into-the-splc|the Florida civil probe into the SPLC]].[8]


## Badlands critique of the SPLC's definition of hate

Commenting on a June 2026 congressional hearing involving the SPLC, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that it had become "pretty clear that the core of what the SPLC calls hate is actually intolerance of sin."[9] In her account, abortion, LGBTQ advancements, and resurgent religiosity — categories she equated respectively with murder, sexual immorality, and religions concerned with sin — are what the organization treats as hate, and that to stop "hate" it engaged in lying, subversion, coercion and manipulation while positioning itself as an arbiter of morality in society.[9] She added that the SPLC had been indicted and, "if justice exists, will soon face accountability."[9]
