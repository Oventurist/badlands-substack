# W.A.C. Bennett

**W.A.C. Bennett** was Premier of [[british-columbia|British Columbia]] from 1952 to 1972 and is described by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as the province's "great Premier," who fought a hard battle to ensure that British Columbia would become more than the colonial, underdeveloped region its British rulers had long condemned it to be.[1] Bennett was convinced that the opening of the Arctic and an industrial boom in British Columbia were possible if state-directed investment into capital-intensive infrastructure, social programs and industrialization were made the focus of the national purpose.[1]

## Clashes with Ottawa

Bennett's clashes with the technocrats in Ottawa began with the sabotage operation against the [[columbia-river-treaty|Columbia River Treaty]] and continued beyond the ouster of Prime Minister John Diefenbaker in 1963.[1] These conflicts instilled in Bennett a resolve to use the vast powers of the province over its own economy: he nationalized BC Power Corporation and created new BC-run institutions such as the Bank of British Columbia to execute his grand design.[1] His struggle to develop the province was also made possible through his association with [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]], who sided with Bennett over Ottawa's civil service on the Columbia River Treaty.[1]

## Rail expansion and the northern vision

Using revenues generated from the Columbia River Treaty, Bennett invested in a vast rail expansion process that continued through the 1960s, opening development corridors in previously inhospitable regions of the great north.[1] Although these projects began on the basis of fossil fuel, lumber and other raw material exploitation, manufacturing industries soon began to appear, along with permanent cities in the Peace River region.[1] The northernmost rail line, extending as far as Dease Lake, British Columbia, was built more than fifty years ago under Bennett and carried a nominal price tag of one dollar; for decades it was derided as "the railway to nowhere," which Ehret calls a relic of a sabotaged northern vision that was always designed to connect Canada and the USA while opening the north for development.[1]

Alaska–British Columbia–Yukon conferences through this period were instrumental in advancing Bennett's vision for an eventual connection between British Columbia's rail system and that of Alaska.[1] Due to the sabotage of the rail system by Bennett's son, Premier Bill Bennett, the great Canada-Alaska rail connection became forever known as the "rail to nowhere," ending at Dease Lake.[1] Bennett astutely observed Canada's destiny with Asia when he said in 1971, "there are great mountains separating B.C. from Ottawa, but between us and Japan, there's only the Peaceful Sea (Pacific Ocean)."[1]

## Legacy

Ehret presents the completion of Bennett's rail vision in the twenty-first century as a necessary component of the Bering Strait rail corridor program proposed by the governments of Russia and China, and argues that the unfulfilled dreams of visionaries such as Bennett and [[john-diefenbaker|John Diefenbaker]] could finally blossom through the [[alaska-canada-railway|Alaska-Canada Railway]].[1]

## Spiritual father of NAWAPA

Matthew Ehret describes the water management projects built in Canada under Bennett's leadership as British Columbia premier as having set the stage for the [[north-american-water-and-power-alliance|North American Water and Power Alliance]] itself, a case he made in a 2012 historical study titled "W.A.C. Bennett: Canada's Spiritual Father to NAWAPA."[3] The Peace River dam in northern British Columbia, built during that era, was to supply the 19 million acre-feet per year that NAWAPA's second circuit would carry across the Canadian prairies into Lake Superior.[3]

## Restatement ahead of the 2025 Alaska summit

Writing ahead of the [[2025-trump-putin-alaska-summit|2025 Trump–Putin Alaska summit]], [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] again described Bennett as British Columbia's great Premier from 1952 to 1972, whose state-directed investment programme aimed to end the province's colonial underdevelopment.[2] He reiterated that Bennett's clashes with Ottawa technocrats over the [[columbia-river-treaty|Columbia River Treaty]] and the 1963 ouster of [[john-diefenbaker|John Diefenbaker]] drove Bennett to nationalize BC Power Corporation and found the Bank of British Columbia, and that [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] sided with Bennett against Ottawa's civil service.[2] Ehret listed Bennett with Kennedy and Diefenbaker among the statesmen whose pro-industrial growth visions were displaced after the 1971 floating of the US dollar by a 45-year slide into consumerism, speculation and zero-technological growth.[2]
