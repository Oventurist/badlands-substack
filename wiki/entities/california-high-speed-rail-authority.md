# California High-Speed Rail Authority

The California High-Speed Rail Authority is the state agency responsible for building the country's costliest infrastructure project, a bullet train intended to connect San Francisco to Los Angeles.[1]

## Federal funding clawback and litigation

In July 2025 Transportation Secretary [[sean-duffy|Sean Duffy]] announced that his agency would claw back $4 billion in federal funds previously awarded to the project, after a compliance review determined that the Authority "simply cannot meet its obligations under the grant agreement."[1] The funds had originally been awarded by the [[federal-railroad-administration|Federal Railroad Administration]] during the Obama and Biden administrations.[1] Duffy also cited the state's failure to identify a sustainable source of funds to cover the full cost of the San Francisco–Los Angeles connection, estimated to run as high as $135 billion.[1]

Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]] responded by announcing that California would sue the Trump administration, with his office alleging that the cancellation of the agreements was "political retribution, motivated by President [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s animus toward California and the high-speed rail project, not by facts on the ground."[1] Newsom called the clawback "a heartless attack on the Central Valley that will put real jobs and livelihoods on the line," and said the state was suing to stop Trump "from derailing America's only high-speed rail actively under construction."[1]

## Construction status and cost history

Construction work is underway on a 119-mile section in California's Central Valley, between Fresno and Bakersfield.[1] New chief executive [[ian-choudri|Ian Choudri]], a rail and infrastructure veteran, is developing a business plan intended to lower costs, accelerate construction and add private sector partners.[1]

Badlands commentary noted that the project was approved in 1982 with a proposal of $33 billion and revenue-generating trains promised by 2008, but that service is now projected only for 2030 at a cost of $128–135 billion — presented as an indictment of government-run infrastructure.[1] The commentary argued that the answer was not merely to cut funding but to investigate where the money went on "the trains to nowhere."[1]
