# Office of Management and Budget

The Office of Management and Budget (OMB) is the White House budget office responsible for preparing the president's budget, overseeing agency spending plans, and coordinating executive branch contingency planning for lapses in appropriations.[1] Under the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] it has been directed by [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]].[1]

## September 2025 shutdown firing directive

In September 2025, OMB instructed federal agencies to prepare reduction-in-force plans for mass firings in the event of a government shutdown, focusing on employees attached to programs that are not legally required to continue operating.[1] The directive, contained in a memo shared with Politico before its distribution to agencies, told agencies to identify programs, projects and activities where discretionary funding would lapse on October 1 with no alternative funding available, and to draft plans that would go beyond standard furloughs to permanently eliminate jobs in programs not consistent with President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s priorities.[1]

The instruction escalated the stakes of the funding standoff with Democrats in [[congress|Congress]] and broke with decades of practice in which shutdown furloughs were temporary and workers returned once funding was restored.[1] OMB wrote that "programs that did not benefit from an infusion of mandatory appropriations will bear the brunt of a shutdown."[1] The [[white-house|White House]] posture was welcomed by Badlands contributor Jordan Sather, who wrote simply, "SEND IT."[1]

## October 2025 shutdown role

During the shutdown that began October 1, 2025, OMB under director [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]] became the focal point of both Democratic alarm and Republican unease.[2] Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] said Democrats should have expected that Vought's OMB "could broadly target government workers and programs in a shutdown," describing the lapse as "handing the keys to Russ Vought."[2] Republican members objected to specific OMB actions, including holds on major transportation projects in New York and the prospect of mass layoffs.[2]

An OMB memo also confirmed that construction of the 90,000-square-foot [[white-house-ballroom|White House ballroom]] would proceed during the funding lapse, since the project was financed by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and other private donors and its funds were "not tied to a FY26 enacted appropriation."[2]

## Race and ethnicity standards

On March 28, 2024, OMB announced revisions to the federal minimum categories on race and ethnicity — the [[2024-federal-race-and-ethnicity-standards-revision|first such revision in 27 years]] — combining previously separate race and ethnicity questions into a single question and adding a Middle Eastern and North African category.[3] The revisions also struck the terms "majority" and "minority" from the standards.[3] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the move as the "change management" arm of a broader narrative strategy, arguing it supports societal segmentation and creates new grievance categories, and questioned whether OMB had "just opened a massive can of worms" by removing a term embedded in decades of discrimination case law.[3]
