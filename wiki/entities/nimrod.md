# Nimrod

Nimrod, the great-grandson of Noah, was a king of ancient Babylon described in the texts of all three major religions as well as by Greek and Roman historians as a wicked ruler who claimed to be God, promoted idolatry through polytheism and blood magic, and constructed the Tower of Babel.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights)

## Conflict with Abraham

In the Quran, Nimrod is written as having set himself against the will of God.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights) Warned by his astrologers that a baby — also a descendant of Noah — would be born and bring an end to idolatry, he ordered the slaughter of all newborns in Mesopotamia; the child was [[abraham|Abraham]], whose mother escaped into the wilderness to give birth.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights)

When Abraham later confronted him to end idolatry, Nimrod ordered him burned at the stake, fire being one of the main focuses of Nimrod's godless worship.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights) Abraham emerged unscathed and witnesses began to regard him as a prophet.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights) Fearing for his rule, Nimrod challenged Abraham to a public debate, staging an execution and a pardon to claim that he too was a giver of life and death — only to be confounded by Abraham's demand that he make the sun rise from the West.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights) Humiliated, Nimrod banished Abraham from his Mesopotamian kingdom.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights)

## Significance

Nimrod stands in the article as the archetype of self-indulgent narcissism and occult idolatry against which Abrahamic monotheism defined itself — the Good versus Evil framing the author treats as a recurring theme of Islamic history and tradition, and as a precursor to the idolatry and "ancient occult magic" that [[muhammad-ibn-abd-al-wahhab|Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab]] would later denounce in 18th-century Arabia.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights) Bedouin oral accounts of Babylonian blood magic and its gods, preserved outside any written record, entered the Quran and were later corroborated by 20th-century archaeology.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/arabian-nights)
