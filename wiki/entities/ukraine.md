# Ukraine

Ukraine is presented in the article as a textbook example of labor unions used as geopolitical pawns.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union) The 2014 Maidan uprising saw the [[solidarity-center|Solidarity Center]], through [[national-endowment-for-democracy|NED]] grants exceeding $500,000, support pro-Western labor groups that amplified strikes and protests against the Yanukovych government.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union)

These actions dovetailed with [[state-department|State Department]] and [[pentagon|Department of Defense]] objectives to pull Ukraine into NATO's orbit, with Ukrainian unions trained in "nonviolent resistance" tactics derived from Gene Sharp's manuals becoming foot soldiers in what the article calls a U.S.-orchestrated regime change operation.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union) After Maidan, Solidarity Center funding continued in order to reshape Ukraine's labor laws in line with EU standards — a process the article says was less about worker protections than about integrating Ukraine into Western economic structures.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union)

Leaked and declassified State Department cables from the 2010s are cited as showing that union organizers in Ukraine, as in Venezuela, were tasked with reporting on strikes, protests and opposition leaders via secure apps linked to U.S. embassies.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union)


## As a case study in narrative management

Badlands Media used Ukraine as its principal comparison for Western coverage of the fall of [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al-Assad]].[2] It argued the conflict could not have happened without a spigot of U.S. tax dollars, that it was on the verge of resolution in April 2022 before being thwarted via Boris Johnson, and that public support depended on shaping consensus perception with images of bombing and the claim that the invasion was unprovoked and Vladimir Putin the "new Hitler."[2]

The article listed as omitted context that the [[state-department|State Department]] and its NGO blob orchestrated a color revolution and regime change in 2014, that the teaching of Russian language and mythology was prohibited, that large portions of the country prefer rule from Moscow, and that hundreds of thousands of fighting-age men were slaughtered — outcomes it says could have been prevented but for interference by [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and Boris Johnson.[2] It also invoked the "Ghost of Kyiv" and the Azov Battalion as earlier examples of Western audiences cheering manufactured heroes.[2]

## Defense exports and the war economy (2026)

By 2026 President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelenskyy]] was positioning Ukraine as a global exporter of combat-tested military technology, particularly drones and robotic systems refined in fighting against Russia, pitching them to foreign buyers as a defense export strategy.[3] Commentary paired this with the [[pentagon|Pentagon]]'s outreach to automakers and manufacturers as evidence of an emerging [[war-economy|war economy]] in which frontline states monetize battlefield innovation while Western nations retool industrial capacity for long-term competition.[3] Supply shortages earlier in the Russia–Ukraine war, where limited stockpiles and slow production exposed weaknesses in Western readiness, were cited as the impetus for scalable, distributed manufacturing.[3]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that Ukraine's rapid pivot from a supposed human meat grinder to autonomous ground platforms, drone swarms, and unmanned vehicles suggested the war served as narrative cover for Ukraine and Russia alike to fast-track war economies, sovereign supply chains, and leaps in autonomy that peacetime bureaucracy would have delayed for decades.[3]

## 2026 strike on Türkiye-linked gas infrastructure

In July 2026, [[gazprom|Gazprom]] said Ukrainian drones targeted the Krasnodarskaya compressor station serving the [[blue-stream-pipeline|Blue Stream]] pipeline, which carries Russian gas to [[turkey|Türkiye]] across the Black Sea; the company reported minor damage to a building but no interruption of deliveries.[4] Ukraine had not publicly commented on the strike at the time of reporting, while Kremlin spokesman [[dmitry-peskov|Dmitry Peskov]] condemned it as a threat to critical international energy infrastructure.[4] The strike came as Ukraine expanded long-range attacks on Russian energy infrastructure and [[russia|Russia]] continued missile and drone attacks on Ukrainian cities and energy facilities.[4]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued the attack was intended to disrupt gas flows to Turkey and was carried out on behalf of [[israel|Israel]], anticipating that Ukraine and Turkey might enter open conflict with [[greece|Greece]] potentially coming to Ukraine's aid.[4]


## June 2026: western operations and cartel allegations
[[russia|Russia]] claimed in June 2026 that it was expanding military operations in western Ukraine and accused [[nato|NATO]] of supporting Ukrainian strikes on Russian airfields.[5] Russian officials also alleged links between Ukrainian forces and Mexican drug cartels.[5] Badlands commentary treated these claims, together with [[germany|Germany]]'s conscription plans and [[poland|Poland]]'s border measures, as a constellation of implausible headlines while still concluding that Eastern Europe had become a powder keg because of the war.[5]

## Government reshuffle, July 2026
Ukraine's parliament voted 289–22 on July 16, 2026 to confirm [[sergii-koretskyi|Sergii Koretskyi]], former chief executive of [[naftogaz|Naftogaz]], as prime minister, replacing [[yulia-svyrydenko|Yulia Svyrydenko]] as President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] reshaped his wartime government ahead of another winter of Russian attacks.[6] The reshuffle also included planned changes at the Defense Ministry and other senior posts, and drew criticism from opposition lawmakers who questioned replacing officials without broader structural reforms.[6] Reports circulated by RT and some Ukrainian critics linked businessman [[timur-mindich|Timur Mindich]] to members of the incoming government, though such allegations were not substantiated by evidence presented by the Ukrainian government or by Reuters' reporting.[6]

## Escalation and drone operations (2026)

On May 22, 2026, a Ukrainian drone struck a college dormitory in Starobelsk in Russia's Lugansk People's Republic, reportedly killing 21 people, mostly teenage girls, and injuring 70 — the [[starobelsk-dormitory-attack|Starobelsk dormitory attack]] that triggered a large-scale Russian retaliatory missile and drone campaign.[7] A separate fiber-optic-guided drone struck the machine hall of the sixth power unit at the [[zaporozhye-nuclear-power-plant|Zaporozhye Nuclear Power Plant]]; [[rosatom|Rosatom]] described it as Kiev's first deliberate attack on the plant's main equipment, while Ukrainian authorities denied involvement.[7]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] characterized Ukrainian leadership as "increasingly unhinged," pointing to the nuclear plant strike, an armed drone that reached Finland, a drone incident in Romania attributed by Moscow to a Western or Ukrainian false flag, and reporting that a film-scouting company linked to President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]]'s friends had become the country's principal long-range drone manufacturer.[7]

## July 2026: new army chief, withheld aid, and Black Sea strikes

Ukraine's newly appointed commander-in-chief, Lt. Gen. [[mykhailo-drapatyi|Mykhailo Drapatyi]], declared in July 2026 that "Russia has no right to exist" while outlining his vision for the country's military.[8] The remark coincided with Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]]'s meeting with Russian Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] in Manila, where Rubio said Washington remained prepared to play a "constructive role" in ending the war while warning that no quick settlement was available.[8] Kyiv has reiterated that any peace agreement must preserve its sovereignty and territorial integrity.[8]

The [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] withheld portions of previously planned military assistance pending a review of US weapons stockpiles, and President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] separately withheld $400 million promised by [[congress|Congress]] to President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]], saying the funds would not be available "until the next president is in office."[8] Badlands commentary tied this to allegations that Congress has used Ukraine to launder US tax dollars and called for an Inspector General report from [[robert-storch|Robert Storch]].[8]

A Russian-appointed governor in Kherson said Ukrainian drone strikes targeted 13 Turkish civilian vessels in the Black Sea, killing one Turkish sailor and wounding three others; Turkish authorities did not publicly confirm the casualties and the circumstances remained disputed.[8] Russia condemned the strikes as attacks on civilian shipping while [[turkey|Turkey]] announced no military response, and Badlands commentary raised the question of whether [[article-5|Article 5]] might be invoked over an attack on a NATO member.[8] The commentary also described the deployment of [[laura-loomer|Laura Loomer]] to Ukraine for a pro-war publicity campaign as part of an effort to widen the conflict.[8]

## Juncker on corruption and EU accession (2023)

In October 2023, former [[european-commission|European Commission]] President [[jean-claude-juncker|Jean-Claude Juncker]] slammed the possibility of Ukraine joining the [[european-union|European Union]], calling the country "corrupt at all levels of society" and saying that "despite its efforts, it is not ready for accession; it needs massive internal reform processes."[9] He warned that "making false promises" to Ukrainians about accession "would be neither good for the EU nor for Ukraine," and said he was "very angry about some voices in Europe who are telling Ukrainians that they can become members immediately."[9] The remarks came while Kyiv was fending off [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]]'s full-scale invasion, begun in February 2022.[9]

## Prisoner mobilisation law (May 2024)

On 8 May 2024 Ukraine's parliament, the [[verkhovna-rada|Verkhovna Rada]], passed a bill enabling certain categories of prisoners to serve in the armed forces as the country faced a critical shortage of manpower on the front lines.[10] Kyiv had long opposed the measure and had criticised Moscow's own mobilisation of prisoners, but reversed course amid fresh Russian advances; mobilisation under the bill would be voluntary and limited, and required signature by the Rada's chairperson and President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] to take effect.[10]

Badlands commentary was scathing, questioning the meaning of "voluntary" for an incarcerated population, noting that the exclusions for serious corruption were being applied in a country that had fired the prosecutor investigating the US vice president's son, and observing that only prisoners who had killed fewer than two people were eligible.[10] The commentary further charged that the United States was standing with a country that persecutes religion, gives aid and comfort to actual Nazis, and cancelled its elections, and characterised the war effort as a "blue and yellow money laundering operation."[10]

## Lukashenko warning on Ukrainian statehood (October 2025)

In October 2025 Belarusian President [[alexander-lukashenko|Alexander Lukashenko]] warned that Ukraine could cease to exist as a state if no diplomatic settlement to the conflict with [[russia|Russia]] were reached and Moscow's forces continued to advance.[11] Speaking to the Russian journalist Pavel Zarubin, Lukashenko said meaningful negotiations must begin "urgently," adding that "Russia is moving forward on the front line… and this could result in the disappearance of Ukraine as a state."[11] Earlier that month President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] had said Russian forces were advancing almost everywhere along the front line, claiming that since the start of the year they had taken almost 5,000 square kilometres of territory and more than 210 settlements previously held by Ukrainian troops.[11]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] responded that Ukraine has historically hardly ever been a country, with different portions belonging to a number of other nations and empires and alternating moments of sovereignty and subjugation, and suggested that whatever remains at the end of the war should be called "The Ukraine."[11]

## Reported training of Latin American mercenaries

A 2025 report raised the question of whether Ukraine had been training drug-cartel-linked mercenaries from Latin America, noting accounts of individuals with criminal connections coming to Ukraine to gain experience with drones and advanced tactics they hoped to bring back to their criminal bosses.[11] Many foreigners, including Latin Americans, have fought in Ukraine — some for pay, others for the cause of Ukrainian independence — and an October 2024 CEPA article recorded that some Colombians left after facing intense combat.[11] The same article quoted a Colombian fighter who said cartels in [[colombia|Colombia]] had already drawn lessons from the war and were using first-person-view (FPV) drones to strike at the army and police.[11]

## Erosion of European arms support (2023)

In October 2023 the new government of [[slovakia|Slovakia]] under Prime Minister [[robert-fico|Robert Fico]] followed through on a campaign pledge to terminate arms transfers to Ukraine and urged the rest of Europe to adopt the same policy.[12] Fico told Slovak legislators that Bratislava was abandoning the West's proxy war against Russia, said he would "support zero military aid to Ukraine," and argued that "an immediate halt to military operations is the best solution we have for Ukraine" with the [[european-union|EU]] shifting "from an arms supplier to a peacemaker."[12] He also declined to back further sanctions on Moscow absent impact analyses for Slovakia and said "further killing will not help anyone."[12]

Badlands commentary in the same period tied the war to American domestic politics, noting that the generation most inclined to support arms and funding for Ukraine — and to accept the argument that sacrificing Ukrainian draftees was "cheaper" than deploying American troops — had grown fearful once a [[military-draft|draft]] became conceivable for themselves.[12] The commentary held that wars are won with soldiers rather than technology and money, and that without a steady supply of troops any defensive or offensive posture degrades.[12]

## Alleged subversion in Africa's Sahel (2025)

In August 2025 Russia's deputy representative to the United Nations, [[dmitry-polyansky|Dmitry Polyansky]], told a [[united-nations-security-council|UN Security Council]] briefing that Ukraine was providing weapons, drones and training for militants to stage coordinated attacks on governments in Africa's Sahel region.[13] He said "concrete facts" demonstrated that Ukrainian special services, including the Defense Intelligence of Ukraine, had been involved in subversive activities in Sahel countries and other parts of Africa, and called for an investigation into what "the Kiev regime is doing on the African continent."[13] Badlands commentary treated the allegations as consistent with its long-running thesis linking the 2011 NATO intervention in Libya, the creation of [[al-qaeda|Al Qaeda]] and [[isis|ISIS]], and present-day Western intelligence operations conducted through Ukrainian proxies.[13]

## Putin's terms for ending the war (August 2025)

Moscow's formal demands for ending the war, delivered to Washington days after the Alaska meeting between [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], were no [[nato|NATO]] membership for Ukraine, no Western troops on Ukrainian soil, and surrender of the Donbas region.[13] Reporting indicated Putin would agree to freeze the front lines in Kharkiv and Zaporizhzhia and relinquish some captured territory in the Kharkiv, Sumy and Dnipropetrovsk regions.[13] Intelligence and analysis on the negotiations was simultaneously withheld from the [[five-eyes|Five Eyes]] partners under a NOFORN directive signed by [[tulsi-gabbard|Tulsi Gabbard]].[13]

## Long-range strikes into Russia (November 2024)

In November 2024, Ukraine launched British-made [[storm-shadow-missile|Storm Shadow]] cruise missiles at military targets inside [[russia|Russia]] for the first time, a day after its first use of U.S. long-range ATACMS missiles on Russian soil following authorization by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[14] Kyiv had pleaded for months with Western allies for permission to strike Russian territory as its troops struggled to hold ground in the [[kursk-incursion|Kursk region]]; a Russian military blog posted images of purported Storm Shadow fragments near the village of Marino, while the local governor said air defences downed two Ukrainian missiles.[14]

## Contours of a negotiated settlement

Reuters reported the same month that [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] was open to a ceasefire discussion with [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] but ruled out major territorial concessions and demanded that Ukraine abandon its bid to join [[nato|NATO]].[14] Russian officials indicated the Kremlin could accept freezing the conflict along the front lines, with negotiation possible over the division of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson — regions where Russian forces controlled 70–80% of the territory while Ukrainian troops still held roughly 26,000 square kilometres — and possible Russian withdrawal from smaller holdings in Kharkiv and Mykolaiv.[14] Czech President [[petr-pavel|Petr Pavel]] warned that any such deal would "most probably not be in our interest and in the interest of Ukraine."[14]

## Escalation after Belgorod (January 2024)

Moscow's large-scale air assault on cities across Ukraine on December 29, 2023 killed 23 people, and was followed a day later by a strike on the Russian city of Belgorod that killed at least two dozen.[15] President Vladimir Putin then vowed that [[russia|Russia]] would "intensify" its strikes against Ukraine, saying he was "burning with rage" but maintaining that Russian forces would continue to hit military targets rather than civilians.[15] The [[belgorod-attack|exchange]] marked a sharp escalation at the turn of the year.[15]

## May 2023 long-term security aid

U.S. officials said on May 8, 2023 that Washington would provide Ukraine with an additional $1.2 billion in long-term military aid under the [[ukraine-security-assistance-initiative|Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative]], to bolster air defenses as [[russia|Russia]] pounded the country with drones, rockets and surface-to-air missiles.[16] Unlike equipment drawn quickly from [[pentagon|Pentagon]] stocks, the money was to be spent over months or years, funding HAWK air-defense systems, air-defense munitions, drones, artillery, rockets, satellite imagery assistance and sustainment for existing systems.[16] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that "the only people this proxy war is hurting are the American people," citing Associated Press polling showing only 19% of Americans had high confidence in [[joe-biden|Biden]]'s Ukraine strategy against 43% with hardly any.[16]

## Patriot transfers and sabotage inside Russia (June 2025)

Israeli Ambassador to Kiev Mikhail Brodsky confirmed in June 2025 that [[israel|Israel]] had quietly transferred US-made [[patriot-missile-system|Patriot]] air defense systems to Ukraine, systems originally deployed in Israel in the early 1990s, after years in which Israel described its assistance to Kiev as purely humanitarian.[17] Axios reported that the United States moved around 90 Patriot missiles from Israel to Poland for onward delivery, and the New York Times reported that Ukraine would receive a full Patriot system previously stationed in Israel.[17]

The same month, Russian Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] accused Britain of enabling Ukrainian terrorist acts inside Russia, saying that Ukrainian operations "would have been helpless without the support... from the British."[17] Several sabotage incidents struck Russian railways, the worst on June 1 when a bridge collapsed in front of a moving passenger train in Bryansk Region, killing seven and injuring more than 100; that same day Ukraine struck Russian airbases from Murmansk to Irkutsk using explosive-laden drones launched from commercial trucks.[17]

## Drone support to Syrian rebels (2024)

In December 2024 the Washington Post confirmed prior reporting that Ukrainian intelligence had supplied Hayat Tahrir al-Sham with direct drone support ahead of the offensive that overthrew Syrian President [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al-Assad]], with columnist David Ignatius writing that Kyiv sent "about 20 experienced drone operators and about 150 first-person-view drones to the rebel headquarters in Idlib, Syria."[18] Ukrainian officials had previously boasted that they would help strike Russian assets and bases in Syria in order to bog down Russian forces and distract Moscow's command from the Ukrainian front.[18] Western intelligence sources judged the aid played only a modest role in Assad's fall, but framed it as part of a broader Ukrainian effort to strike covertly at Russian operations in the Middle East, Africa and inside Russia itself.[18] [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] cited the confirmation as evidence that NATO and the Deep State were backing Al Qaeda and ISIS-linked forces and that Ukraine under Zelensky amounted to a terrorist state, further alleging that the "broader Ukrainian effort" encompassed the financing of ISIS in Africa.[18] See [[hayat-tahrir-al-sham|Hayat Tahrir al-Sham]].[18]

## Energy-sector kickback scandal and EU funding (November 2025)

Earlier in November 2025, Ukraine's anti-corruption bodies revealed that a close associate and former long-time business partner of President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] had run a $100 million kickback scheme in the energy sector, a sector heavily dependent on Western aid.[19] Russian Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] cited the affair in arguing that beneficiaries of Ukrainian corruption may exist within the [[european-union|European Union]] itself, given the bloc's continued determination to fund Kiev — including plans to raise €135 billion ($156 billion) for 2026 and 2027.[19] Badlands commentary characterized the Ukrainian government as an organized crime syndicate whose network extends into the EU and beyond, noting that Zelensky's cabinet members were resigning in disgrace over the embezzlement scandal.[19]

## Exhaustion of US funding and the Davos "What If Ukraine Loses?" panel

By January 2024 the United States had run out of money for Ukraine and could no longer supply the ammunition and missiles Kyiv needed to fend off the Russian invasion.[20] With the aid caught up in domestic politics, the Biden administration hosted the monthly meeting of roughly 50 supporting nations empty-handed for the first time, looking to allies to bridge the gap while awaiting congressional approval of further funds; Defense Secretary [[lloyd-austin|Lloyd Austin]], who had established the group in April 2022, urged participants by video to "dig deep" for ground-based air defense systems and interceptors.[20]

In the same period the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] convened a Davos panel titled "What If Ukraine Loses?", treated as an admission that Ukraine might be defeated despite Western money, armaments and intelligence support.[20] Commentary noted that the narrative had shifted since 2022 from complete victory including the retaking of the Donbas and Crimea to merely holding the front line, and that the failure of the heavily promoted counter-offensive — which gained no significant ground — had been a propaganda error that cast doubt on Ukraine's other claims.[20]

## F-16 friendly fire claim (August 2024)

In late August 2024 Ukrainian lawmaker Maryana Bezuglaya said that a US-made F-16 delivered to Ukraine earlier that year had been shot down by a Ukrainian Patriot air defense system in a friendly fire incident, writing on Telegram that the aircraft flown by pilot Alexey "Moonfish" Mes was destroyed "due to a lack of coordination between the [military] units."[21] She criticized the Ukrainian Air Force for describing the loss as a crash and denounced what she called a "culture of lies" in the Air Force Command and other senior military headquarters that she argued was degrading military decision-making, noting that no generals had been punished over the loss of the aircraft and its pilot.[21] Badlands Media used the episode to question the wisdom of rapidly training Ukrainian pilots on the F-16, citing the reported deaths of three other F-16 pilots during a training exercise, and raised the possibility that the account was itself a fiction masking diverted funds.[21]

## April 2024 attacks on the power grid

A large Russian missile and drone offensive on April 11, 2024 bombarded Ukraine's power grid and underground natural gas storage facilities.[22] Six hypersonic Kinzhal missiles struck critical infrastructure near Kyiv and around Lviv without being intercepted, while Ukrainian defenses downed 18 of 42 conventional missiles and one of 40 drones.[22] Energy Minister [[german-galushchenko|German Galushchenko]] reported damage to plants near Kyiv and in the Kharkiv, Zaporizhzhia and Lviv regions, and [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] said a plant in Odesa was hit; the [[trypilska-power-plant|Trypilska plant]] south of Kyiv, the region's largest, was struck by six missiles.[22] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] contrasted the strikes with Ukraine's newly passed conscription measures intended to raise 500,000 additional men, argued that ending the conflict quickly would spare those lives, and attributed the war's seemingly existential character to the [[cia|CIA]] and to unresolved questions about biological research laboratories in the country.[22]
