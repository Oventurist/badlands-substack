# Election Assistance Commission

The US Election Assistance Commission (EAC) is an independent federal agency that certifies voting systems, accredits voting system testing laboratories, administers federal election grants, and maintains the national mail voter registration form.[1]

## July 2026 dismissals

On Thursday, July 9, 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] dismissed the remaining members of the EAC.[1] One Republican commissioner resigned, while the two Democratic commissioners were notified by email that their positions had been terminated.[1] The [[white-house|White House]] had not announced replacement nominees, though the president has authority to appoint new commissioners subject to [[united-states-senate|Senate]] confirmation.[1]

## Badlands criticism

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the EAC as the designated "expert" body that assures the public that erroneous code, code read errors, glitches, or database coding mismatches are "no big deal," and that even genuine problems will be downplayed because public trust requires it.[1] She argued that once observers recognize that such bureaucracies speak about elections with the primary goal of preserving trust — "even if it means lying to the public" — they begin to question every election story they have been told, concluding that "elections are just that: stories."[1]

In her view, US elections are incapable of delivering trustworthy outcomes because they are run in "unauditable black boxes with fake voters, fake ballots, fake counts, and a whole lot of obstruction."[1] She wrote that real elections are possible only with honesty and accountability for past fakery, that firing the EAC principals was "not enough," and that those who participated in lying to the public under the banner of "preserving trust" should be prosecuted.[1]

## Statutory basis and functions

[[congress|Congress]] created the commission under the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act of 2002]].[2] Its four presidentially appointed and Senate-confirmed members are required to be divided evenly between the two major political parties, and the agency tests and certifies voting equipment, distributes election grants, supports state and local election officials, and maintains the national mail voter-registration form.[2]

## Complete vacancy and the removal power

The July 9, 2026 removals left the four-seat bipartisan agency without any sitting commissioners.[2] Democratic commissioners [[thomas-hicks|Thomas Hicks]] and [[benjamin-hovland|Benjamin Hovland]] were notified by email that their positions had been terminated effective immediately; Republican commissioner [[christy-mccormick|Christy McCormick]] resigned; and Republican [[donald-palmer|Donald Palmer]] had voluntarily left earlier in the year.[2] A [[white-house|White House]] official said [[donald-trump|Trump]] had authority to remove officials who were not aligned with the administration's election-security priorities, citing the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]]'s recent decision in [[trump-v-slaughter|*Trump v. Slaughter*]] as precedent.[2]

## Debate over replacement versus abolition

In the days after the gutting there was public discussion about replacing the commissioners with individuals focused on election integrity.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued against this, writing that corrupted bureaucracies should be ended rather than evolved.[2] She described the EAC as "born from hanging chads, established through HAVA," and as little more than a Potemkin Village of security standards and certification across its 24 years of existence — an entity whose post mortem in any other industry would lead to dissolution.[2]

In her account, HAVA gave birth to both the modern elections industry and its regulators, a public-private partnership she calls "the story of democracy" and "a cancer to the Republic," whose net effect over two dozen years has been a crisis of trust in elections and in government itself.[2] Rather than preserving the body, she urged shutting it down and pursuing investigation and prosecution, offering as one line of inquiry why the EAC covered for the lack of machine certification when the machines were not certifiable.[2] She added that she remained unwilling to invest hope in the process, viewing real elections as an unattainable dream and the seating of new members in corrupted EAC seats as a negative indicator supporting her skepticism.[2]

## Trump election executive order (2025)

Created by Congress after the "hanging chads" ballot controversy of the 2000 presidential contest in Florida, the U.S. Election Assistance Commission was established as an independent commission to help states update their voting equipment.[3] Though it had operated in relative anonymity since, the agency became central to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order seeking to overhaul U.S. elections, with one of the commission's boards meeting in North Carolina in late April 2025 — the first commission-related meeting after the directives were announced.[3] Among other things, Trump directed the agency to update the national voter registration form to add a proof of citizenship requirement, though whether a president can order an independent agency to act, and whether the commission has authority to do what Trump wants, was expected to be settled in court.[3]

Badlands contributor [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] framed the moment in existential terms, writing that the 2024 election was approached "as if this was our last chance" and that this is "the last opportunity we have to right the ship."[3] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] disputed the Associated Press framing of the EAC as a "little-known federal agency," calling it infamous and saying she and CannCon's newsletter had covered its cover-ups for years.[3] She cited a November 2023 post noting that a report had found the "direct cause" of an anomaly "inconclusive" while admitting that "erroneous code" was found in EAC-certified systems, with the offered explanation of a "misread of the QR code" described by many experts as absurd.[3] In her view, the recurring pattern of erroneous code, code read errors, glitches, and database coding mismatches shows that code-based elections are unreliable, that the EAC knows it, and that the agency exists to cover it up — leaving her "very interested to see where this whole 'independent agency' thing goes in the courts."[3]

## Origins in HAVA (2023 account)

In a March 23, 2023 Badlands Media article, [[holly-at-altitude|Holly at Altitude]] wrote that the EAC was created to "quasi-execute" the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]], with responsibilities including distributing federal funding and testing and approving electronic voting equipment and vendors.[4] She identified the passage of HAVA together with the creation of the new commission as the pivotal moment at which U.S. elections changed, launching a multi-billion-dollar tech-driven election industry and, in her view, placing election integrity, security, and validity on a path to destruction.[4]

The same article treated the resulting apparatus as a public-private partnership whose "winners" — companies, government agencies, politicians, the corporate media, NGOs, and assorted power brokers — have an interest in preserving it, and argued that citizens can loosen that chokehold through local action such as challenging election equipment budgets and contracts.[4]
