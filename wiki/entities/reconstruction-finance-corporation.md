# Reconstruction Finance Corporation

The **Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC)** was the alternative lending mechanism created outside [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] control by [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]], and became the number one lender to infrastructure in America throughout the 1930s.[1]

Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presents the RFC as central to FDR's disempowerment of the London-controlled Federal Reserve, alongside the installation of the industrialist Marriner Eccles as Chair, who forced the Fed to obey national commands for the first time since 1913.[1] Once liberated from the shackles of the central banks, FDR and his allies restored confidence in banking: within 31 days of the bank holiday 75% of banks were operational, the FDIC was created to insure deposits, four million people were given immediate work, and hundreds of libraries, schools and hospitals were built and staffed — all funded through the RFC.[1]

From 1933 to 1939, 45,000 infrastructure projects were built under a grand design FDR termed the "Four Quarters," featuring the [[tennessee-valley-authority|Tennessee Valley Authority]] zone in the southeast, the Columbia River Treaty zone in the northwest, the St. Lawrence Seaway zone in the northeast and the Hoover Dam/Colorado zone in the southwest — a scheme the article compares to China's Belt and Road Initiative.[1]

## Role in the New Deal

In [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s account of [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]]'s first hundred days, the RFC was unleashed to provide broad credit, acting as a national bank that bypassed the private [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and channeling $33 billion into the real economy by 1945 — more than all private commercial banks combined.[2] It stood alongside [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]], the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] and the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|SEC]] as pillars of the [[new-deal|New Deal]] response to the depression.[2]
