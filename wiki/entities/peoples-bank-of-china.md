# People's Bank of China

The **People's Bank of China** (PBoC) is the central bank of the People's Republic of China, responsible for monetary policy, currency issuance, and the management of the country's official reserves.[1]

## Gold accumulation

The PBoC emerged as the single largest official-sector gold buyer in February 2023, increasing its reported holdings by 24.9 tons in that month alone.[1] It was the fourth consecutive month of publicly reported Chinese purchases, over which China's official gold reserves grew by 102 tons — a significant share of the global [[central-bank-gold-buying|central bank gold buying]] streak that had run eleven straight months by that point.[1]

Financial advisor [[kirk-elliott|Kirk Elliott]] noted that China is "notorious for withholding key data" about its gold holdings, and argued that publicly disclosed record purchases therefore imply that the PBoC's actual reserves are considerably larger than reported.[1] The bank's accumulation is widely read as part of the broader [[de-dollarization|de-dollarization]] trend, alongside the growing use of the yuan in trade settlement.[1]

## Liquidity injections and the 2023 slowdown

In August 2023 the PBoC injected the largest amount of short-term cash into China's financial system since February, one day after cutting interest rates, as confidence across Chinese financial markets declined week by week.[2] Reporting at the time described the country's economy as on the "verge of collapse," with a PBoC central banker calling for helicopter money, and [[fitch-ratings|Fitch Ratings]] warning it might reconsider China's A+ sovereign credit rating if non-government debt liabilities ballooned.[2]

[[badlands-media|Badlands]] contributor [[paul-fleuret|Paul Fleuret]] wrote that the PBoC had attempted, "much like our own [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]," to prop up a faltering economy with injections of more paper money, and argued that with multiple banks defaulting, interest rates climbing and the housing and job markets in free fall, a large Chinese recession was a matter of when rather than if.[2] He cited the Xinhua News Agency to the effect that local government finances and the national economy were "on the verge of collapse, and the thunder could explode at any time," and contended that [[chinese-communist-party|CCP]] propaganda portraying China as the coming preeminent superpower concealed a country closer to falling apart at the seams.[2]
