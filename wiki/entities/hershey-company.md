# The Hershey Company

The Hershey Company is an American confectionery manufacturer and the owner of the Reese's Peanut Butter Cup brand.[1]

## 2026 ingredient reformulation dispute

In February 2026, Brad Reese — grandson of the inventor of Reese's Peanut Butter Cups — penned an open letter to Hershey accusing the company of "quietly replacing" the candy's ingredients, writing that the product's "identity is being rewritten."[1]

"My grandfather built REESE'S on a simple, enduring architecture: Milk Chocolate + Peanut Butter. Not a flavor idea. Not a marketing construct. A real, tangible product identity that consumers have trusted for a century," Reese wrote.[1] "But today, REESE'S identity is being rewritten, not by storytellers, but by formulation decisions that replace Milk Chocolate with compound coatings and Peanut Butter with peanut-butter-style crèmes across multiple REESE'S products."[1]

The episode was carried as a bonus item in the [[badlands-brief|Badlands Brief]].[1]

## 2025 cocoa hedging request

In January 2025, amid a worsening global cocoa squeeze, Hershey reportedly sought approval from the [[commodity-futures-trading-commission|Commodity Futures Trading Commission]] to purchase a massive quantity of cocoa through the New York exchange, a move analysts said highlighted that cocoa cost inflation would grow even more extreme in 2025.[2] First reported by Bloomberg, the request from the maker of Reese's Peanut Butter Cups was for 90,000 metric tons of cocoa on ICE Futures US — equivalent to roughly 5,000 twenty-foot containers and more than nine times the amount the exchange allows.[2]

Goldman Sachs analyst Natasha de la Grense told clients that "the headlines on Hershey today suggest the outlook for confectionary (cocoa) cost inflation is getting even more extreme."[2] The request followed a mid-December call by Goldman commodity derivatives analyst Hugo Fuentes for clients to "go long cocoa," on the view that prices were positioned for significant upside driven by structural supply deficits, under-hedged consumers, and historically low warehouse stocks.[2]

Badlands writer Ashe in America connected the episode to the 1919 Brooklyn Chocolate Flood, in which a fire spread to a floor where butter was stored and sent melted chocolate and butter flowing through Brooklyn streets, drawing residents who reportedly licked the fudgy landscape.[2] She argued that the story is a reminder that the economy runs on real goods and services, that Americans need an economic awakening, and that rising chocolate prices could become an authentically unifying political moment.[2]
