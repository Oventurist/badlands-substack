# Iroquois Confederacy

The Iroquois Confederacy, or Six Nations, and its constituent Mohawk nation appear in the Badlands Media essay [[a-new-american-renaissance|A New American Renaissance]] as a decisive strategic actor in the struggle between the British and French empires in North America.

The essay recounts that [[william-johnson|William Johnson]], an Irish-born New York colonist, negotiated treaties with the Six Nations, learned several native dialects, was made a Mohawk civil chief by 1742, and was appointed colonel of the Six Nation Mohawks. The Mohawks reportedly requested that Johnson serve as the official British representative to them, because no other British official respected them. The article argues that the [[jesuits|Jesuits]] controlling French North America had long cultivated alliances with native bands, and that if the Mohawks or Iroquois had sided with the French, the outcome of the Battle of Quebec would likely have differed; their decision to fight alongside the English "tipped the scales of battle entirely."

[[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]]'s *The Death of General Wolfe* places a Mohawk warrior at the center of the composition, carrying, in the source's reading, more visual weight than the dying general himself. The Delaware people, separately, taught the young West to paint with clay and bear grease and were the counterparty to the [[penn-treaty-of-1682|Penn Treaty of 1682]]. [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] frames the harmonization of European and First Nations civilizations as the central unresolved problem of the American founding, arguing that only when Americans treat Native peoples as "sacred partners in a common mission" — rather than clearing them from their land or confining them to "invisible prisons called 'reservations'" — will the country "acquire the real moral fitness to survive." He notes that James Fenimore Cooper pursued the same themes in his popular tales.
