# World Business Academy

The World Business Academy is the organization whose published analysis of Costa Rican public banking supplies the empirical core of Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]]'s argument for a [[public-banking-option|public banking option]] in the United States.[1]

## Material cited

Lange quotes the Academy's article "Public Banking in Costa Rica: A Remarkable Model," which relays the account of political activist and long-time [[costa-rica|Costa Rica]] resident Scott Bidstrup.[1] Bidstrup reports that Costa Rica has had a "Public Option" for 64 years: of 29 licensed banks, mutual associations and credit unions in the country, four were established as national, publicly-owned banks in 1949 and have remained in public hands ever since, despite enormous pressure from the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]] and the United States to privatize them alongside other public assets.[1] Costa Ricans resisted that pressure because the value of a public banking option had become abundantly clear to everyone in the country.[1]

Bidstrup contrasts the record of private institutions — countless private banks, mutual associations and credit unions came and went over three decades, with depositors inevitably losing most of the value of their accounts — against the four state banks, which compete fiercely with one another, have had no failures in 31 years, and now hold fully **80% of all retail deposits**, leaving the 25 private institutions to share the remainder.[1]

The Academy material further argues that the stability of the local economy is a principal reason Costa Rica has never had much difficulty attracting direct foreign investment and remains the regional leader in that regard, and that "the value and importance of a public banking sector to the overall stability and health of an economy has been well proven by the Costa Rican experience."[1] Neighbouring countries with fully privatized banking systems have, de facto, encouraged people to keep money in "Mattress First National," leaving their financial sectors unable to prosper, while in Costa Rica most money sits in banks carrying the full faith and credit of the Republic and is therefore available for lending.[1]

## The Bank of North Dakota reference

It is also the World Business Academy that supplies Lange's American comparison: Costa Ricans "are amazed to hear there is only one public depository bank in the United States (the [[bank-of-north-dakota|Bank of North Dakota]]), and few people have private access to it."[1]
