# TikTok

TikTok is described in Badlands Media commentary as a CCP-run platform whose algorithms are deliberately tuned to degrade its users.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] writes that "we know CCP-run TikTok manipulates their algorithms so that intelligent things go to the bottom and stupid and degrading things go to the top, so more people see them," with the result that "stupidity and degradation are rewarded."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

He describes the effect on users, and particularly young people, as a pursuit of likes, views, adoration and approval that they believe they need — without realising that every aspect of the equation is manipulated through [[artificial-intelligence-manipulation|artificial intelligence]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america) TikTok functions in the article as the clearest documented instance of a broader claim that social-media interactions people believe to be human are in substantial part AI-driven.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

Within the article's argument, this algorithmic degradation is a mechanism of the first stage of the [[communist-takeover-of-america|Communist takeover]] — the stripping away of American identity, values and morals before the economy is destroyed.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

## Proposed American buyers

In "A New Hollywood," [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] records that, at the same time he rolled out [[project-stargate|Project Stargate]], [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] suggested that either [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] or [[oracle|Oracle]] cofounder [[larry-ellison|Larry Ellison]] buy TikTok.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-hollywood) Carlson cites the suggestion as evidence that Ellison is a significant part of Trump's plans going forward.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-hollywood)

## 2024 forced-divestiture legislation

On Tuesday, April 23, 2024, the [[united-states-senate|Senate]] followed through with congressional plans to force the sale of TikTok, despite vows from its Chinese owners that they would fight the legislation in court.[3] The measure, tucked into a $95 billion foreign aid package that passed the upper chamber 79–18, headed to the desk of President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], who had promised to sign it.[3]

The House originally passed the bill — which requires [[bytedance|ByteDance]], TikTok's parent company, to divest or face a nationwide ban — in March 2024, but the version expected to become law reflected a compromise between the two chambers.[3] The House extended the divestment window from six to nine months and gave the president authority to extend that runway another 90 days, creating in effect a one-year grace period; the tweak proved decisive in winning the support of Sen. [[maria-cantwell|Maria Cantwell]], who leads the Senate panel with jurisdiction over the app.[3]

## December 2024 Supreme Court challenge

By the end of December 2024 a U.S. statutory deadline for a potential ban on TikTok was set to take effect the day before the presidential inauguration, and the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] had agreed to hear the company's challenge on an expedited schedule.[4] President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] intervened, asking the justices to delay the deadline so that he could "negotiate a resolution to save the platform" once in office; the filing was authored by his personal appellate attorney and solicitor general nominee [[d-john-sauer|D. John Sauer]].[4] See [[tiktok-ban|TikTok ban]].[4]

## US joint venture (September 2025)

On Thursday, September 25, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed an executive order approving a deal placing TikTok's U.S. operations under the control of American businesses.[5] The document published by the [[white-house|White House]] stated that "TikTok's US application would be operated by a new joint-venture company based in the United States," run by a board of directors with national security and cybersecurity credentials and subject to strict rules protecting Americans' data and national security.[5] The White House said the venture would be "majority-owned by US investors," with [[bytedance|ByteDance]] holding less than 20% of the stock.[5] After signing, Trump said he had discussed the deal by phone with Chinese President [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] and received his consent.[5]

## Romanian election controversy (December 2024)

The Bucharest think tank [[expert-forum|Expert Forum]] reported that the TikTok account of Romanian presidential candidate [[calin-georgescu|Călin Georgescu]] experienced an explosion of engagement before the November 2024 first round that appeared "sudden and artificial."[6] Romanian officials used the finding to argue that Georgescu had "benefited from massive exposure due to preferential treatment" granted by TikTok and to allege Russian interference in the result.[6] The [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] disputed the causal claim that an artificial TikTok following could translate into a mass shift in votes.[6]


## 2024 divestiture legislation

In March 2024 President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] said he would sign the [[protecting-americans-from-foreign-adversary-controlled-applications-act|Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act]] if [[congress|Congress]] passed it, telling reporters outside Air Force One, "If they pass it, I'll sign it."[7] The House Committee on Energy and Commerce had unanimously approved the measure 50–0 on March 7, 2024.[7] The bill would allow the president to force divestiture of social media companies more than 20 percent controlled by China, Iran, North Korea, or Russia, naming TikTok — owned by [[bytedance|ByteDance]] — explicitly.[7]

Security experts had long warned that TikTok is a weaponized application usable to promote [[chinese-communist-party|Chinese Communist Party]] propaganda or feed Americans' data to the regime, and TikTok officials had previously acknowledged suppressing and "heating" content at the regime's request while claiming the practice had ceased.[7] The U.S. government was investigating ByteDance for allegedly using TikTok geolocation data to stalk and harass American journalists reporting on its CCP links.[7]

Badlands writer [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] called the situation odd given Biden's overt use of TikTok as a campaign platform, suggesting either a flip-flop setting up a new narrative that deepens CCP access to American culture, or a pretext for legally shutting down social media companies deemed dangerous by the administration.[7] He also cited TikTok as a probable vector of foreign AI-driven psychological operations modifying youth behaviour toward gender ideology and radical leftism.[7]

## April 2025 sale deadline and tariff linkage

In early April 2025 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he had come "pretty close" to a deal with China on a TikTok sale, but that Beijing reversed course after he announced new tariffs of 54 percent on Chinese goods.[8] Speaking to reporters aboard Air Force One on 6 April, Trump said: "We had a deal pretty much for TikTok — not a deal but pretty close — and then China changed the deal because of the tariffs."[8] He added that "if I gave a little cut in tariffs, they'd approve that deal in 15 minutes, which shows you the power of tariffs."[8]

Trump had announced the previous Friday that he would sign an executive order granting [[bytedance|ByteDance]], TikTok's Chinese owner, 75 more days to divest its stake or see the app banned in the United States.[8] "We do not want TikTok to 'go dark,'" he wrote on Truth Social, adding that he looked forward to working with TikTok and China to close the deal.[8] Reporting characterized the tariffs as a bargaining chip aimed at forcing Chinese concessions on the sale.[8]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that renewed fears of TikTok going dark dominated social media over the weekend, with users defending the platform's value to independent creators, and argued that the underlying contest is over control of the code — described as Beijing's greatest leverage and the means of access to the minds of children worldwide.[8]


## India comeback signals and 2025 ban deadlines

In September 2025 TikTok India opened two positions at its Gurgaon office, posting jobs on LinkedIn for a "Content Moderator (Bengali Speaker), Trust and Safety" and a "Wellbeing Partnership and Operations Lead, Trust and Safety."[9] The hiring came weeks after TikTok's website became partially accessible to some Indian users, fuelling speculation that the Chinese video platform was preparing for a comeback, though the app remained banned in India and unavailable on both the Google Play Store and Apple App Store there.[9] The moderator listing specified that the role involves content "related to every-day life, but it can also include (but is not limited to) bullying; hate speech; child safety; depictions of harm to self and others, and harm to animals."[9]

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] tied the development to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s own posting about his dominance on the platform, noting that [[dan-scavino|Dan Scavino]] tweeted about Trump breaking the internet a minute later.[9] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that TikTok was supposed to be banned in the US on September 17, that Australia was implementing an under-16 TikTok ban later that year, and that India remained publicly undecided over whether the platform would return — all while Trump was "pumping the platform like it's preparing for an IPO" and dominating its content in the process.[9]

## First Amendment challenge (May 2024)

On Tuesday, May 7, 2024, TikTok and [[bytedance|ByteDance]] petitioned the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit to strike down the divest-or-ban law signed by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], arguing it violated the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] rights of the platform's 170 million American users and rested on "speculative and analytically flawed concerns about data security and content manipulation."[10] The filing asserted that "for the first time in history, Congress has enacted a law that subjects a single, named speech platform to a permanent, nationwide ban," and asked the court to block Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] from enforcing it.[10] See the [[2024-tiktok-lawsuit-against-the-divest-or-ban-law|2024 TikTok lawsuit]].[10]

## US version split (July 2025)

In July 2025, reporting indicated that owner [[bytedance|ByteDance]] was planning to create a separate US version of TikTok, as *The Information* reported on Sunday, July 6, 2025.[11] The plan paralleled a documented scheme to split US users of the company's video-editing app [[capcut|CapCut]] into a new "CapCut US" build, and suggested that ByteDance might be making a broader push to separate itself from the United States beyond a TikTok sale.[11] Both moves were framed as part of compliance with the 2024 law requiring ByteDance to divest TikTok and its other US assets or essentially cease operating in the country.[11]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that she had been skeptical of the Trump 1.0 plan to have [[oracle|Oracle]] host a US version of TikTok because she was unsure it could work without sacrificing the user experience and the revenue model.[11] She argued that if the new app ran on the same underlying infrastructure the solution would be narrative only, whereas changing the infrastructure alongside the app would make success depend on UX and raise questions about dev teams and product roadmaps.[11] She added that corporations and their technology consultants often design aspirational or theoretical solutions to regulatory requirements they know are unworkable — citing cloud exit strategies as an analogue — and that such solutions are usually accepted because the problem statements are theoretical and the approval bodies non-technical.[11]

## The 2023 US Army "TikTok mutiny"

In December 2023 the Daily Mail reported that the [[united-states-army|US Army]] was facing a "TikTok mutiny" as Generation Z recruits used the platform to complain about low pay, poor food and fitness tests, posting in uniform from US bases amid a recruiting shortfall of 25 percent against the prior year's target.[12] Military influencer Anthony Laster, who had more than a million TikTok followers, drew over 600,000 views on a post criticizing Army life for "No Privacy, The Pay Sucks, Sh***y Food, Disrespectful Leadership, NO SLEEP!" and in another claimed to have spent a full day watching TikToks while ostensibly fighting the Taliban.[12]

Commentators argued the posts presented a damaging image of the US military to prospective recruits and were likely to intensify hostility toward the platform among politicians of both parties who cite its links to China and accuse it of pushing subversive anti-US propaganda.[12] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] allowed that Chinese use of TikTok to demoralize US troops was plausible while insisting the complaints themselves were legitimate, arguing that the US government was degrading morale on its own.[12]
