# Frank Vanderlip

Frank Vanderlip was a U.S. [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] official who was recruited by Citibank president [[james-stillman|James Stillman]] to be his eventual successor at the bank.[1] According to the account cited by Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]], Vanderlip secretly accepted Stillman's offer and then spent another year at Treasury — described as a patent conflict of interest — before moving to the bank.[1]

Vanderlip later became one of the architects of America's third central bank, the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve System]].[1] Lange treats this as decisive evidence that [[citibank|Citibank]] was behind the formation of the Federal Reserve, and that the revolving door between the bank and Washington was deliberate rather than incidental.[1] In his framing, the United States has operated under a centralized banking system controlled by private and foreign bankers ever since — a system he predicts [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] intends to end by returning control of banking to the people.[1]

## Jekyll Island and the Federal Reserve

Frank Vanderlip was president of National Citibank of New York — an institution the article identifies with the Rockefeller interests — and attended the secret [[jekyll-island-meeting|Jekyll Island meeting]] as a representative of the Rockefeller family.[2] Years later he confirmed the trip in the Saturday Evening Post, writing: "I was as secretive indeed, as furtive as any conspirator ... Discovery we knew, simply must not happen, or else all our time and effort would be wasted. If it were to be exposed that our particular group had got together and written a banking bill, that bill would have no chance whatever of passage by Congress."[2]

Together with Senator [[nelson-aldrich|Nelson Aldrich]], Vanderlip publicly opposed the Glass-Owen Bill in order to persuade the public that it differed radically from the [[aldrich-plan|Aldrich bill]] — posturing the article says was sufficient to quell suspicion.[2] He later admitted, again in the Saturday Evening Post, that "although the Aldrich Federal Reserve Plan was defeated when it bore the name Aldrich, nevertheless its essential points were all contained in the plan that finally was adopted."[2]
