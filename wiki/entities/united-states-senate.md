# United States Senate

The United States Senate is the upper chamber of [[congress|Congress]], responsible for passing legislation including the annual defense authorization bill.[1]

## Fiscal Year 2027 NDAA and the strategic investment fund

In July 2026 the Senate's version of the Fiscal Year 2027 [[national-defense-authorization-act|National Defense Authorization Act]] was reported to include a provision authorizing the [[pentagon|Department of Defense]] to establish a strategic investment fund capable of taking equity stakes in private companies deemed critical to national security.[1] The proposal was intended to strengthen domestic defense and technology supply chains in areas such as advanced manufacturing, artificial intelligence, semiconductors and critical minerals.[1]

The Senate language would centralize the Pentagon's direct equity investment authority, establish congressional reporting requirements and oversight mechanisms, and require regular disclosures regarding investment decisions.[1] Supporters argued the fund would preserve critical industrial capabilities that might not otherwise attract private capital, while critics contended it would expand the federal government's role as an investor in private enterprise and expose taxpayers to investment risk.[1] The provision would still need to survive negotiations with the House of Representatives before becoming law.[1]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the provision as the legislative codification of the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration's break from tradition in taking direct equity positions in firms such as Intel and MP Materials, noting the ironic parallel to policies long advocated by [[bernie-sanders|Bernie Sanders]].[1]

## Venezuela war powers vote (January 2026)

On January 8, 2026, the Senate advanced a bipartisan war powers resolution led by Sen. [[tim-kaine|Tim Kaine]] by a 52–47 procedural vote, with several Republicans joining Democrats; the measure would have required President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to obtain congressional approval before further military action in or against [[venezuela|Venezuela]].[2] On January 14, 2026, after administration pressure, the Senate voted 51–50 to block the [[venezuela-war-powers-resolution|resolution]], with Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] casting the tie-breaking vote and Senators [[josh-hawley|Josh Hawley]] and [[todd-young|Todd Young]] reversing their earlier support.[2] The resolution did not become law and Democrats decried the outcome.[2]

## Calls for abolition (July 2026)

A column published by [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] examined the [[democratic-socialists-of-america|Democratic Socialists of America]]'s renewed call to abolish the Senate, arguing the chamber is structurally undemocratic because every state receives equal representation regardless of population, and contending that it has become a major obstacle to advancing legislation favored by national popular majorities.[3] The piece revisited longstanding criticisms including the Senate's role in slowing or blocking federal legislation, confirming judicial nominees, and giving disproportionate influence to smaller states, and argued for constitutional reform while acknowledging that abolition would require an amendment approved through the Constitution's demanding amendment process.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] responded that the column's author, [[jonathan-chait|Jonathan Chait]], was at minimum a DSA apologist, and that his position effectively amounted to wanting a second House of Representatives.[3] She argued the episode revealed that the Senate — "despite all its transformation and ineffectiveness" — remains a barrier to the progressive agenda, and proposed making that barrier stronger by repealing the [[seventeenth-amendment|17th Amendment]] to restore the Senate as a body representing the states rather than the parties.[3]

In the same period the Senate was set to take up the [[fy2027-national-defense-authorization-act|FY2027 National Defense Authorization Act]] after House passage, with senators considering their own version before any final bill could be sent to the president.[3]

## SAVE America Act debate and intelligence oversight (March 2026)

The Senate began debating the [[save-america-act|SAVE America Act]], a Republican-backed bill requiring documentary proof of US citizenship — such as a passport or birth certificate — to register to vote in federal elections; Democrats opposed the measure, it faced procedural hurdles, and reporting considered passage unlikely.[4] [[donald-trump|President Trump]] posted on [[truth-social|Truth Social]] complaining that the bill's proper name was "THE SAVE AMERICA ACT, not the SAVE ACT."[4]

The same week the [[senate-intelligence-committee|Senate Intelligence Committee]] held its annual national security hearing with Director of National Intelligence [[tulsi-gabbard|Tulsi Gabbard]], [[cia|CIA]] Director [[john-ratcliffe|John Ratcliffe]] and [[fbi|FBI]] Director [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]], part of a broader set of Senate proceedings addressing national security issues and executive branch policy.[4] Badlands commentary noted that senators justified their conduct by invoking the anger of "the People" from whom their mandate derives.[4]

## Cabinet confirmations and the 30-hour rule (February 2025)

As of mid-February 2025, six of 22 Cabinet-level picks in the second [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration remained for the Senate to confirm.[5] The chamber voted on Thursday, February 13, to invoke cloture on two nominees — [[kelly-loeffler|Kelly Loeffler]] for the [[small-business-administration|Small Business Administration]] and [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]] for [[department-of-commerce|Commerce Secretary]] — with votes awaiting the chamber's reconvening after the Presidents Day holiday, and [[linda-mcmahon|Linda McMahon]] having testified before the Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee.[5]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] attacked the 30-hour post-cloture debate requirement as "fabricating power obstruction," noting that the Senate writes and governs its own rules: formal changes routed to the Rules Committee need 67 votes, but the [[nuclear-option|nuclear option]] allows a majority to change practice with 51 votes after a procedural ruling by the presiding officer, as both parties did in 2013, 2017 and 2019.[5] Unanimous consent and reinterpretation of existing rules offer alternative but rarely used paths.[5] She argued the rules serve as little more than ceremony in terms of value to the American people and urged Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] to cut the 30 hours to two.[5]

## First use of the new confirmation rules (September 2025)

On Thursday, 18 September 2025, Senate Republicans confirmed 48 of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s nominees along party lines in the first test of the chamber's new confirmation rules, covering undersecretary and assistant secretary positions across federal agencies as well as ambassadorships.[6] The rules had been changed the previous week when Republicans invoked the [[nuclear-option|nuclear option]] after a last-minute deal with Democrats collapsed, allowing unlimited nominees to be confirmed en bloc by simple majority, though still requiring procedural steps and 30 hours of debate.[6]

Majority Whip [[john-barrasso|John Barrasso]] blamed "Democrat obstruction" for the absence of voice-vote or unanimous-consent confirmations.[6] Badlands contributor Ashe in America derided the chamber as "the most impotent body in the federal government" and questioned why it had spent eight months of Trump's term appearing to negotiate with Democrats over appointments.[6]

## Advice and consent in the 2024 transition

The withdrawal of [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]] as attorney general nominee in November 2024 dramatically affirmed that the Senate retained its "advice and consent" power — a short phrase mentioned in passing in the Constitution — over the vetting and installation of a president's Cabinet.[7] Reporting at the time framed the outcome as possibly only a brief reprieve from more intense struggles to come as Senate Republicans sought to preserve their constitutional role, with [[donald-trump|Trump]] making Cabinet announcements at a rapid clip, often over social media, and frequently catching GOP senators off guard.[7] Anticipated difficulty winning Senate confirmation was also reported as a reason the transition considered installing [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] as deputy FBI director or in an appointed investigative role at the Justice Department rather than as director outright.[7]

### Confirmation of Kash Patel as FBI director
On February 20, 2025 the Senate took up the nomination of Kash Patel as FBI director, after he cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee the prior week by a 12–10 party-line vote.[8] Democrats raised concerns over his qualifications and the prospect that he would do President Donald Trump's bidding, but the Republican-controlled chamber was expected to confirm him unless more than three Republican senators defied Trump, which was seen as unlikely; Trump had already secured approval for most of his nominees despite initial Republican skepticism toward several of his choices.[8]
