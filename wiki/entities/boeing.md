# Boeing

Boeing is the American aerospace manufacturer whose share-price collapse and steady stream of negative coverage are used in Badlands Media commentary as evidence of a westward-to-eastward shift in [[deep-state|Deep State]] power.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Stock collapse and the Airbus contrast

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] notes that Boeing stock stood at $323 on the day [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] took office and fell to $95 a share within eight weeks.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) He then asks whether readers have heard any bad news about [[airbus|Airbus]] over the same period, and where Airbus is located — answering: France.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

The contrast supports Carlson's broader forecast that the Deep State in America will continue to shift toward Europe, outside [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s control, with Cabal families in England, France and Germany contending for a larger share of power.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Safety crisis and the Barnett whistleblower death (2024)

In March 2024 Boeing was at the center of a widening safety scandal following the death of [[john-barnett|John Barnett]], a 62-year-old quality manager who had worked for the company for 32 years before leaving in 2017 and who died of a self-inflicted gunshot wound according to the Charleston County coroner's office in South Carolina.[2] Barnett had exposed what his attorneys described as "very serious safety problems with the Boeing 787 Dreamliner" and said he had been retaliated against and subjected to a hostile work environment; he died in the middle of a legal deposition against the company.[2] Barnett had reportedly told a close family friend not to believe any announcement that he had committed suicide.[2]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the [[truth-community|truth community]] is "rightly skeptical of highly-convenient suicides," stressing that Barnett was testifying against a powerful Department of Defense contractor, was in good spirits, and had shown no indication of planning to take his own life.[2] She situated the death within a longer pattern of institutional decay at Boeing, citing an expert-panel report on the company's safety culture that found a "disconnect" between senior management and regular staff and signs that employees hesitated to report problems for fear of retaliation.[2] Former senior manager Adam Dickson, who worked on the 737 Max programme, was quoted saying the culture at Boeing "has been toxic to trust for over a decade now" and that added safety steps and procedures are almost ineffective while that distrust persists.[2]

The article also noted a [[federal-aviation-administration|Federal Aviation Administration]] warning that improperly installed wiring bundles on 737 Max aircraft could become damaged, causing wing controls to deploy unexpectedly and the aircraft to begin rolling.[2] Together with doors blowing off mid-air, wheels failing to deploy and software glitches, Ashe argued the accumulation of revelations made it unlikely Boeing could exit the inquiry with its brand intact, adding that if the company were intentionally endangering passengers and killing off witnesses, business failure "should be the least of their consequences."[2]

## Supplier whistleblowers

In 2024 attention turned to Boeing's supply chain when [[josh-dean|Joshua Dean]], a former quality auditor at Boeing supplier [[spirit-aerosystems|Spirit AeroSystems]], died suddenly at age 45 after a fast-spreading infection.[3] Dean had been one of the first whistleblowers to allege that Spirit leadership ignored manufacturing defects on the 737 MAX, giving a deposition in a shareholder lawsuit and filing a complaint with the [[federal-aviation-administration|Federal Aviation Administration]] over alleged "serious and gross misconduct by senior quality management of the 737 production line."[3]

## Barnett death and the March 2024 media cycle

The Hill reported in March 2024 that [[john-barnett|John Barnett]], a 32-year Boeing veteran and prominent whistleblower, was found dead in his truck in a Charleston hotel parking lot of an apparent self-inflicted wound while in the city to be questioned for a long-running retaliation suit against the company.[4] Barnett's 2019 allegations claimed overworked employees at the South Carolina plant frequently fitted substandard parts on planes and that faulty oxygen systems could leave as many as one in four masks inoperative; Boeing denied the claims, though a [[federal-aviation-administration|Federal Aviation Administration]] follow-up found more than 50 untraceable "non-conforming" parts lost in the company's system.[4]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described a rapid acceleration of anti-Boeing coverage — emergency landings, falling tires — that had cratered the company's stock and reputation and made many Americans anxious to fly.[4] He proposed a "Bicameral approach" to the Boeing stories: either the enemy is trying to get out ahead of something, or its attempts to do so are being thwarted.[4]

## 2024 leadership overhaul

On Monday, March 25, 2024, Boeing abruptly announced it was overhauling its leadership amid its most significant safety crisis in years, including the departure of chief executive [[dave-calhoun|Dave Calhoun]] at the end of that year.[5] The company had been under mounting pressure from regulators, airlines and passengers over its response to a January 2024 incident in which a panel blew off a 737 Max 9 aircraft midair during an Alaska Airlines flight.[5] The episode renewed concerns about the safety and quality commitments of a company widely considered a prized American institution, five years after two 737 Max 8 crashes killed nearly 350 people in total.[5] [[stan-deal|Stan Deal]], head of the commercial airplanes division, retired immediately and was replaced by chief operating officer [[stephanie-pope|Stephanie Pope]].[5] Badlands commentary read the shake-up through the frame of diversity, equity and inclusion programs damaging the aviation industry, and asked whether new management would make Boeing a symbol of corporate recovery from what the writer called the "woke mind virus."[5]

## Munitions held up in the 2024 Israel arms pause

In May 2024 the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration held up shipments of two Boeing-made precision weapons to Israel as a way of signalling displeasure with Israeli policy in Gaza.[6] The United States had not signed off on a pending sale of Boeing's Joint Direct Attack Munitions — both the munitions themselves and the kits that convert them into smart weapons — or of Small Diameter Bombs, according to six industry and congressional sources.[6] Rather than formally denying the sale, the administration withheld approvals and other steps in the transfer process, an approach one U.S. official described as taking "action through inaction."[6] See the [[2024-us-weapons-holdup-to-israel|2024 US weapons holdup to Israel]].[6]

## 2025 St. Louis defense workers' strike

About 3,200 Boeing machinists at the company's St. Louis-based defense unit walked off the job early on Monday, 4 August 2025, halting production of flagship defense products including the F/A-18 Super Hornet and the F-15EX.[7] Members of the [[international-association-of-machinists-and-aerospace-workers|International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers]] had voted on the preceding Sunday to reject Boeing's latest contract offer, beginning the strike at midnight — the first such action for IAM District 837 since 1996.[7]

The St. Louis workforce also manufactures the Joint Direct Attack Munition, the MQ-25 tanker drone and the T-7 trainer, and is slated to build the sixth-generation F-47 fighter, for which Boeing won a contract in March 2025.[7] District 837 had rejected an earlier agreement on 28 July 2025; Boeing subsequently offered improvements to retirement benefits and rescinded an alternative workweek proposal opposed by workers, but did not raise wages above its initial proposal.[7] Union representative Tom Boelling said members "deserve a contract that reflects their skill, dedication, and the critical role they play in our nation's defense."[7]

Badlands commentary questioned the strike's rationale, arguing that bargaining over workweek structure and retirement is misjudged in an industry facing accelerating automation, artificial intelligence and robotics, and connected the dispute to McKinsey's "new operating model for a new world."[7]

## Attribution of the Alaska Airlines door plug blowout (January 2024)

In late January 2024 an industry source told The Seattle Times that the fuselage panel that blew off an [[alaska-airlines|Alaska Airlines]] 737 MAX 9 earlier that month had been removed for repair and then reinstalled improperly by Boeing mechanics on the Renton final assembly line.[8] If verified by the [[national-transportation-safety-board|National Transportation Safety Board]], the account would leave Boeing primarily at fault rather than supplier [[spirit-aerosystems|Spirit AeroSystems]], which had originally installed the plug into the fuselage in Wichita, Kansas.[8] The [[alaska-airlines-flight-1282-door-plug-blowout|door plug blew out]] of Flight 1282 as it climbed out of Portland on January 5, drawing fresh criticism of Boeing's quality control systems and safety culture.[8]

A separate anonymous whistleblower with apparent access to Boeing's manufacturing records for the specific airframe supplied extensive additional detail on an aviation website about how the plug came to be removed and mis-installed.[8] [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] seized on the insider's line that the error "speaks volumes about the quality culture at certain portions of the business," arguing that Boeing's cultural decline — which he linked to DEI — calls for a conscious return to a culture of exceptionalism, possibly through the parallel economy.[8]

## March 2024 United Airlines statement and whistleblower death

On March 18, 2024, [[united-airlines|United Airlines]] chief executive [[scott-kirby|Scott Kirby]] told passengers the airline was reviewing a series of aviation incidents involving its commercial jets, including Boeing-made aircraft, describing the incidents as unrelated but acknowledging they had sharpened the carrier's focus on safety.[9] Badlands contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] read the cluster as engineered rather than coincidental, invoking the [[operation-northwoods|Northwoods Memorandum]]'s "generated instances" language and arguing that a Boeing whistleblower had been "suicided," which in his view signaled a panicked establishment pursuing the [[great-reset|Great Reset]] agenda.[9]

## Saudi F-15EX export campaign (2025)

As reported by Aviation Week on November 16, 2025, Boeing again placed [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] at the center of its export strategy, promoting the [[f-15ex|F-15EX Eagle II]] as a candidate for the Kingdom's next fighter jet review.[10] Ahead of the Dubai Airshow, the company indicated that it aimed to secure new F-15EX orders in the Middle East and that Saudi Arabia remained a primary focus for both upgrades of existing F-15C/D airframes and possible new-build acquisitions.[10] The proposal competes against the European Eurofighter Typhoon and the Dassault Rafale as well as a potential [[f-35|F-35]] approval, and follows Boeing's first public acknowledgment of Saudi F-15EX interest at the World Defense Show in Riyadh in early 2024.[10]
