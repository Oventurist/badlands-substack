# Stuart W. Bowen Jr.

Stuart W. Bowen Jr. is an American lawyer and a Texas friend of President [[george-w-bush|George W. Bush]] who in 2004 was appointed Special Inspector General for Iraq Reconstruction, charged with investigating corruption and waste in Iraq.[1] In the Badlands Media article "Bad Actors," Joe Lange presents Bowen as an unusual figure in his narrative: an insider appointment who, contrary to the expectations of the man who appointed him, took the mandate seriously.[1]

## The missing Iraqi cash

The Boston Globe account quoted in the article describes how, not long after American forces defeated Saddam Hussein's government in 2003, caravans of trucks began arriving at Andrews Air Force Base carrying pallets of shrink-wrapped $100 bills withdrawn from Iraqi government accounts held in the United States.[1] The cash was loaded onto Air Force C-17 transports bound for Baghdad, where the Bush administration hoped it would provide a rapid infusion for Iraq's new government and battered economy.[1] Over the following year and a half, $12 billion to $14 billion was flown to Iraq in the airlift and an additional $5 billion was sent by electronic transfer; some estimates put the total as high as $20 billion.[1] What happened to the money after arrival became one of the great unanswered questions of the occupation.[1]

Lange asks why so much was moved physically rather than electronically and answers that cash is far harder to trace, concluding that the absence of controls was purposeful.[1] House Government Reform Committee investigators charged in 2005 that US officials "used virtually no financial controls to account for these enormous cash withdrawals once they arrived in Iraq," with evidence of substantial waste, fraud and abuse; millions of dollars were reportedly stuffed into gunnysacks and hauled on pickups to Iraqi agencies and contractors.[1] The head of the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] team working on Iraq's financial reconstruction said, "We did not know that Bremer was flying in all that cash... I can't see a reason for it."[1]

## Brick Tracker and the Lebanon bunker

Finding the money became first Bowen's job and then, in the Globe's description, his obsession.[1] After years in which billions could not be accounted for, his investigators achieved a breakthrough, discovering that between $1.2 billion and $1.6 billion had been stolen and moved to a bunker in rural Lebanon for safekeeping.[1] "I don't know how the money got to Lebanon," Bowen said. "If I knew that, we would have made more progress on the case."[1] He kept the discovery and the investigation — code-named Brick Tracker — secret, discussing it publicly only in the interviews the Globe published in 2014.[1]

The investigation was obstructed. When Bowen and his staff tried to move the search into Lebanon, they met resistance from the US embassy in Beirut: Bowen himself was not allowed to travel there, and two investigators who did travel were denied embassy permission to see the bunker.[1] His staff met Lebanon's prosecutor general, who initially agreed to cooperate and later decided against it, while the Iraqi government made no attempt to retrieve the money and kept information about the bunker secret.[1] Bowen expressed frustration that neither the Bush administration outside his office nor the Obama administration pursued the lead after his investigators briefed the [[cia|CIA]] and the [[fbi|FBI]], and he was told one reason officials had not acted was "because it was Iraqi money stolen by Iraqis."[1] "Billions of dollars have been taken out of Iraq over the last 10 years illegally," he said, adding that the case could not be closed "for reasons beyond our control."[1]

## Lange's reading

Lange argues Bush installed Bowen expecting a friendly whitewash that would make congressional investigations of Iraq war waste disappear, but that "Mr. Bowen was actually a man of character, and put his country over his friendship."[1] The Lebanon destination is treated as the key revelation, Lebanon being both the base of Hezbollah and, in Lange's account, the place where [[prince-alwaleed-bin-talal|Prince Alwaleed bin Talal]] was raised and retains extensive family connections.[1] The "Iraqi money stolen by Iraqis" line is characterised as the narrative manufactured to close down inquiry once Congress and the public learned billions had gone missing.[1] By 2011 the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] and the Iraqi government claimed to have accounted for all but $6 billion of the missing funds — a result Lange treats as an admission of failure that also depends on believing the audit.[1]
