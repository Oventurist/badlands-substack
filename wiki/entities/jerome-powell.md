# Jerome Powell

Jerome Powell became Chairman of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] in 2018, having been elevated to the post by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Rate normalization under Trump

Badlands Media commentary describes Powell as having begun raising interest rates aggressively upon taking over, to Trump's displeasure.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) A roughly 20% stock market decline followed in the fall of 2018, which led to rate cuts in 2019 and a recovery in equities.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] writes that Trump understood rates needed to rise but insisted the process had to be gradual, adding that Trump "was right, as he always seems to be."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Covid-era money printing

When the Covid lockdowns were imposed in early 2020, Carlson holds that Trump forced the Fed to print money at a rate never previously seen in order to save the economy, knowing that massive [[inflation|inflation]] would follow — an act Carlson frames as fighting the [[cabal|Cabal]] with its own weapon.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## End of term and succession

Powell's term as Chair ends in May 2026.[2] In January 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] stated that a decision had been made regarding a nominee to succeed him, though no formal nomination had been announced.[2] Badlands contributor Burning Bright wrote that Trump had been publicly leaning on the Fed to supercharge the "Golden Age" economy since before assuming office, that the Fed had stalled, and that Trump's mortgage buy-back program was the first in an expected rapid cascade of moves demonstrating that he does not need Powell.[2]

## Announcement of a successor (January 2026)

Speaking at the premiere of *Melania* on January 29, 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he would announce a replacement for Powell as Fed Chair the following morning; Powell's term as chair runs to May 15, 2026.[3] The shortlist, vetted by Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]], comprised [[kevin-warsh|Kevin Warsh]] — widely regarded as the frontrunner and reported to have met Trump at the [[white-house|White House]] — [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]], [[christopher-waller|Christopher Waller]], and [[rick-rieder|Rick Rieder]].[3] All of the candidates were described as supportive of cutting interest rates, the central point of contention between Trump and Powell; Trump has claimed Powell's policies obstructed his economic strategy.[3]

## March 2026: succession, subpoenas, and the question of departure

By mid-March 2026, Powell's term as Federal Reserve Chair was set to end on May 15, and President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was expected to nominate [[kevin-warsh|Kevin Warsh]] to succeed him amid escalating tensions over interest-rate policy and Fed independence.[4] Trump had repeatedly and publicly criticized Powell over interest rates and broader monetary policy, a fight ongoing since the President's second inauguration.[4]

In a related development, Federal Judge [[james-boasberg|James "Jeb" Boasberg]] dismissed the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]]'s Federal Reserve subpoenas on a Friday in March 2026.[4] The DOJ had sought documents concerning the central bank's building renovation and Powell's allegedly false testimony about it.[4] Boasberg wrote that he was "left with no credible reason to think that the Government is investigating suspicious facts as opposed to targeting a disfavored official," and that "it is hard to see the renovations and testimony as anything other than a convenient pretext" for "another, unstated purpose: pressuring Powell to knuckle under."[4]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that rather than quietly running out his term, Powell remained a main character in at least four active storylines: Trump's demand for immediate rate cuts; Boasberg's characterization of him as a political target, which by extension accuses the administration of weaponizing government; Senator [[thom-tillis|Thom Tillis]] acting as Powell's political "security detail" by refusing to confirm a successor unless the case is dropped; and court filings suggesting Powell may refuse to leave his post in May.[4] Citing Barron's, she noted that Powell's lawyer told federal prosecutors in January that the chair believes he would remain on the central bank's board of governors past the expiration of his chairmanship if the criminal investigation into him stays open.[4]

Ashe speculated that the third and fourth threads may be linked, raising the question of a conspiracy in which Powell coordinates with certain senators to remain in place, decline rate cuts, and obstruct the President's fiscal agenda.[4] Drawing an analogy to intelligence assets in the television series *Homeland*, she suggested an alternative reading in which Powell is a "secret white hat" whose continued activity spotlights the true targets of disclosure — while registering ethical objections to the use of secret white hats generally, and closing with the question of "what color does Powell's hat need to be to permanently end the Fed?"[4]

## Opening of the criminal probe (January 2026)

In January 2026 the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] opened a criminal investigation into Powell, issuing grand jury subpoenas over claims that he misled [[congress|Congress]] about a $2.5 billion Federal Reserve headquarters renovation project.[5] Powell called the probe politically motivated pressure on monetary policy and vowed to resist it.[5] Badlands grouped the [[powell-renovation-criminal-investigation|renovation investigation]] with Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]]'s dark-money NGO probes and the hemispheric anti-cartel campaign as parts of a single convergent enforcement posture.[5]

## Public confirmation and congressional reaction

Powell publicly confirmed the criminal investigation in a statement issued on Sunday, January 11, 2026, concerning his congressional testimony about the renovations to the Federal Reserve's headquarters; no corresponding press release about the probe appeared on the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] website.[6] Several Republican lawmakers responded publicly, and the [[white-house|White House]] denied any involvement in the probe, while Powell's term as chair was noted as ending in May.[6]

Senator [[thom-tillis|Thom Tillis]] criticized the investigation and said he would oppose confirmation of new Federal Reserve nominees while it remained ongoing.[6] House Financial Services Chair [[french-hill|French Hill]] offered a character defense, recalling that he had known Powell since the two worked together at Treasury during the George H.W. Bush administration and describing him as forthright, candid, and "a person of the highest integrity."[6]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the probe had "all the right people in a tizzy," treating the Republican defensiveness as evidence of institutional capture rather than exculpation.[6] She argued that the objective went beyond prosecuting Powell for allegedly lying under oath, framing him as having dedicated his career to expanding global communism, picking economic winners and losers while claiming impartiality, and invoking independence and public trust while embodying the opposite — with the ultimate aim being to "end the Fed."[6]

## January 2026 FOMC meeting and criminal subpoenas

Following the January 2026 Federal Open Market Committee meeting, the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] left its benchmark interest rate unchanged, announcing neither a change in policy stance nor forward guidance signaling an imminent move.[7] Powell held a press conference after the meeting — his first since reports emerged of a [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] investigation related to his congressional testimony about the Federal Reserve's headquarters renovation project and allegations that he lied during that testimony.[7]

Federal prosecutors issued criminal subpoenas connected to that investigation, and reports indicated the Fed had failed to comply with them.[7] Powell's term as Federal Reserve Chair ends on May 15, 2026.[7]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that Powell's alternation between halting cuts and outright freezes had left the American economy in a holding pattern between the old system and a replacement, and predicted that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] would keep Powell in place through the end of his term precisely in order to cultivate public mandate for dissolving the institution he represents.[7] In that reading, Powell's successor would "push the throttle down," accelerating a final crack-up boom while gold, silver, [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] and stablecoin rails absorb the transition.[7]

## Succession shortlist (January 2026)

With Powell's term as chair of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] ending in May 2026, Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] said in early January that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had narrowed the field of possible successors to four and would announce a decision that month.[8] The candidates were [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]], former chair of the Council of Economic Advisers and then National Economic Council director, known for pro-growth fiscal policy, deregulation and a more accommodative monetary stance aligned with executive priorities; [[kevin-warsh|Kevin Warsh]], a former Fed governor emphasizing Fed credibility, financial stability and skepticism of prolonged unconventional policy; [[christopher-waller|Christopher Waller]], a sitting governor associated with the Fed's inflation-fighting stance; and [[rick-rieder|Rick Rieder]], the [[blackrock|BlackRock]] executive overseeing global fixed income.[8]

The incoming chair was expected to take office as US economic growth continued at a moderate pace, supported by tax cuts, ongoing investment in AI and technology, and reduced tariff uncertainty, even as political pressure on the Fed intensified amid a crisis of public trust in the institution.[8]

## May 2024 press conference

At the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s May 1, 2024 meeting, the central bank held interest rates at a 23-year high as inflation continued to delay the anticipated timing of a rate cut.[9] Powell indicated during the accompanying press conference that policymakers believed it was unlikely they would raise rates again in this cycle, a remark that briefly lifted stocks before the rally faded and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq closed 0.3 percent lower.[9]

## November 2023 hawkish remarks and bond market turmoil

On Thursday, November 9, 2023, a double-whammy of a disastrous 30-year Treasury auction and a considerably more hawkish-than-expected tone from Powell sent bonds and stocks sharply lower while the dollar soared, in what [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] described as a "crisis brewing" in credit markets even as crypto rallied.[10] Powell's remarks broke a multi-decade winning streak in the S&P 500 and Nasdaq; weakness hit at the cash open after overnight gains, the auction's spike in yields pushed stocks lower, and Powell's comments drove them to the day's lows just as dip-buyers stepped in.[10] Commentators noted the irony that Powell's earlier FOMC statement — that the market was tightening financial conditions on the Fed's behalf — had itself prompted a dramatic easing of those conditions.[10]

## Final Jackson Hole speech as chairman (August 2025)

Powell was scheduled to speak on Friday, August 22, 2025 in what NPR described as his final appearance as Federal Reserve chair at the high-profile meeting in Jackson Hole, Wyoming.[11] The speech came as the central bank faced mounting pressure from the [[white-house|White House]] to lower interest rates, three and a half weeks before the Fed's next rate-setting meeting, and as the wide-ranging tariffs President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] imposed by executive order on April 2 began taking shape after delays and adjustments.[11] Powell was expected to address the economic outlook and the central bank's longer-term balancing act between fighting inflation and unemployment; Joe Brusuelas, U.S. chief economist at RSM, cautioned that he "wouldn't hold my breath waiting for Jay Powell to tip his hand on whether the Fed is going to cut rates" in September.[11]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] asked "who watches the watchmen?", describing the establishment framing of the speech as a careful balancing act between taming inflation still above the 2% target and bolstering a weakening labor market in which July's jobs report showed fewer additions than expected and downward revisions erased prior gains.[11] He argued Powell was trapped: raising rates would deepen the jobs slowdown and risk a deflationary slump, while lowering them would spark inflation amid tariff pressures even as it juiced asset valuations and delivered a market surge aligned with Trump's "Golden Age" promises.[11] In this reading, rate cuts would prime the system for a full monetary reset, flooding liquidity into a debt-riddled structure until it forced a shift toward sound money and severed central bankers' control over value creation.[11]

The commentary tied the episode to the [[lisa-cook|Lisa Cook]] controversy as a key fracture point, to the fast-tracking of [[stephen-miran|Stephen Miran]] for a Fed board vacancy, and to Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]]'s anticipation of a "global economic reordering."[11] Badlands contributor GMONEY added that the Abolish the IRS movement "can only succeed when enough of us stop voluntarily participating in outright theft."[11]

## 60 Minutes interview, February 2024

In an interview with CBS's [[60-minutes|60 Minutes]] recorded on Thursday, February 1, 2024 and broadcast the following Sunday evening, Powell told correspondent Scott Pelley that Americans might have to wait beyond March for the central bank to begin cutting interest rates, as officials looked for more economic data confirming that inflation was heading toward 2%.[12] He said the "danger of moving too soon is that the job's not quite done, and that the really good readings we've had for the last six months somehow turn out not to be a true indicator of where inflation's heading."[12] Powell added, "We don't think that's the case," but said "the prudent thing to do is to just give it some time and see that the data continue to confirm that inflation is moving down to 2% in a sustainable way."[12] He judged it unlikely that the Federal Open Market Committee would reach that level of confidence by its March 19–20 gathering, echoing remarks he had made at a press conference the preceding Wednesday.[12]

Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the episode primarily as narrative warfare rather than economics, arguing that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had executed a "bluff charge" at the Fed by predicting it would cut rates during the election cycle to manufacture a faux economic rebound benefiting [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], while noting the hypocrisy that Trump had himself pressured the Fed to cut rates during his own term.[12]

## July 2025 call for a congressional investigation

On July 2, 2025, [[bill-pulte|Bill Pulte]], director of the [[federal-housing-finance-agency|Federal Housing Finance Agency]], publicly called on [[congress|Congress]] to investigate Powell, alleging that his recent Senate testimony about the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s planned headquarters renovations was "deceptive."[13] In a post on X, Pulte said he was asking Congress to investigate "Chairman Jerome Powell, his political bias, and his deceptive Senate testimony, which is enough to be removed 'for cause,'" without providing evidence for the claim that Powell made inaccurate statements.[13] Pulte had separately urged Powell to resign and, as head of a major housing regulator, pressed him to lower interest rates on the grounds that high borrowing costs were harming Americans.[13]

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], a long-standing critic of Powell's refusal to cut rates more quickly, amplified the FHFA chief's call for the Fed chair's resignation on the evening of July 2.[13] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] endorsed Pulte's criticism, citing the Fed's reported plan to spend $2.5 billion renovating its building and arguing that "it's past time to end The Fed," with "step one" being an end to Powell's tenure.[13]

## National Capital Planning Commission gambit (July 2025)

In July 2025 the National Pulse reported that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] appeared set to use a little-known federal entity — the [[national-capital-planning-commission|National Capital Planning Commission]] — in a renewed effort to oust Powell or force the central bank chief to yield to pressure to slash interest rates.[14] Trump had quietly replaced a number of Biden-era appointees on the commission with key figures from his [[white-house|White House]], a maneuver compared to President Richard Nixon's campaign to push his Fed chairman, Arthur Burns, into rate cuts.[14] Because the commission holds authority over government buildings, including the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s headquarters, the move was read as a vehicle for a negative media campaign against Powell over a lavish renovation of the central bank's offices that had far exceeded its approved budget.[14]

## September 2024 rate cut

On September 18, 2024, Powell's [[federal-open-market-committee|Federal Open Market Committee]] voted 11 to 1 to cut the benchmark rate by 50 basis points to a target range of 4.75%–5.0%, the first cut in more than four years, with only 9 of 113 surveyed economists having expected a move that large.[15] Governor [[miki-bowman|Miki Bowman]] dissented in favor of a 25 basis point cut, the first dissent by a Fed governor since 2005.[15] Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that while easing itself had been universally anticipated, only about 8% of economists believed Powell would "cave to the current regime's bully pulpit power" and cut so dramatically less than two months before the November election.[15] See [[september-2024-fed-rate-cut|September 2024 Federal Reserve Rate Cut]].

## Erosion of the Republican firewall (July 2025)

During Trump's first term, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] publicly badgered Powell over the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s refusal to lower interest rates, at one point declaring the chair had "No guts, no sense, no vision!"[16] At that time GOP lawmakers served as a firewall against the president's attacks on the central bank; then-Senator [[pat-toomey|Pat Toomey]] of Pennsylvania said he was "grateful" for Powell's effective management of borrowing costs.[16]

By July 2025, six months into Trump's second term, that legislative check was showing signs of degradation.[16] A small but growing contingent of Republicans had become comfortable demanding Powell's ouster after the Fed declined to adjust interest rates four times over the course of the year, and they were using the expensive renovations of two buildings at the Fed's historic headquarters as leverage.[16]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that he did not believe Trump would fire Powell, nor that he wanted to, arguing that Powell was acting as a barrier and, more importantly, "a bridge to the Golden Age."[16]

## "Sound and resilient" remark (May 2023)

At the FOMC press conference on May 3, 2023 — held as the Fed hiked rates another 25 basis points — Powell stated that the US banking system was "sound and resilient," a claim [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] described as an open lie to the American people given that more than half a trillion dollars in bank failures had occurred in the preceding weeks, making the episode worse than the global financial crisis.[17] Hours later, Bloomberg reported that [[pacwest-bancorp|PacWest Bancorp]] was weighing a sale, breakup or capital raise, and its shares cratered roughly 60 percent.[17] [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued the Fed's rhetoric was "off-base" and that Powell was likely describing not the visible financial system but the "hidden agreements and trusts" behind it, framing the collapse as a controlled demolition preceding a [[central-bank-digital-currency|CBDC]] rescue.[17]

## September 2024 50 basis-point cut

On September 18, 2024 Powell's Federal Open Market Committee delivered a 50 basis-point rate cut accompanied by dovish dot-plot projections, a move that unsettled markets: equities closed lower while the dollar and Treasury yields rose.[18] Powell justified the decision by saying "the US economy is in a good place and our decision today is designed to keep it there," and, asked whether political considerations factored into Fed decisions two months before a presidential election, answered: "We just don't do that."[18]

The size of the cut drew criticism from Republican Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota, who said that "25 basis points would've received minor grumblings, but the claim rings hollow with 50 basis points," while [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] and Congresswoman Maxine Waters praised the [[federal-reserve|Fed]] for demonstrating independence.[18] Badlands commentary characterized a crisis-scale cut delivered at record highs for stocks and home prices as an attempt to seed economic disaster for an incoming [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration, noting the Fed had made an identical cut exactly 17 years earlier ahead of the 2008 crisis.[18]

## December 2023 dovish pivot

At the December 13, 2023 FOMC meeting, Jerome Powell presided over a pivot from the Federal Reserve's pause to a projected rate-cutting trajectory for 2024, signaled through the committee's "dot plot" and delivering what markets had already priced in.[19] The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at a record high in response, while the session was described as a bloodbath for hedge funds.[19] Critics noted that Powell said essentially nothing about the massive easing of financial conditions — the tightening of which had been the stated rationale for pausing at the September meeting — and made no attempt to walk back market exuberance, with ZeroHedge asking whether Powell had received a "'Biden-esque' tap on the shoulder" ahead of an election year.[19]

## Tariff inflation warnings (April 2025)

In the aftermath of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s April 2025 tariffs, Powell publicly flagged the inflationary impact of the levies even as bond markets rallied on recession fears.[20] [[kathryn-kaminski|Kathryn Kaminski]] of [[alphasimplex-group|AlphaSimplex Group]] argued that investors focused solely on weaker growth were ignoring Powell's comments at their own peril, and that the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] faced a difficult situation in which it would likely need to cut rates on growth weakness while inflation complicated that path.[20]


## Trump Removal Speculation (April 2025)

In April 2025 speculation intensified that [[donald-trump|President Trump]] would attempt to fire Powell as [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] chair before his term expired in May 2026, with Trump publicly deriding him as "Mr. Too Late [to lower interest rates], a major loser."[21] Commentary in The New Republic argued that such a removal would not be truly unprecedented — although no president had ever fired a Fed chair, Trump had already removed roughly a dozen officials at nominally independent agencies including the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the Federal Trade Commission, the National Labor Relations Board, the Federal Election Commission, the Office of Special Counsel, the Merit Systems Protection Board, the Federal Labor Relations Authority and the [[consumer-financial-protection-bureau|Consumer Financial Protection Bureau]] — and predicted the Supreme Court would permit it.[21] Markets reacted where they had not to earlier firings: after [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]] said the administration was still studying options for removing Powell, the dollar index collapsed to a fresh three-year low in thin Asian holiday trading while gold hit an all-time high and Bitcoin spiked.[21] Hassett accused the Powell Fed of acting politically, raising rates after Trump's first election and cutting them just before the 2024 vote while staying silent on what he called textbook inflationary spending under Joe Biden.[21] Badlands commentator Jon Herold argued the feud was self-resolving: if Powell would not cut rates, Trump's browbeating and firing threats would crash the dollar low enough to deliver financial easing anyway.[21]

## July 2025 firing speculation

In mid-July 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] privately indicated that he was on the verge of firing Powell as [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] chair, rattling Wall Street and renewing questions over the US central bank's independence.[22] The reporting placed the episode alongside Trump's simultaneous effort to quell the [[epstein-files|Epstein files]] uproar among his base.[22]

## June 2024 FOMC decision and one-cut projection

On June 12, 2024, the Federal Open Market Committee announced that it would hold interest rates steady as the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] continued working to bring inflation down to its 2 percent target.[23] The accompanying Summary of Economic Projections showed the committee penciling in just one interest rate cut for 2024, with further cuts expected in 2025.[23] At the Wednesday press conference Powell cautioned that the projections should be viewed as projections only, subject to change on upcoming economic data, and said it was "plausible" that a rate cut could come as soon as September, adding, "We want to gain further confidence. Certainly, more good inflation readings will help with that."[23]

Badlands contributor Burning Bright described the decision and the preceding CPI print as the latest in a series of "Kobayashi Marus" — no-win scenarios — facing the establishment and the Biden administration on economic messaging, arguing that officials were pitching a slowdown in the rate of price increases as good news while ignoring the underlying problem of the money itself and its custodial banks.[23] The commentary held that continued hikes would intensify the stagflationary pressure cooker while cutting risked a "blow-off top of inflationary combustion" capable of tanking what remained of the dollar, all against the backdrop of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] making inroads with [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] maximalists and promising across-the-board tax cuts.[23]


## April 2025 tariff shock

Powell's scheduled Friday, April 4, 2025 remarks became a focal point for markets after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[liberation-day-tariffs|"Liberation Day" tariffs]] triggered the worst one-day equity declines since 2020.[24] Investor's Business Daily reported that the March employment report and the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] chairman were both "on tap Friday," with traders looking to Powell for signals on how the central bank would respond to a tariff shock that had raised simultaneous fears of recession and renewed inflation.[24] Currency markets reflected the same uncertainty: the dollar struggled to regain footing against a safe-haven yen near a six-month peak, having bounced off six-month troughs to the euro and sterling, as investors weighed the outlook for monetary easing.[24]

## April 2025 clash with Trump over rate cuts

In April 2025, Powell became the target of a sustained barrage of posts by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] on Truth Social after Powell delivered a speech at the Economic Club of Chicago in which he was not enthusiastic about the president's "Liberation Day" tariff strategy and framed a dilemma between taming inflation and increasing economic growth.[25] Trump posted that the European Central Bank was expected to cut interest rates for a seventh time while "'Too Late' Jerome Powell of the Fed, who is always TOO LATE AND WRONG," had issued "another, and typical, complete 'mess'" of a report; he argued that oil and grocery prices were down and the United States was "getting RICH ON TARIFFS," concluding that "Powell's termination cannot come fast enough!"[25] Commentators asked whether Trump could actually remove Powell as [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] chair, with Breitbart describing Powell as "basically a lame duck."[25]

Badlands analyst Burning Bright argued that the escalation was deliberate and timed to the trade war storyline, contending that Powell holds no genuine position of power but occupies a central narrative one, and that Trump was using him to expose the role of the fiat system and its centralized controllers to a broader public.[25] In this reading, Trump, [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] and [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]] appeared unconcerned with short-term economic strife because their end game was a complete reordering of global trade predicated on a redefinition of valuation itself, and no tariff or trade deal would matter unless the central bank were first brought to heel.[25]

## January 2025 response to Trump rate pressure

At a Washington press conference on Wednesday, January 29, 2025, after the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] held interest rates steady, Powell declined to comment on President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s call for lower rates, saying it would be "inappropriate" to respond.[26] He told reporters that "the public should be confident that we'll continue to do our work as we always have" and that the Fed would continue to "use our tools to achieve our goals," and said he had had "no contact" with Trump since the president's public demand the previous week.[26]

## Eccles Building renovation dispute (July 2025)

In July 2025 the White House financial watchdog attacked Powell over the [[eccles-building-renovation|$2.5 billion revamp]] of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s Washington headquarters and warned that construction could be halted.[27] In a letter obtained by the [[new-york-post|New York Post]], [[office-of-management-and-budget|Office of Management and Budget]] Director [[russ-vought|Russell T. Vought]] criticized the project, which public disclosures showed had run $700 million over budget.[27] The Post had reported in April that the Fed was pushing ahead with the overhaul despite mounting losses, prompting critics to liken the central bank's leadership to French royalty building a "Palace of Versailles."[27]

## Shadow Fed chair speculation (June 2025)

In late June 2025, President Donald Trump said he would announce his pick to succeed Powell "very soon," even though Powell had eleven months remaining in his term.[28] CNN reported that naming a successor that far in advance would be unprecedented in the Federal Reserve's 111-year history and would effectively create a [[shadow-fed-chair|"shadow" Fed chair]] — an idea first floated by Scott Bessent before he became Treasury secretary.[28] Trump's frustration stemmed from the Fed's refusal to lower interest rates, and former Fed officials and academics warned that the maneuver could undermine Powell and deepen economic uncertainty.[28]

## White House meeting on interest rates (May 2025)

Powell was summoned to the [[white-house|White House]] on May 29, 2025, for a closed-door meeting with [[donald-trump|President Trump]], his first visit there during Trump's second term.[29] Powell's office said the agenda was "to discuss economic developments, including for growth, employment, and inflation," and both sides said the meeting came at the president's invitation.[29] Trump told Powell in person what he had been saying publicly for months — that the [[federal-reserve|Fed]]'s refusal to cut interest rates was a "mistake" damaging the economy — but Powell stayed the course.[29] The Board of Governors had voted unanimously in early May to hold the primary credit rate at 4.5%, meaning rates for lenders and consumers would not move in the near term.[29]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the standoff as a long-anticipated "pincer" in which Trump traps the Fed between raising and lowering rates, arguing that with inflation metrics cooling Powell now had to cut or else reveal that he was "at war with the American economy."[29]

## "Too Late" and the GENIUS Act

Badlands commentary in July 2025 reinterpreted President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s long-running nickname for Powell — "Too Late," used for months in the context of interest rate policy — in light of the [[genius-act|GENIUS Act]], signed into law on July 18, 2025.[30] The argument held that fully reserved, Treasury-backed U.S. stablecoins would stabilize and decentralize the dollar, rendering the money-printing function of central banks obsolete, and that Powell and his central banking counterparts had been "too late to stop the one move they never saw coming."[30] The same commentary declared that central bank digital currencies were "dead."[30]

## December 2023 dovish reversal

Under Powell's chairmanship the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] held rates steady on December 13, 2023 and signaled that the tightening cycle of the prior two years had ended, with lower borrowing costs expected in 2024 — a message markets read as decisively dovish, sending the S&P 500 up nearly 1.4% and pushing the 10-year Treasury yield to about 3.96%.[31] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] noted that Powell had signaled hawkish sentiment only two weeks earlier and suggested, citing analysts including [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]], that the reversal followed a phone call from the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration amid its retreat from "Bidenomics" messaging — behavior he characterized as partisan politicking or election interference.[31]

## July 2025: defense of the headquarters renovation

In a letter dated Thursday, July 17, 2025, Powell defended the Federal Reserve's $2.5 billion headquarters renovation budget against accusations from a senior Trump administration official who alleged mismanagement and potential legal violations.[32] "While periodic work has been done to keep these buildings occupiable, neither building has seen a comprehensive renovation since they were first constructed," Powell wrote of the Fed's Washington, D.C. headquarters, built in the 1930s.[32] He added that "the Board has made a small number of design changes to scale back or eliminate certain elements and has added no new elements," describing the changes as intended to simplify construction and reduce the likelihood of further delays and cost increases.[32]

The renovation became a Trump administration talking point amid the broader feud with the central bank over interest rates, after the original $1.9 billion price tag set in 2017 ballooned to $2.5 billion by 2023.[32] Badlands commentary argued that the story had been underplayed relative to Epstein coverage, called the sum "mental" and cause for public outrage, and criticized Powell for defending the project while much of the country awaited the abolition of his institution — urging prosecution of those who approved it rather than mere cancellation.[32]

## Search for a successor (August 2025)

In August 2025, Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] confirmed that the race to replace Powell as chair of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] had narrowed to 11 candidates, an array including past and present central bank officials, economists, a [[white-house|White House]] advisor, and several Wall Street market experts.[33] Bessent said he would begin meeting candidates shortly after Labor Day in order to bring down the list for presentation to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], calling the field "an incredible group."[33]

Although Powell's term as chair did not expire until May 2026, the White House was keen to advance the succession process as it pressed what it characterized as an urgent need for interest rate cuts.[33]

## July 2023 rate hike

The Badlands News Brief of July 27, 2023, headlined in part "Powell's Plight," covered the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s decision the previous day to raise its baseline interest rate range by 0.25 percent to 5.25-5.5 percent, a 22-year high and the central bank's 11th hike since March 2022, adopted unanimously by the [[federal-open-market-committee|Federal Open Market Committee]] under Powell's chairmanship.[34]

## Tariff warnings (May 2025)

In May 2025 Powell issued dire warnings about interest rates, the economy and President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s tariffs, saying the economy had been resilient and that it was smart to wait and see how conditions developed before making any moves.[35] "The tariff increases announced so far have been significantly larger than anticipated," he said, adding that if the announced increases were sustained they were "likely to generate a rise in inflation, a slowdown in economic growth, and an increase in unemployment."[35] He said that "given the scope and scale of the tariffs," the risks of higher inflation and higher unemployment had increased, and that if the tariffs were ultimately imposed at those levels the [[federal-reserve|Fed]] would see not further progress toward its goals but a delay in achieving them.[35]

## Succession speculation (August 2025)

Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] said on August 12, 2025 that Trump had a "very open mind" about Powell's successor and had even considered re-appointing [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] to the post, with the administration interviewing candidates and casting a "very wide net" ahead of the expiration of Powell's chairmanship in May 2026.[36] Badlands commentary held that Powell — nicknamed "Too Late" by Trump — was not going anywhere until Trump was done with him, because his tenure was seen as exposing the failings of the fiat central banking system.[36]


## "Fighting a ghost" (August 2024)

Amid the [[august-2024-global-market-selloff|August 2024 global market sell-off]], the investor [[cole-smead|Cole Smead]] told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" that Powell was "doing all he can" to understand and counteract the effects of massive federal deficit spending — some 7% of U.S. GDP — with monetary policy, but that the underlying difficulty was fiscal and was not ending.[37] Smead framed the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] under Powell as attempting to "fight a ghost" created by fiscal stimulus it had not itself produced, at a moment when critics were blaming the central bank for holding interest rates at two-decade highs rather than cutting them.[37]

## September 2025 rate cut debate

Following the Federal Reserve's decision to leave interest rates unchanged at its late-July 2025 meeting, market expectations for a September cut shifted.[38] Those favoring cuts by Powell argued that the risk of tariff-driven inflation was overblown and that threats to the job market mattered more, while opponents said it was too soon to conclude inflation would not spike and that recent data showed unemployment remained manageable.[38] Reporting noted little dispute that inflation had proven sticky and that cracks had appeared in the labor market: the Consumer Price Index showed inflation at 2.7% in June, matching November and up from an April low of 2.3%, while job losses picked up through 2025, including layoffs associated with the Department of Government Efficiency's efforts that spring.[38]

## "Is Jerome Powell a Patriot?" (April 2023)

In an April 3, 2023 essay for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] posed the question of whether Powell was secretly a "Patriot" working with [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] against what Carlson called the corrupt banking cabal, stressing that the title was a question rather than a statement and that the facts did not appear to add up to Powell working with the deep state.[39] Carlson prefaced the argument by saying he despised the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] — its founding, its creation of money "out of nothing," the inflation that follows, and the income tax created to pay the bankers — but insisted that the present Fed "isn't your father's Federal Reserve."[39]

Carlson recounted that Trump appointed Powell to head the Federal Reserve on February 5, 2018, and appointed [[john-c-williams|John C. Williams]] to lead the [[federal-reserve-bank-of-new-york|New York Fed]] two months later, and that Trump "couldn't jettison [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] from the Federal Reserve fast enough."[39] After his appointment Powell immediately began raising interest rates despite very low inflation, saying he feared inflation could eventually rise; after four hikes the S&P 500 fell roughly 20% by the end of 2018 and Trump publicly blamed Powell, which Carlson described as the first time he could remember a president criticizing the Fed.[39] Powell then cut rates three times in 2019, restoring the market — a sequence Carlson read in hindsight as "kayfabe," with Trump and Powell only pretending to be at odds.[39]

Powell was re-appointed for a second four-year term by [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] in May 2022 even though, Carlson wrote, Biden had made it known he did not want Powell to head the Fed and was pressured by Wall Street to keep him; coming off the Afghanistan withdrawal with low approval ratings, Biden feared Wall Street would crash the market if he replaced him.[39] Democrats had wanted Lael Brainard, who was aligned with Biden's climate agenda, while Powell had said climate would not be a top priority; Carlson concluded that Biden "wasn't allowed" to replace Powell.[39]

Carlson contrasted Powell's behavior under the two presidents: in Biden's first year inflation rose from 1.4% to 7.9%, yet six months into the presidency Powell called inflation "transitory" and declined to raise rates, despite having raised them under Trump when inflation was low.[39] Rate increases resumed in early 2022 at 0.25% shortly before his reappointment and jumped to 0.75% per month afterward, which Carlson took as evidence that Powell had held off to secure reappointment and was then "activated to take down the cabal banking system."[39]

The essay's core financial claim was that Powell's hikes — taking rates from 0.25% to 5% over a year — strengthened the US dollar (dollar futures from $98 to $103) while weakening the [[eurodollar|Eurodollar]] (futures from $99 to $95), a $9 relative swing that Carlson interpreted as deep state money being crushed and American money strengthened.[39] He noted that historically financial collapses have followed rate CUTS rather than hikes, citing cuts from 4.75% in September 2007 to 2% in April 2008 six months before the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 collapse]], and cuts from 6% in January 2001 to 3% by September 2001 before the 9/11 attacks.[39] Carlson added that Yellen, as Biden's Treasury Secretary, had "gone out of her way to undo any good that Powell has done" by using the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|US Treasury]] to counter Federal Reserve moves, slowing the process.[39] He asked why money-laundering banks were failing and why no US troops were being sent into a foreign war to bail out the financial system if Powell were working with the cabal.[39]

## The "Trump's puppet" thesis (January 2023)

In "Master and Commander," [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] characterised Powell's elevation to the chairmanship as a "genius move" by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]: by promoting a sitting governor to chairman in place of [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]], an Obama appointee, Trump both installed his own chair and freed an additional seat on the Board of Governors, bringing him to six of seven positions and near-total control of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]].[40] Lange concluded flatly that "Trump controls the Fed and Powell is now his puppet."[40]

As supporting evidence he cited a November 2019 report that Powell met Trump and Treasury Secretary [[steven-mnuchin|Steven Mnuchin]] at the [[white-house|White House]] in a previously unannounced meeting absent from the official calendar, to discuss the economy, growth, employment and inflation — the second face-to-face meeting between the two men, following a February dinner and subsequent phone calls, amid Trump's relentless public criticism of the central bank.[40] Lange found the choice of topic significant given that inflation was, in his account, unleashed on the Biden administration only after Trump left office.[40]

He further argued that Powell's post-2021 rate increases — faster and more aggressive than anybody expected — were a deliberate "boomerang" that nearly crashed the market under [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and would earn Democrats the blame for a recession, while simultaneously driving up the dollar and inflicting severe losses on foreign central banks.[40]
