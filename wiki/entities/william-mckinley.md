# William McKinley

William McKinley was a President of the United States who implemented his own tariff program and was assassinated.[1] [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes him as the last of the great republican peacemakers and an anti-imperialist of the highest order, and the last President to have fought in the Civil War.[2]

## Invocation in the 2025 tariff debate

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] cited a McKinley quotation on the eve of Supreme Court oral arguments over President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s tariffs, framing McKinley as a president who pursued a tariff program and "was assassinated for it," and casting the case as an attempt by the [[deep-state|Deep State]] to stop a tariff policy that its supporters argue would eliminate the need for an income tax and free the American economy from globalism.[1]

## The last Lincoln Republican

McKinley supported two complementary policies: internally, [[abraham-lincoln|Lincoln]]'s [[american-system|American System]] of protectionism, internal improvements, productive credit and African-American suffrage; and externally, the [[monroe-doctrine|Monroe Doctrine]] that had defined America's anti-imperial foreign policy since 1823.[2]

Speaking in 1901 at the Pan-American Exposition, which featured visions of rail grids connecting all of the Americas driven by national banking, protectionism and bilateral treaties, McKinley set out a vision of win-win cooperation: "The Tower of Light is the tower of peace and good will… Science, discovery, and industry are the great, immortal democrats whose teaching shall wipe out political boundaries, and heal national jealousies… We shall have all America united; and what America becomes is the prototype of what the world must be."[2]

In an earlier 1898 speech McKinley attacked British free trade and the obsession with cheap goods, declaring that "everything would be cheap and everybody would be cheap. I do not prize the word 'cheap.'… cheap merchandise means cheap men, and cheap men mean a cheap country," and concluding "we want labor to be well paid."[2]

## Foreign policy and the imperial turn

Ehret argues that while McKinley is often blamed for America's imperial turn, the reality was the opposite.[2] The Spanish-American War begun in 1898 was, in this account, launched unilaterally by [[theodore-roosevelt|Theodore Roosevelt]], who used a four-hour window as acting Undersecretary of the Navy to order Captain Dewey's Pacific fleet into a fight with Spain over the Philippines; McKinley had resisted the war hawks until that point but ultimately bent to the momentum.[2]

During the [[boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]], McKinley — like Russia's [[sergei-witte|Sergei Witte]] — worked to reject taking Chinese territory in the imperial free-for-all, producing fears in the British oligarchy that a US–Russia alliance was imminent.[2] In 1898 the American Ambassador to Russia, Ethan Allan Hitchcock, wrote to Secretary of State John Sherman outlining a US–China–Russian rail development program that McKinley fully supported, arguing that "Russian preferential friendship for our country… is as sincere, and well worth cultivating, as it is traditional."[2]

## Assassination and aftermath

McKinley's assassination on September 18, 1901 catapulted the Mahan-admiring Vice President Theodore Roosevelt into high office, and with it, Ehret argues, made [[alfred-thayer-mahan|Alfred Thayer Mahan]]'s worldview rather than [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]]'s the dominant American doctrine for the next 120 years.[2] Journalist Martin Sieff has linked the anarchist assassination of McKinley to the murder of Tsar [[alexander-ii|Alexander II]].[2] Ehret closes by asking whether President Donald Trump has the fortitude to accept a Russia–US–China alliance capable of reviving McKinley's American System.[2]
