# Serbia

Serbia is a Balkan state led as of 2025 by President [[aleksandar-vucic|Aleksandar Vučić]], and the site of a months-long anti-government protest movement that reached a boiling point in March 2025.[1]

## 2025 Belgrade demonstrations

On Saturday, March 15, 2025, it seemed "as if all of Serbia converged on Belgrade," as tens of thousands of protesters poured into the capital's central streets from all directions in an almost unbroken tide of umbrellas and placards, defying relentless rain and unseasonably humid air.[1] The demonstrations had been swelling for months and posed a growing threat to Vučić's government.[1] Dinko Gruhonjić, a university professor from Novi Sad and an early supporter of the movement, said that "fear has changed sides in Serbia" and that citizens who were no longer afraid now saw fear in the eyes of the government.[1]

The protests began in November 2024 after the canopy of the main railway station in Novi Sad, the center of Serbia's lush northern region, collapsed onto unsuspecting bystanders and killed 14 people on the spot, including children aged 9 and 5; another victim later succumbed to injuries, and several others underwent amputations.[1]

## Geopolitical framing

Badlands commentary noted the difficulty of identifying the sides in the confrontation: Vučić is billed as a populist but has been branded a ruthless autocrat by mainstream media, while the catalyzing station collapse was raised as a possible false flag.[1] Vučić himself accused foreign-funded NGOs of starting the revolution, a pattern the commentary linked to campaigns against Robert Fico in Slovakia and Irakli Kobakhidze in Georgia, and observers emphasized that he had been urging [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to force a Ukraine peace deal since before the 2024 U.S. election.[1]

## EU accession posture (April 2025)

In April 2025, Serbia's Acting Foreign Minister [[marko-djuric|Marko Djuric]] reaffirmed that European Union membership remained the country's strategic choice during a meeting with EU foreign policy chief [[kaja-kallas|Kaja Kallas]].[2] A Serbian government statement said Djuric stressed the country's firm commitment to its European path and its desire to contribute to peace, stability, and prosperity in Europe as a reliable partner, while expressing hope the EU would positively assess Serbia's integration efforts and pledging continued work to ease tensions in the Balkans.[2] Serbia was at the time an official candidate for EU membership alongside Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Türkiye, and Ukraine.[2] Badlands commentary characterized events in Serbia as an intelligence-community coup attempt fueled by [[usaid|USAID]] and [[european-commission|European Commission]]-funded "independent media," following the same playbook as the Maidan.[2]
