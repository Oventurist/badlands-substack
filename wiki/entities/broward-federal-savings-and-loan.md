# Broward Federal Savings and Loan

Broward Federal Savings and Loan of Sunrise, Florida was the thrift that became insolvent after lending [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]] and a partner about half the money toward the purchase of a $9 million office building, with the federal government ultimately repaying most of the loan.[1] The New York Times, as reported by the Los Angeles Times, called it an example of the poor lending practices that led to the thrift industry's troubles, while noting it involved no criminal behavior.[1]

In 1985 Bush, then 37, and his partner [[armando-codina|Armando Codina]] — through a partnership called 1390 Brickell — bought a Miami office building at that address for $9 million, using a $7 million mortgage from an insurance company and a $4.6 million loan from Broward Federal.[1] The surplus money was to be used for improvements and a "reserve account," a detail Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] flags as important.[1] The Broward loan was acquired by a third partner, [[j-edward-houston|J. Edward Houston]], who owned his own thrift, South Florida Savings, and defaulted when that institution went bankrupt and needed a bailout.[1]

Broward Federal became insolvent in 1988, and the federal government paid more than $4 million to make good the loan on the Miami property as part of the industry bailout; the failure would cost taxpayers around $235 million overall.[1] California Federal Bank of Los Angeles bought the failed thrift from federal regulators two years later.[1] Bush and Codina negotiated a settlement with regulators under which they repaid $505,000 and retained control of the building.[1]

Lange details the terms as a sweetheart deal: regulators ordered a reassessment that valued the $9 million building at only $6.5 million, just under the $7 million mortgage, giving the partners instant equity, while the $505,000 they nominally paid came out of the "reserve account" funded by the very loan they had defaulted on.[1] In his reading they literally did not pay a dime back to the government and kept the building.[1] The episode is presented as a perfect example of the corruption of the whole [[savings-and-loan-crisis|savings and loan scandal]].[1]
