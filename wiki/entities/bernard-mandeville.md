# Bernard Mandeville

Bernard Mandeville is described in the Badlands Media essay "In Defense of Benjamin Franklin" as the leading philosopher of the [[hellfire-club|Hell-Fire Club]] and the spiritual father of laissez-faire economics, asserting that "Do What Thou Wilst" should form the basis of all economic theory.[1]

## Private vices, public benefits

In his *Private Vices Public Benefits* (1714), Mandeville argued that a killer who steals money and spends it on a prostitute is doing a service to society, since she will use the money to buy new buttons for her dress, which employs a button maker, who feeds his family, and so on down the chain.[1] Mandeville defined human nature as fundamentally lustful and selfish, writing: "One of the greatest reasons why so few people understand themselves, is that most writers are always teaching men what they should be, and hardly ever trouble their heads with telling them what they really are… I believe man to be a compound of various passions, that all of them, as they are provoked and come uppermost, govern him by turns whether he will or no."[1]

His poem *The Grumbling Hive* dramatised the same thesis, describing a hive in which "every Part was full of Vice, / Yet the whole Mass a Paradise," and in which "The Worst of all the Multitude / Did Something for the common Good."[1] The essay asks what becomes of evil in such a scheme and answers that, for Mandeville, evil occurs only when nation states attempt to regulate or direct the economic behaviour of society according to a moral principle.[1]

## Political consequences

The essay reports that Mandeville's thesis was applied to deregulate the economy of England, which soon became driven by the [[south-sea-bubble|South Sea Bubble]], premised on rampant speculation that the author compares to the bubbles which transformed modern economies from industrial powerhouses into speculative globalized systems.[1] Quoting historian [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]], the article states that after George I took the throne in 1714 satanic cults proliferated among Britain's elite, the most notorious being the branches of the Hell-Fire Club, "modeled after Mandeville's doctrine that evil, vice, and corruption were the ideal means of the state's controlling its servants, or the drones of the hive."[1]

[[james-franklin|James Franklin]] is said to have returned from London in 1720 having acquired both a printing press and a familiarity with "the degenerate admirers of Bernard Mandeville."[1] Mandeville's outlook stood in direct contrast to the worldview of [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], whose approach to indiscriminate acts of improvement was shaped by [[cotton-mather|Cotton Mather]]'s *Essays to Do Good*.[1]
