# The Pentagon

The Pentagon — the U.S. Department of Defense — appears in the article chiefly through its delayed notification and non-deployment on the night of the Benghazi attack.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

Word of the attack reached the [[state-department|State Department]]'s Diplomatic Command Center immediately, but according to the House Select Committee report it took roughly an hour for that notice to reach the Pentagon.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) Department of Defense Chief of Staff Jeremy Bash nevertheless offered assistance to State that night, reporting that forces "are spinning up as we speak."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) Secretary of Defense [[leon-panetta|Leon Panetta]] testified he had identified "three distinct capabilities" by 6 p.m. and issued the order to deploy the identified assets by 7 p.m.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

Despite these steps no forces arrived. An anonymous squadron member at Aviano Air Base in northeastern Italy described planes being armed and a flight line full of personnel ready to go, waiting for an order that never came: "I know everything was spun up and nothing was done... At our level, we were doing everything we were supposed to be doing. At everybody else's level above us, it was political."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) [[africom|AFRICOM]] learned of the attack within thirty minutes and was preparing to respond, but was never given the order.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) The article treats the gap between orders issued and forces deployed as the core evidence of a [[dereliction-of-duty|dereliction of duty]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

## Use of labor movements abroad

A separate Badlands piece names the Department of Defense as one of the "deep government" bodies — alongside the [[cia|CIA]], the [[state-department|State Department]] and the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|NED]] — that exert influence through organized labor.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union) Though focused on military reconstruction after World War II, the department indirectly supported the anti-leftist labor campaign through the [[marshall-plan|Marshall Plan]]'s labor provisions, which conditioned aid on union reforms in recipient countries, and it provided cover through NATO-linked training programs that inculcated pro-Western ideologies in foreign labor leaders.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union)

Its role grew during the Vietnam era, when unions in Asia and Africa were used for "hearts and minds" work; the [[asian-american-free-labor-institute|Asian-American Free Labor Institute]] received DoD contracts to counter communist unions in the Philippines and Indonesia.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union) Alumni of the [[american-institute-for-free-labor-development|American Institute for Free Labor Development]] fed intelligence to the CIA and DoD ahead of [[brazilian-coup-1964|Brazil's 1964 coup]].[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union) In the modern period, the article says the department's objectives shaped [[solidarity-center|Solidarity Center]] activity in [[ukraine|Ukraine]] and Hong Kong, while AFRICOM coordinated with union programs in mineral-rich African states to favour U.S. mining contracts over Chinese ones.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-more-perfect-labor-union)

## Assessment of Chinese missile advances

A Pentagon finding reported by the [[new-york-times|New York Times]] in 1998 held that the reliability of China's nuclear missiles was significantly advanced in 1996 after scientists working for American space satellite companies provided Beijing with expertise on guidance systems.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1) That assessment noted that the technology needed to place a commercial satellite in orbit is similar to the technology that guides a long-range nuclear missile to its target.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1) The finding prompted several congressional committees to investigate whether the [[bill-clinton|Clinton]] administration's policy of exporting space satellite technology to China had helped China and other countries develop and use nuclear missiles.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1) Senator [[thad-cochran|Thad Cochran]] framed the resulting controversy as a failure of American policy-makers responsible for the transfer rather than of China.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1)

## Funding the Atlantic Council

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] names the Pentagon among the U.S. government bodies funding the [[atlantic-council|Atlantic Council]], alongside the [[state-department|State Department]], [[usaid|USAID]], the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] and several UK agencies.[4] Because the Council is also funded by the major Western energy firms, DeLarme argues those firms can lobby the Pentagon, the [[cia|CIA]] and the State Department for policies that would open [[iran|Iran]] to Western capital.[4]


## Syria and the seven-countries memo

Badlands Media asked why the Pentagon and the [[cia|CIA]] fund militant proxy groups in [[syria|Syria]].[5] It reported that while the CIA's [[timber-sycamore|Timber Sycamore]] program armed groups pressuring [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al-Assad]], the Pentagon's efforts were more focused on fighting [[isis|ISIS]], particularly through support for the predominantly Kurdish [[syrian-democratic-forces|Syrian Democratic Forces]].[5]

The article also cited General [[wesley-clark|Wesley Clark]]'s 2007 account of a memo he saw in the Pentagon after 9/11 outlining a plan to "take out seven countries in five years, starting with Iraq and then Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Somalia, Sudan, and finishing off with Iran."[5]

## Civilian industrial mobilization (2026)

A [[department-of-defense|Department of Defense]] effort to expand weapons production reached into the civilian industrial base in 2026, with outreach to automakers and manufacturers to scale up capacity for munitions, vehicles, and defense systems.[6] Reporting characterized this as a structural shift toward a sustained wartime production footing in which commercial manufacturing flexibility is repurposed for military supply chains, drawing on lessons from supply shortages earlier in the Russia–[[ukraine|Ukraine]] war that exposed weaknesses in Western defense readiness.[6]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the Pentagon was not merely replenishing stockpiles but actively courting America's automakers to convert civilian production lines into the backbone of a rebuilt arsenal for a war that would never arrive — in his phrase, the aftermath of a [[war-economy|war economy]] without the war.[6]

## OpenAI contract controversy (2026)

In March 2026 the Pentagon became the focus of a public dispute over military artificial intelligence after it announced a contract with [[openai|OpenAI]] to supply models for classified U.S. military operations, a deal reached only after negotiations with rival [[anthropic|Anthropic]] collapsed over that company's insistence on red lines banning domestic surveillance of Americans and lethal autonomous weapons.[7] Backlash from OpenAI employees and an open letter signed by nearly 900 workers at OpenAI and Google prompted a rewrite adding explicit prohibitions on intentional domestic surveillance of U.S. persons and excluding Pentagon intelligence agencies such as the NSA pending separate agreements.[7] The department stated that the tools remained limited to lawful military use through cloud access only.[7]

## Israeli espionage raised to "critical"

In June 2026, NBC News and [[new-york-times|The New York Times]] reported that the Pentagon's [[defense-intelligence-agency|Defense Intelligence Agency]] had elevated its assessment of the threat posed by Israeli espionage to a "critical" level, in a seven-page document with an accompanying chart evaluating Israeli intelligence-gathering capabilities.[8] The assessment allegedly identified Israeli efforts to gather information on U.S. administration deliberations concerning [[iran|Iran]] and [[lebanon|Lebanon]], naming [[steve-witkoff|Steve Witkoff]], [[elbridge-colby|Elbridge Colby]] and [[michael-dimino|Michael DiMino]] among officials targeted.[8]

The Pentagon declined comment on the reports, while a [[white-house|White House]] official told NBC News "the entire story is false" and the Israeli Embassy in Washington called the allegations politically motivated.[8] DiMino, sworn in at the Pentagon on January 20, 2025 as Deputy Assistant Secretary of War for the Middle East, is the principal policy advisor on defense and security issues across more than a dozen Middle Eastern countries.[8]

## Interceptor expenditure in the Iran war

Reporting in May 2026 indicated the Pentagon assessed that the United States fired more than 200 [[thaad|THAAD]] interceptors during the Iran war — roughly half the American inventory — along with large numbers of SM-3 and SM-6 naval missiles, while [[israel|Israel]] conserved more of its own stockpile.[9] Analysts said the conflict exposed a major vulnerability in Western missile-defense production capacity, since THAAD and Patriot systems are expensive and slow to replace, and critics argued the expenditure strained US readiness for other potential conflicts, particularly in the Indo-Pacific.[9] Supporters countered that the joint missile-defense effort prevented far greater regional escalation and civilian casualties, and Israel denied being dangerously low on interceptors, saying its operations were coordinated closely with Washington throughout.[9]

## AUKUS review and reversal (2026)
Earlier Pentagon review of the [[aukus|AUKUS]] pact generated concern among partner governments, which eased after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] reaffirmed US support; War Secretary [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]] said in June 2026 that Washington remained committed and was moving quickly to expand submarine capabilities in the Pacific.[10] See [[aukus-undersea-drone-program|AUKUS undersea drone program]].[10]

## Requested FBI search in a leak investigation (January 2026)

Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] said the January 14, 2026 [[fbi|FBI]] search of [[washington-post|Washington Post]] reporter [[hannah-natanson|Hannah Natanson]]'s Virginia home was carried out at the Pentagon's request, as part of a federal investigation into Pentagon contractor [[aurelio-perez-lugones|Aurelio Perez-Lugones]], who is accused of unlawfully retaining classified national defense information including allegedly taking home and printing sensitive intelligence.[11] Bondi stressed a zero-tolerance approach to leaks of classified information and said the individual suspected of the leak was "behind bars."[11]

## 2026 National Defense Strategy

In January 2026 the Pentagon released an unclassified [[2026-national-defense-strategy|National Defense Strategy]] that places defense of the U.S. homeland at the top of its priority list while calling for greater burden-sharing from allies as Washington reassesses its global military footprint.[12] The document ranks homeland defense above the Indo-Pacific and signals potential reductions to U.S. force posture in Europe and South Korea, while emphasizing allied contributions and expanded investment in the U.S. defense industrial base.[12]

Following the [[white-house|White House]]'s National Security Strategy of December 2025, the document references President [[donald-trump|Trump]] far more frequently than the 2022 edition despite being shorter, and highlights the [[golden-dome|Golden Dome]] missile defense initiative, counter-drone systems, a modernized nuclear deterrent, and an updated interpretation of the [[monroe-doctrine|Monroe Doctrine]].[12] Its stated priorities are homeland defense, deterring [[china|China]] in the Indo-Pacific "through strength, not confrontation," increased allied burden-sharing, and "supercharging" the defense industrial base.[12]

## Sustained campaign planning against Iran (February 2026)

U.S. defense and administration officials were reported in February 2026 to be planning for the possibility of a sustained, weeks-long military campaign against [[iran|Iran]] if President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] ordered such action, moving beyond limited strikes toward broader targeting of Iranian state and security infrastructure.[13] The planning came amid increased U.S. military deployments, including the arrival of an additional aircraft carrier in the Middle East, and ongoing U.S.–Iran diplomacy mediated by [[oman|Oman]].[13] Trump publicly suggested regime change in Iran would be desirable, while Israeli leaders called for dismantling Iranian nuclear capabilities.[13]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] cited the planning as evidence for claims of [[israel-lobby|Israel lobby]] influence over U.S. policy, questioning why Iran requires a new government and arguing there is no evidence Iran threatens the United States or [[israel|Israel]].[13] He noted Iran's strengthened ties with Russia and China and its central role in the [[north-south-transport-corridor|North-South Transport Corridor]], contending that economic entanglement deters kinetic conflict.[13]

## Iran contingency planning (February 2026)

During the early-2026 escalation, U.S. military officials were preparing for potential conflict with [[iran|Iran]] but were assessed as able only to carry out limited strikes, and the Pentagon moved additional air defense systems to bases across the Middle East to protect American forces and regional allies in the event of Iranian retaliation.[14] A senior Israeli military delegation led by [[israel-defense-forces|Israel Defense Forces]] Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. [[eyal-zamir|Eyal Zamir]] made a previously undisclosed visit to the Pentagon to present intelligence and discuss military options concerning Iran amid the growing U.S. presence in the region.[14]

## March 2026 messaging during the Iran war

As President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] told CBS News in March 2026 that the war with [[iran|Iran]] was "very complete" and largely over, the Pentagon posted on X that it had "Only Just Begun to Fight" with "no mercy" — a tone that contrasted sharply with Trump's insistence that the decision to end the war was entirely his own.[15] Badlands coverage also reported that the Pentagon, alongside the [[white-house|White House]], had confirmed and was investigating an incident in which an early Israeli strike on a school killed 175 girls under the age of 10.[15]

## February 2023 aerial object shootdowns

During the [[2023-north-american-aerial-object-shootdowns|February 2023 shootdowns]] of high-altitude objects over Alaska, Canada, and Lake Huron, the Pentagon shared little detail publicly about the objects.[16] [[nbc-news|NBC]] host [[chuck-todd|Chuck Todd]] criticized both the Pentagon and [[national-security-council|National Security Council]] spokesman [[john-kirby|Adm. John Kirby]] for a lack of transparency, noting that Kirby had not volunteered information about fighter jets being scrambled until a reporter asked.[16] A "Meet the Press" post stated that in both the Alaska and Canada cases "the White House and Pentagon missed opportunities for transparency."[16]

## DOGE at the Pentagon (February 2025)

In February 2025 Defense Secretary [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]] welcomed the [[doge|Department of Government Efficiency]] to the Pentagon, where [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] faced an accounting system that has never passed an audit and decades of rampant price gouging by defense contractors.[17] Defense policy experts said the department's $850 billion budget was in sore need of oversight, with investigators regularly uncovering billions of dollars of waste and the disjointed accounting system still unable to pass an audit.[17]

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] floated cutting the defense budget in half once things "settle down" with Russia and China — a sharp turn from Hegseth's statement earlier the same day that "nobody can or should test the extent of America's willingness to invest in national security."[17] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the Pentagon deployment as part of an accelerated, transparency-driven inversion of the [[hegelian-dialectic|Hegelian Dialectic]] across the administrative battlespace.[17]

## Shift to "long-term defense" in Ukraine (November 2023)

In November 2023 the Pentagon's public framing of the [[russian-invasion-of-ukraine|war in Ukraine]] shifted from battlefield gains toward "long-term defense requirements," a change that ZeroHedge noted began as it became clear Ukraine's counteroffensive was failing.[18] Defense Secretary [[lloyd-austin|Lloyd Austin]] made an unannounced visit to Kiev on November 20, 2023 — his second since the Russian invasion — telling President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] that the United States and its allies would continue to support "Ukraine's urgent battlefield needs and long-term defense requirements."[18] The trip came at an awkward moment, with Zelensky publicly complaining that artillery shell deliveries had decreased because stocks were being redirected to Israel, and with [[congress|Congress]] dragging its feet on President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s supplemental funding requests amid wrangling over the Israel-versus-Ukraine split.[18] The Austin visit coincided with a separate secretive trip by [[cia|CIA]] Director [[william-burns|William Burns]], which commentators read as evidence of alarm in Washington over battlefield conditions in a proxy war into which many tens of billions of dollars had been invested.[18]

## Red Sea deterrence dispute (December 2023)

During the escalating [[red-sea-shipping-crisis|Houthi campaign against commercial shipping]] in late 2023, US defense officials were reported to be frustrated that the Pentagon was being held back by the [[joe-biden|Biden]] White House from responding forcefully against [[houthis|Houthi]] positions in Yemen.[19] Politico reported that some current and former military officials were frustrated by the administration's initial response to the Houthis' Sunday attacks on ships, while top military brass pushed for a more forceful posture lest the Iranian proxies grow more emboldened.[19]

By late December container ships were coming under drone and missile attack on a daily basis, major liners were avoiding Red Sea transit, and *The Wall Street Journal* ran the headline "U.S. Naval Deterrence Is Going, Going, Maybe Even Gone."[19] No military response was ordered even when American warships in waters off Yemen came under direct attack, leaving Pentagon leaders describing themselves as handcuffed.[19] Analysts quoted in the coverage attributed the restraint to the president's desire to avoid antagonizing [[iran|Iran]] and keep global energy prices down ahead of the 2024 election, and warned it might already be too late to reestablish deterrence.[19]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] drew two inferences from the military's public complaints: that whatever was occurring in the Red Sea had so far failed to trigger a US military response under the framing of National Essential Functions, and that Biden either had no desire or — in the view of many in the Badlands community — no ability to intervene, a reading consistent with the [[devolution|Devolution]] thesis.[19] He said he had assigned [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] to the story ahead of the following week's Power Hour.[19]

## Intervention in the Ukraine long-range missile decision (2024)

In September 2024, Pentagon officials reportedly intervened to block the release of long-range missiles for Ukrainian strikes inside Russia, overruling a political decision by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and Secretary of State [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]].[20] Colonel [[lawrence-wilkerson|Lawrence Wilkerson]] recounted that at the September 13 meeting with British Prime Minister [[keir-starmer|Keir Starmer]], Starmer was told to keep his target list in his bag because, per the Pentagon, the missiles were not being deployed — a reversal of what [[state-department|State Department]] leaders had said publicly a day earlier.[20] [[scott-ritter|Scott Ritter]] said "phone calls were made" before officials told Biden and Starmer the deal was off; the identity of those officials was not specified, though commentators drew a parallel to [[centcom]] commander General [[michael-kurilla|Michael Kurilla]]'s intervention in the Israel theater.[20]

Badlands commentary framed the episode as US military officials placing the leaders of two of the most powerful nations on earth "into time out," and cited it as evidence supporting [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]]'s [[devolution]] thesis.[20]

## Recruitment shortfalls and eligibility (2023)

Reporting cited in the October 2023 [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] recorded that military recruitment numbers had been dismal for several years, with a Pentagon study finding that up to 77 percent of Gen Z Americans did not meet preliminary physical and mental qualifications or could not pass criminal background checks.[21] In some cases the Department of Defense had been forced to build pre-boot facilities colloquially called "fat camps" simply to get recruits physically ready enough to survive normal boot camp.[21] The shortfall framed contemporaneous speculation about a renewed [[military-draft|draft]] should the United States be drawn into a multi-front conflict in the Middle East.[21]

## September 2024 Middle East force posture

In September 2024 Pentagon press secretary Maj. Gen. Pat Ryder announced that the United States was sending a small number of additional military personnel to the Middle East in response to a sharp spike in violence between [[israel|Israel]] and [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]] forces in [[lebanon|Lebanon]], declining for operational security reasons to specify numbers or tasks and framing the move as "an abundance of caution."[22] The United States already had roughly 40,000 troops in the region.[22] On the same Monday, the aircraft carrier USS Harry S. Truman, two Navy destroyers and a cruiser sailed from Norfolk, Virginia for the Sixth Fleet area in Europe on a regularly scheduled deployment, opening the possibility of keeping both the Truman and the USS Abraham Lincoln, then in the Arabian Gulf, within reach.[22]

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that no escalation by the Pentagon had in fact occurred, calling the coverage a deceptive framing of a long-planned carrier deployment and of the routine rotation of the [[101st-airborne-division|101st Airborne Division]] from Fort Campbell, which was replacing the 10th Mountain Division's Combat Aviation Brigade.[22] He tied the point to Colonel [[lawrence-wilkerson|Lawrence Wilkerson]]'s claim, made in an interview with Judge [[andrew-napolitano|Andrew Napolitano]], that CENTCOM Combatant Commander General [[michael-kurilla|Michael Kurilla]] had usurped the authority of the [[white-house|White House]] and [[state-department|State Department]] and was effectively conducting US foreign policy, and said the routine movements did nothing to contradict that account.[22]

## Black Sea drone incident (March 2023)

Following the loss of a United States [[mq-9-reaper|MQ-9]] drone over the Black Sea on March 14, 2023, the Pentagon alleged that a Russian Su-27 aircraft had dumped fuel on the drone before it went down.[23] The [[russian-ministry-of-defence|Russian Defense Ministry]] said nothing about that allegation and blamed the drone's own sharp maneuvering, while the Kremlin accused the Pentagon of ignoring military-to-military protocols related to the war in [[ukraine|Ukraine]] that had been established to avoid inadvertent [[nato|NATO]]–Russia clashes.[23]

## Palmyra casualties (December 2025)

Following the December 2025 [[palmyra-attack-on-iowa-national-guard-members|Palmyra ambush]] in Syria, the Pentagon confirmed that two of the injured Iowa National Guard members were in stable condition and a third in good condition.[24] The U.S. Army identified the two Guard members killed as Sergeant Edgar Brian Torres-Tovar, 25, of Des Moines, and Sergeant William Nathaniel Howard, 29, of Marshalltown.[24]

## Charlie Kirk recruiting campaign (September 2025)

In September 2025, Pentagon leaders were reported to be weighing a new recruiting campaign encouraging young people to honor the legacy of assassinated conservative activist [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]] by joining the military.[25] According to two officials familiar with the planning, the campaign would be framed as a national call to service, with candidate slogans including "Charlie has awakened a generation of warriors."[25] [[anthony-tata|Anthony Tata]], undersecretary of defense for personnel and readiness, was said to be leading the effort.[25] Planners also reportedly considered using chapters of Kirk's organization, [[turning-point-usa|Turning Point USA]], at schools across the United States as military recruitment centers.[25] Badlands contributor Burning Bright treated the proposal as evidence that Kirk's death was being weaponized by actors hostile to the public interest.[25]

## Interim National Defense Strategic Guidance (2025)

A newly issued internal Pentagon document reported in March 2025 set priorities for the U.S. armed forces around a single overriding contingency: a potential conflict with [[china|China]].[26] The nine-page paper, the [[interim-national-defense-strategic-guidance|Interim National Defense Strategic Guidance]], stated that "China is the Department's sole pacing threat, and denial of a Chinese fait accompli seizure of [[taiwan|Taiwan]] — while simultaneously defending the U.S. homeland is the Department's sole pacing scenario."[26] Other potential threats such as Russia and [[iran|Iran]] were effectively left to America's allies, and Beijing was named the only potential enemy in a "major power war" requiring contingency planning.[26] Defense Secretary [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]] had previously described China as the main U.S. defense priority.[26]

## October 2023 Middle East air-defense surge

In late October 2023, as Israel again delayed its planned ground invasion of Gaza at Washington's request, the Pentagon was described as "scrambling to deploy nearly a dozen air-defense systems to the region," including protection for US troops serving in Iraq, Syria, Kuwait, Jordan, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates against missiles and rockets.[27] US officials had persuaded the Israelis to hold off until those systems could be placed, with placement expected as early as later that week.[27] Reporting framed the buildup as a sign that Washington was bracing for significant escalation, and crude prices jumped while stocks faded on the news that the Pentagon was massing further military assets in the Middle East.[27]

## Withdrawal from 9/11 plea agreements

On July 11, 2025 a panel of the [[dc-circuit-court-of-appeals|D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals]] ruled that the Pentagon could withdraw from plea agreements that would have ruled out death sentences for three Guantanamo Bay prisoners accused of plotting the September 11, 2001 attacks.[28] The agreements covering [[khalid-sheikh-mohammed|Khalid Sheikh Mohammed]], Walid bin Attash and Mustafa al-Hawsawi had been signed in July 2024 by Brigadier General [[susan-escallier|Susan Escallier]], the convening authority for the military commissions, and were voided two days later by then–Defense Secretary [[lloyd-austin|Lloyd Austin]], who reinstated death-penalty prosecutions.[28] Judges Patricia Millett and Neomi Rao wrote that "the Secretary of Defense indisputably had legal authority to withdraw from the agreements."[28]

Badlands commentary noted the significance of the Pentagon as the setting of [[donald-rumsfeld|Donald Rumsfeld]]'s September 10, 2001 remark that "according to some estimates, we cannot track $2.3 trillion in transactions," made one day before the attacks and cited as part of a broader argument about the post-9/11 expansion of federal power through the [[patriot-act|Patriot Act]] and successor institutions.[28]

## Grok contract and Aspen withdrawal (July 2025)

On July 14, 2025, the Department of Defense was reported to be set to begin using [[grok|Grok]], the chatbot developed by [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s [[xai|xAI]], following the company's announcement of "Grok for Government."[29] The move came amid scrutiny of Grok's antisemitic and pro-Hitler outputs the prior week and raised broader concerns about deploying fast-evolving commercial AI inside sensitive military contexts.[29] Badlands contributors read the timing as staged narrative work following the publicized [[trump-musk-feud-2025|Trump–Musk feud]].[29]

The same day, the Pentagon abruptly pulled senior Defense Department officials from the [[aspen-security-forum|Aspen Security Forum]], one day before the four-day Colorado summit was to begin.[29] Spokesperson [[kingsley-wilson|Kingsley Wilson]] said the event "promotes the evil of globalism, disdain for our great country, and hatred for the President of the United States," while Chief Pentagon Spokesperson [[sean-parnell|Sean Parnell]] said officials would not participate "because their values do not align with the values of the DoD."[29] Defense Secretary [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]] endorsed the reporting on X with a single word: "Correct."[29]

## August 2024 reversal of the September 11 plea deals

The Pentagon announced on Friday, August 2, 2024 that Defense Secretary [[lloyd-austin|Lloyd J. Austin III]] had revoked the pretrial agreements signed days earlier with [[khalid-sheikh-mohammed|Khalid Sheikh Mohammed]], [[walid-bin-attash|Walid bin Attash]] and [[mustafa-al-hawsawi|Mustafa al-Hawsawi]].[30] The announcement came with the release of a memorandum relieving [[susan-escallier|Susan K. Escallier]], the senior Defense Department official responsible for military commissions, of her oversight of the capital case at [[guantanamo-bay|Guantánamo Bay]].[30] The Pentagon was itself one of the September 11 attack sites, along with New York City and a field in Pennsylvania, in attacks that killed nearly 3,000 people.[30]

## Statement on Israeli operations in Lebanon (September 2024)

## Shutdown contingency guidance, September 2025

## Congressional limits on troop reductions (December 2025)

The fiscal year 2026 [[national-defense-authorization-act|National Defense Authorization Act]] moved to constrain the department's ability to reduce U.S. military forces in Europe and South Korea, requiring the maintenance of 76,000 troops in Europe and 28,500 in South Korea unless the Pentagon could assure [[congress|Congress]] that such reductions would not compromise security.[49] The legislation also required that the position of Supreme Allied Commander Europe continue to be held by an American general.[49]

Despite earlier internal discussions of troop drawdowns, U.S. officials emphasized that European allies must assume greater defense responsibilities by 2027.[49] The bill further allocated $400 million for Ukrainian security assistance along with conditions governing the reclamation of military equipment.[49] Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] disputed that Congress holds the constitutional power to impose such limits on the executive's disposition of forces.[49]

## Ukraine aid accounting error

In May 2023 the Pentagon announced that it had mistakenly assigned a higher-than-warranted value to U.S. weaponry shipped to Kyiv, because staff had used "replacement value" instead of "depreciated value" when tabulating the billions of dollars' worth of ammunition, missiles and other equipment sent to [[ukraine|Ukraine]]; the error was later revised upward to $6.2 billion.[50] Because the mistake freed accounting headroom, it worked to Kyiv's benefit, allowing more equipment to be shipped from U.S. stocks within days or weeks under [[presidential-drawdown-authority|Presidential Drawdown Authority]].[50] Two U.S. officials told Reuters that Washington would begin doling out the recovered funds, the first $200 million tranche of which was announced by the [[state-department|State Department]] on August 14, 2023.[50]

## Rare earth equity investment (July 2025)

In July 2025 the Defense Department agreed to purchase $400 million of preferred stock in [[mp-materials|MP Materials]], making the Pentagon the largest shareholder in the operator of the only working [[rare-earth-elements|rare earth]] mine in the United States, at Mountain Pass, California.[52] Proceeds were earmarked for expanding the company's rare earths processing capacity and magnet production, and the announcement sent MP Materials shares up roughly 50 percent to close at $45.23.[52]

The Pentagon described rare earth magnets as key components in military weapons systems including the F-35 warplane, drones and submarines, and the deal was structured to secure that supply: the department agreed to buy 100 percent of the magnets produced at a new facility called 10X for ten years after construction, guaranteed a floor price of $110 per kilogram over ten years for neodymium-praseodymium oxide stockpiled or sold by the company, and secured 30 percent of the upside above that floor once a second magnet plant became operational.[52] [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan]] and [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] provided $1 billion in financing for the manufacturing facility.[52] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the transaction as evidence that the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] was restructuring the national portfolio to reduce foreign dependence in critical industries, while noting the novelty of private companies having the Pentagon as majority shareholder.[52]

## October 2023 drone attack casualties and the Israel stockpile

The Pentagon confirmed in mid-October 2023 a series of drone attacks on American bases in Iraq and Syria but did not initially disclose casualties; [[us-central-command|CENTCOM]] later told NBC News that two dozen U.S. military personnel had been wounded, twenty at [[al-tanf-garrison|al-Tanf]] in Syria and four at [[ain-al-asad-airbase|al-Asad]] in Iraq.[66]

Separately, at a Pentagon press conference on Monday, October 23, 2023, a senior defense official acknowledged that the [[department-of-defense|Department of Defense]] was returning to Israel 155 mm artillery shells it had earlier withdrawn from its stockpile in Israel for U.S. forces in European Command: "We withdrew 155 [shells] from the stockpile in Israel for our forces in [European Command] and now we are sending that back to Israel for their use."[66] The Pentagon had first acknowledged in January that it dipped into the Israel stockpile to supply Ukraine, and the [[new-york-times|New York Times]] reported that "half" of the shells there were shipped to Europe the previous winter.[66]
