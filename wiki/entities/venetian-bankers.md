# Venetian Bankers

The **Venetian Bankers** are, in the account given by Erik Carlson in Badlands Media, the medieval financial group whose innovations form the origin point of the modern system of global financial control. Writing that the financial system "has controlled the world for centuries," Carlson traces that control back roughly 800 to 900 years, to a group of bankers who congregated in Venice in the 12th century.[1]

## The perpetual debt system

According to the article, the Venetian bankers created a **perpetual debt system** — a debt structure in which the debt would never go away and was deliberately designed so that it could never be paid off.[1] Carlson illustrates the concept by asking readers to imagine a mortgage or car loan that would never be retired: the car could be scrapped and sold for parts while the loan continued, and if a borrower could not pay, the debt would simply be transferred to someone else.[1] He describes this debt as behaving "like a parasite that leaves one host only to infest another," where "the hosts eventually die, but the parasite lives forever."[1]

## Migration to Holland and the City of London

Centuries after their Venetian origins, the bankers are said to have left Venice for Holland, where in 1602 they formed the [[dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]].[1] Carlson further asserts that at this time they took control of the [[house-of-orange|House of Orange]], which later became the Dutch royal family and rose to prominence through its counseling and governing under the [[habsburg-dynasty|Habsburg]] rulers of much of Europe.[1]

In 1688, by this telling, the Dutch East India Company created the [[city-of-london|City of London]], establishing the square-mile banking enclave that Carlson identifies as the seat of the [[cabal|Cabal]] for the past 337 years.[1] The Venetian bankers thus function in the narrative as the ancestral link between medieval Italian finance and the modern Anglo-Dutch financial order.

These claims are presented as the author's historical interpretation rather than as documented mainstream economic history.
