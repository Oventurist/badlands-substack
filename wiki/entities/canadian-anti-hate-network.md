# Canadian Anti-Hate Network

The Canadian Anti-Hate Network is a Canadian non-profit organization that monitors and reports on groups it identifies as hate movements, and whose researchers are frequently cited in Canadian media coverage of extremism.[1]

## Badlands criticism

Badlands Media contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] described the Canadian Anti-Hate Network as a state-funded group used, alongside state media, to portray criticism of activist teachers as dangerous, writing that it collaborates with what he called terrorist networks like [[antifa|ANTIFA]].[1] Commenting on CTV News Vancouver's coverage of the online dispute between Surrey teacher [[annie-ohana|Annie Ohana]] and former teacher [[chanel-pfahl|Chanel Pfahl]], he argued that there is no form of political or ideological opposition to such teachers that will not be reframed as "hate on the verge of violence," and mocked the "Anti-Hate rESurChEr cited by the state media."[1]

## Federal funding and the independence claim

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] wrote that the [[justin-trudeau|Trudeau]] government funds CAHN to produce "Anti-Hate kits," a relationship he said helps validate the network as an authority.[2] Although CAHN describes itself as "fully independent" on its Twitter account, Deschamps pointed to a June 2022 Government of Canada news release stating that the organization received $268,400 in federal support for developing those kits — a release that, he noted, mentioned the group's independence while announcing the money.[2] He called the arrangement doublespeak and argued that it allows Trudeau's media allies to cite CAHN as an authority, offering the headline "Far-right attacks on LGBTQ2S+ community rising in Canada: Canadian Anti-Hate Network" as an example.[2]

## Role in narrative and asymmetric warfare

In Deschamps's analysis, CAHN supplies the [[narrative-warfare|narrative warfare]] component of an [[asymmetric-warfare|asymmetric]] political operation: it targets dissidents as they emerge, damaging their reputations while their criticism becomes fuel for the radicalization of the government's base.[2] He said this allowed officials to frame parents — including Muslim communities opposing [[gender-ideology|gender ideology]] in schools — as naive victims of far-right propaganda and as pawns in the American right's attacks on LGBTQ2S+ people.[2]

## Kay defamation suit

Deschamps reported that CAHN attempted to sue [[jonathan-kay|Jonathan]] and [[barbara-kay|Barbara Kay]] for defamation after they exposed the network's support for [[antifa|ANTIFA]], and that the effort collapsed when an Ontario judge upheld the Kays' characterization.[2] He argued this disproved CAHN's self-description as "an independent, nonprofit organization made up of Canada's leading experts and researchers on hate groups and hate crimes," while cautioning that the ruling may carry little weight with audiences already captured by the operation.[2]
