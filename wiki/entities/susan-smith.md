# Susan Smith

Susan Smith is an American woman imprisoned in South Carolina for the 1994 murders of her two young sons.[1] In the crime that made her nationally notorious, Smith, then a 22-year-old mother, let her car roll into John D. Long Lake in Union County, South Carolina, with three-year-old Michael and 14-month-old Alexander still strapped into their car seats.[1]

## Parole bid

Reporting in September 2024 disclosed that Smith, by then 52, faced a parole hearing roughly six weeks out and had assembled at least four arguments in support of release, among them the claim that she had been mostly well behaved in prison.[1] A relative told the New York Post that Smith "believes she might be a free woman in less than two months" and was "manifesting her freedom."[1] Smith reportedly planned to argue that she grew up in a sexually abusive household that distorted her understanding of right and wrong, and that as a young mother she may have suffered from undiagnosed mental illness and was not in her right mind.[1] She also claimed to have had sex with prison guards while incarcerated, arguing that the asymmetrical power dynamic made genuine consent impossible.[1]

David Smith, the father of the two murdered boys, opposed the parole bid.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that Smith was spared the death penalty but should not have been, calling her a monster and recalling the killings vividly from her own adolescence.[1] Ashe characterized Smith's motive as the pursuit of a relationship with a man who did not want children, and noted that Smith initially blamed a Black man for the crime.[1] The abuse-based mitigation argument, Ashe wrote, is offensive to actual survivors of abuse, who do not conclude that killing children is acceptable and are typically more protective as a result of knowing that monsters exist.[1] Ashe further suggested that Smith's framing of her prison sexual encounters sounded like leftist criminal justice reform messaging and asked who was coaching her for the hearing, concluding that mercy sentencing for monsters in murder cases is a misguided virtue.[1]
