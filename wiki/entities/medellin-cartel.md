# Medellín Cartel

The Medellín Cartel was, in the late 1980s, the most powerful drug-trafficking organization in the world, led by [[pablo-escobar|Pablo Escobar]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

The Badlands Media account places the cartel inside the CIA's covert finance architecture: [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]], working from Caracas, is said to have established banking and money-laundering links between the [[cia|CIA]] and the Medellín and [[cali-cartel|Cali]] cartels, laying groundwork for the Reagan-Bush administration's 1980s covert war against Nicaragua and leftist guerrillas in El Salvador. Cartel figures would help finance the Nicaraguan contras in return for CIA-supplied weapons, and the CIA is said to have trained cartel accountants and private bankers to launder billions annually.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

By 1988, with violence rising in Colombia, Escobar and the Colombian government had entered indirect negotiations, during which cartel leaders proposed a deal to preserve their wealth and end the manhunt against them.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) In 1989 they dispatched an emissary, Vallejo, to Miami to approach a lawyer who had once "sorted out legal difficulties" for cartel member Jorge Ochoa and who was working with a team of corporate attorneys headed by Jeb Bush — the intention being that the lawyer would persuade the president's son to raise the proposal privately with his father.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) The August 1989 assassination of presidential candidate Luis Galán, orchestrated in part by Escobar, is described as one of the events that ended any chance of a successful negotiation.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## The cartel's laundering apparatus

A second Badlands account, by Joe Lange, focuses on the cartel's money rather than its violence. Its chief accountant, [[ramon-milian-rodriguez]], testified to a Senate Foreign Relations subcommittee in February 1988 that he laundered billions of dollars in drug profits through Panama's banking system with the help of General [[manuel-noriega]], paying the Panamanian leader an estimated $320 million to $350 million in commissions.[2] In hundreds of trips he moved roughly $11 billion in drug profits from secret safehouses around the United States, through Miami to Panama, earning $2 million to $3 million a month and, at his peak, laundering $200 million a month.[2] He also laundered through Curaçao, Switzerland, Hong Kong and the Bahamas, and dealt with off-the-books representatives of US banks including Citicorp — now [[citigroup]] — and [[bank-of-america]]; the cartel is reported to have held $11 billion in assets inside the United States in 1983.[2]

Lange argues the cartel was CIA-connected in every respect: Milian Rodriguez had been recruited into the agency in 1972 by [[manuel-artime]], commander of [[brigade-2506]], who arranged his CIA training in accounting; he made payments to the [[watergate|Watergate]] burglars; and he funneled nearly $10 million of cartel money to the Nicaraguan Contras through the CIA operative [[felix-rodriguez]] after his own 1983 arrest.[2] Noriega, for his part, supplied the cartel with the identities of American drug agents, radio frequencies and the schedules of Coast Guard and Navy surveillance vessels — information Lange says came from the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]].[2] In this telling the killing of [[pablo-escobar]], like the indictment and capture of Noriega, was a narrative operation that shielded [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]] and did nothing to slow the drug trade, as unknown successors simply rose to replace him.[2]

## Laundering through Banamex

In "The Battle for Control," Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] restates his claim that [[citibank|Citibank]] was at the center of the Medellín cartel's money laundering, conducted through currency exchange facilities at its Mexican subsidiary [[banamex|Banamex]].[3] He ties the operation directly to the [[cia|CIA]] and [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]], grouping it with the [[salinas-family|Salinas family]] drug trafficking network in Mexico and treating the pair as evidence that the bank served as the intelligence community's principal laundering tool.[3]
