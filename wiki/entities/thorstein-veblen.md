# Thorstein Veblen

Thorstein Veblen was an American economist and social theorist whom Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] characterizes as a social engineer and the intellectual source of the North American technocracy movement.[1]

## The New School and the Technical Alliance

Veblen was a founder of the [[new-school-for-social-research|New School for Social Research]] in Greenwich Village, New York, and created the [[technical-alliance|Technical Alliance]] as part of that project.[1] [[howard-scott|Howard Scott]] joined Veblen's New School study group after the First World War and, through the Alliance, secured contracts with the Industrial Workers of the World and the Railroad Brotherhood to accumulate data on America's agriculture, industry, raw materials and workforce, including behavioural and psychological profiles.[1] Ehret writes that despite having no advanced computers, Veblen's and Scott's organization became obsessed with mass data collection and surveillance of the entire economy in order to gather enough information to "scientifically" control society.[1]

## The Soviet of Engineers

Veblen created and led a body he called "The Soviet of Engineers," envisioning a new world order built on a centralized network of engineers who would permanently monopolize the levers of world power.[1] His worldview combined Darwinian survival-of-the-fittest with a radical determinism in which engineers represented the highest expression of evolution in the machine age, making their rule a scientific necessity.[1] He proposed that this collective be managed by a central directorate overseeing a global array of "sub-centers and local councils" — the model from which Scott's Technate was derived a few years later.[1]

In Veblen's scheme, the elite technicians would first carry out a controlled disintegration of society by using their command over industry and production, staging what he termed, borrowing a Marxist phrase, a "mass strike" of technicians to halt global industry.[1] After this forced reset, the technicians could "take over the economic affairs of the country…[and] take care of the material welfare of the underlying population," a passage Ehret quotes from Veblen's *The Engineers and the Price System*.[1]

## Legacy

The historian William E. Akin wrote that "Scott absorbed many of Veblen's fundamental themes," noting that Veblen's scientific positivism and technological determinism were basic to Scott's later thought and that Scott adopted Veblen's view of society as a mechanical operation.[1] Ehret places Veblen's project in the same lineage as the [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]]'s efforts in Britain and Canada, arguing that the New School served as an entry point for Oxford and London School of Economics Fabian ideas into the United States.[1]
