# Henry Clay

Henry Clay was an American senator and statesman identified in Badlands Media's "Founders & Foundations" series as one of the principal nineteenth-century inheritors and champions of the economic program first designed by [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]].[1] Writer [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] places Clay in a lineage of figures who worked to re-activate the Hamiltonian system of constitutional banking, protective tariffs, and internal improvements after Hamilton's death in 1804.[1]

## Role in the American System

Clay is named alongside [[john-quincy-adams|John Quincy Adams]], President [[william-henry-harrison|William Harrison]], [[zachary-taylor|Zachary Taylor]], [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]], Secretary of State James Blaine, President Garfield, [[william-mckinley|William McKinley]], [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]], and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] as adherents of the [[american-system-of-political-economy|American System of Political Economy]].[1] Ehret argues that the Whig defenders of that system — a group he identifies as Adams, Lincoln, and Clay — fought a "rear-guard action" during the decades after [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] destroyed the [[second-bank-of-the-united-states|Second Bank of the United States]] in 1836, waiting for a political opening to restore national banking and federally financed infrastructure.[1]

That opening appeared with the election of Whig President William Harrison in 1840, who swept to power on a mandate to "revive the national bank" and enact Clay's American System of internal improvements.[1] Harrison's death within three months — with legislation for a third national bank left unsigned on his desk — aborted the attempt, and in Ehret's account the slave power expanded enormously over the bodies of Harrison and of a second Whig president who died a decade later.[1]

## Modern invocation

The Clay–Hamilton program has been invoked in contemporary American politics. Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] encouraged the restoration of the Hamilton–Clay American System, writing that "the strength of the thread is the strength of the fabric," a principle he traced through the American System, the New Deal, and the Civil Rights Movement.[1]
