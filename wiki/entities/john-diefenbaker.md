# John Diefenbaker

**John Diefenbaker** was Prime Minister of Canada whose 1958 "Northern Vision" campaign committed the federal government to opening the Canadian Arctic as a frontier of development, including the planned domed city of Frobisher Bay in what is now Nunavut.[1] [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] treats Diefenbaker, alongside [[wac-bennett|W.A.C. Bennett]] and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]], as one of the post-war statesmen who envisioned pro-industrial growth for the North American continent.[1]

## The Northern Vision

Diefenbaker laid out his program for opening the Arctic frontier by declaring that a national development policy would "create a new sense of national purpose and national destiny," invoking "One Canada... wherein Canadians will have preserved to them the control of their own economic and political destiny."[1] Recalling that Sir John A. Macdonald had opened the West and seen Canada from east to west, Diefenbaker said he saw "a new Canada—a Canada of the North."[1] He proposed to assist the provinces, with their cooperation, in financing and constructing job-creating projects beyond provincial resources, to conserve renewable natural resources, to aid self-liquidating projects and non-self-liquidating projects that would develop national resources, to open the north through improved transportation, communication, power development and access roads, and to inventory Canada's hydroelectric potential.[1]

The [[frobisher-bay-domed-city|Frobisher Bay]] design produced by the Department of Public Works announced a domed city encircled by twelve large towers housing 4,500 workers and families, connected by an underground tunnel network, with recreation facilities, shopping centres and other amenities to ensure that the comfort of Toronto would be accessible even in the Arctic.[1]

## National credit financing

Diefenbaker's Northern Vision, which incorporated a bold "Roads to Resources" program and a frontier science program, was to be funded by a re-chartered [[bank-of-canada|Bank of Canada]].[1] Ehret writes that this financing approach took a page from [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] and [[abraham-lincoln|Lincoln]]'s Greenback system, involving public bonds issued by Canada's national bank to connect maturing Second World War Victory Bonds with new bonds directed toward Canada's development.[1]

In a 1958 radio announcement Diefenbaker described "the largest financial project in our history," offering holders of victory bonds purchased "as an act of patriotic faith during the war years" the opportunity to re-invest them "for the greater development of greater Canada," predicting "a new era of peacetime prosperity far and beyond anything we have ever known."[1]

## Sabotage

Ehret states that the national credit and Northern Vision program was sabotaged by a Rhodes Scholar coup coordinated from London, and that Diefenbaker was ousted in 1963 — an event that also hardened Bennett's resolve in British Columbia.[1] He argues that Diefenbaker's strategy remains just as applicable today as it was in 1958, and that the Frobisher Bay plan remains the paragon of northern development.[1]

## Arctic science cities and the 2025 restatement

In "Trump, Putin, Alaska and the Future of Multipolarity," [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] again presented Diefenbaker's [[frobisher-bay-domed-city|Frobisher Bay]] plan as the paragon of northern development, echoed today in Russia's nuclear-powered domed city of [[umka-arctic-city|Umka]] and in [[sergey-glazyev|Sergey Glazyev]]'s programme for five advanced Arctic cities.[2] He repeated that the Northern Vision was to be funded through a re-chartered [[bank-of-canada|Bank of Canada]] on the model of [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] and [[abraham-lincoln|Lincoln]]'s Greenback system, and that the program was sabotaged by what he calls a Rhodes Scholar coup coordinated from London.[2] Ehret grouped Diefenbaker with [[wac-bennett|W.A.C. Bennett]] and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] as statesmen whose pro-growth continental visions were abandoned after 1971, and linked the Frobisher Bay concept to the multi-trillion-dollar [[trans-eurasian-belt-development|Trans-Eurasian Belt Development]] policy described by [[vladimir-yakunin|Vladimir Yakunin]].[2]
