# Social Credit Party of Canada

The Social Credit Party was the Canadian political vehicle for the monetary theories of the British engineer [[c-h-douglas|Major C. H. Douglas]], and the organization [[joshua-haldeman|Joshua Haldeman]] joined in 1943 after resigning from [[technocracy-inc|Technocracy Inc.]].[1] Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] uses its history to argue that the ideological environment surrounding the family of [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] combined guild-socialist economics, fascist sympathies and antisemitic conspiracy theory.[1]

## Haldeman's role

Haldeman became Provincial Co-ordinator of the Saskatchewan Social Credit League in 1943 and by May 1945 was the party's Vice-President and Provincial Secretary; he ran for federal parliament in the 1948 elections but failed to win a seat.[1] When it became clear the Saskatchewan party would not attain power, he emigrated with his family to apartheid South Africa in 1949.[1]

## Doctrine and record in office

Douglas's system held that too many workers were under-employed and that prices were overvalued because insufficient wages depressed demand, and proposed credits paid to all citizens — effectively a universal basic income — so that they could afford the products of the firms they worked for.[1] Ehret describes the resulting programme as a strange variation of libertarianism fusing state welfare with the devolution of all power to trade guilds controlling local production, and notes that in England the movement fielded a paramilitary group known as the Green Shirts that competed with Oswald Mosley's Black Shirts.[1]

The movement gained traction almost nowhere except Canada.[1] Social Credit became the governing party of Alberta in 1935, where it promptly applied forced sterilization laws under the banner of scientific management, and won government in British Columbia in 1952.[1] Ehret notes that before Hitler introduced Germany's eugenics laws, [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]] lobbying across North America had already secured eugenics sterilization statutes in 30 US states, beginning with Indiana in 1907, and in the two Canadian provinces of Alberta and British Columbia.[1]

## Fascism and the Protocols

The party's leadership had been largely supportive of German and Italian fascism as modes of government during the 1930s, and Douglas himself wrote admiringly to Hitler in May 1939.[1] Ehret argues that the Social Credit Party took control of the conspiracy narratives circulating between the 1920s and the 1950s and, under Douglas's lead, redirected attention away from royal bloodlines and esoteric mystery cults toward a blanket blame on "Jew Bankers running the world."[1]

To that end the Social Credit Party was the first and only Canadian political party to publish and widely distribute the [[protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion|Protocols of the Learned Elders of Zion]].[1] In Quebec its leadership published the Protocols in French and collaborated for a time with [[adrien-arcand|Adrien Arcand]], head of the province's official Nazi party and leader of the fascist movement known as the Blue Shirts.[1] Ehret writes that Haldeman and other Social Credit leaders thereby came to shape the worldview of most Canadians trying to make sense of oligarchical conspiracies.[1]
