# Judicial Watch

Judicial Watch is the public-interest legal organization credited in the Badlands account with establishing, beyond reasonable doubt, that seats on [[ron-brown|Ron Brown]]'s [[department-of-commerce|Commerce Department]] trade missions were sold to raise funds for the [[democratic-national-committee|Democratic National Committee]] and the 1996 Clinton/Gore campaign.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1)

The findings came out of Judicial Watch's suit against the Commerce Department. At the time of his death in April 1996, Brown was under subpoena in that litigation to produce documents relating to the seat sales.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1) His business partner Nolanda Hill testified under oath that Brown had shown her a collection of such documents in an ostrich-skin portfolio; the documents were nevertheless withheld from Judicial Watch in violation of both the subpoena and a Freedom of Information Act request.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1) The article treats the group's work as the evidentiary basis for the claim that [[bill-clinton|Bill]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] knew of and approved the improper and illegal fundraising scheme.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-1)

## The 2009 Clinton email release

Judicial Watch reappears in Part 2 of the America for Sale series as the conservative research organization that released a new trove of Clinton-related emails, reported by the Chicago Tribune, revealing requests for [[state-department|State Department]] action from [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]] employees and a key donor in 2009.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) Its press release carried the headline "Bill Clinton/Doug Band Sought State Department Favors for Foundation Supporters."[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) The featured item was a July 27, 2009 email in which [[doug-band|Doug Band]] asked [[huma-abedin|Huma Abedin]] for diplomatic passports for himself and two junior Clinton aides ahead of the secret trip to [[north-korea|North Korea]]; Abedin answered, "Okay. Will figure it out."[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2) The article uses the release to argue that the Clintons provided favours to friends and supporters.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-2)

## FOIA suit for Durham records (January 2026)

On Wednesday, January 21, 2026, Judicial Watch announced it had filed a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit against the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] seeking fully unredacted records from former Special Counsel [[john-durham|John Durham]]'s probe into the origins of the [[crossfire-hurricane|Crossfire Hurricane]]/[[russiagate-hoax|Russiagate]] investigation.[3] The suit targets previously withheld or missing materials, including [[cia|CIA]] Director [[john-brennan|John Brennan]]'s 2016 White House briefing notes, the DOJ Inspector General report critiquing the [[fbi|FBI]]'s handling of Crossfire Hurricane, the 14 missing pages of the Durham Report appendix, and all unredacted transcripts or notes from Durham's interviews with key figures including [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]], Brennan, [[jake-sullivan|Jake Sullivan]] and [[john-podesta|John Podesta]].[3] The request also seeks unredacted records of Durham's referrals to other federal agencies, emails between [[fusion-gps|Fusion GPS]] and major media outlets, the Clinton campaign or the [[democratic-national-committee|DNC]], and communications involving [[british-intelligence|British Intelligence]] head Sir [[richard-dearlove|Richard Dearlove]], U.S. intelligence officials, [[stefan-halper|Stefan Halper]] and [[christopher-steele|Christopher Steele]].[3]

## Arctic Frost FOIA suit (February 2026)

In the US District Court for the District of Columbia, Judicial Watch filed a lawsuit against the [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] seeking records related to the [[fbi|FBI]]'s [[arctic-frost|Arctic Frost]] investigation, the codename for a Washington Field Office Public Corruption Unit inquiry involving President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[4] The suit followed an unanswered FOIA request submitted on October 7, 2025, and sought records on investigative approvals, techniques, and communications with Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]]'s office.[4]

## Biden–Hur audio litigation

In 2024, Judicial Watch was among the organizations pressing the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] to release audio recordings of President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s interview with special counsel [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]] regarding his handling of classified documents.[5] Group president [[tom-fitton|Tom Fitton]] called the department's refusal "yet another brazen cover-up," and Judicial Watch joined the [[heritage-foundation|Heritage Foundation]] and [[cnn|CNN]] in filing legal complaints to enforce Freedom of Information Act requests for the recordings.[5]

## FOIA suit over the Hunter Biden pardon (2025)

Judicial Watch filed a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit against the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] seeking records related to [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s only hand-signed pardon — the sweeping December 2024 order granting full clemency to his son, [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]].[6] The suit followed the Department's failure to comply with a December 2, 2024 FOIA request and demands all communications from the Offices of the Attorney General, Deputy Attorney General, and Associate Attorney General concerning the pardon, naming then–Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]], Deputy Attorney General [[lisa-monaco|Lisa Monaco]], and Associate Attorney General Benjamin Mizer.[6]

Issued on December 1, 2024, during Biden's final weeks in office, the [[hunter-biden-pardon|pardon]] shielded Hunter Biden from any federal charges "committed or taken part in" between January 1, 2014 and December 1, 2024 — a window that includes his tenure on the board of the Ukrainian energy firm [[burisma|Burisma Holdings]].[6] It was the only one among more than 1,500 pardons issued in Biden's final days signed personally by the president rather than processed by autopen.[6]

## Bost v. Illinois State Board of Elections

In June 2025 Judicial Watch announced that the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] had agreed to hear its appeal of a decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit in a case filed on behalf of Congressman [[mike-bost|Mike Bost]] and two presidential electors.[7] The suit, initially filed on May 25, 2022, challenged an Illinois law extending Election Day for fourteen days beyond the date established by federal law, which defines Election Day as the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November of every even-numbered year.[7] The lower courts had denied that Bost possessed standing to challenge the state's practice of counting ballots received after Election Day.[7]

Judicial Watch president [[tom-fitton|Tom Fitton]] called the standing denial "an injustice" and said the Court's decision to hear the case was "a critical opportunity to uphold federal law, protect voter rights, and ensure election integrity," arguing that Illinois' extension "thwarts federal law, violates the civil rights of voters, and invites fraud."[7]

Badlands commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] welcomed the development while voicing lingering frustration with the organization over its Colorado work, writing that Judicial Watch "got further than anyone else in proving that [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]]'s voter rolls are corrupted beyond reconciliation and fumbled."[7] She noted that the group entered a consent decree with Griswold, that the court declined to retain jurisdiction to enforce it, that Griswold violated it at the first deadline, and that Colorado voter rolls were probably worse than before — urging Fitton and company to revisit that relationship.[7]

## Georgia Open Records Act suit against Fani Willis

Judicial Watch brought the Georgia Open Records Act lawsuit that produced an August 2025 court order requiring Fulton County District Attorney [[fani-willis|Fani Willis]] to clarify her searches for Trump-related records.[8] The suit claimed Willis had falsely denied possessing records responsive to an earlier request for communications with Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]]'s office or the [[january-6-committee|January 6 Committee]].[8] A March 7, 2025 order required Willis to turn over 212 pages of records and to submit an affidavit explaining how they were located and why they had initially been withheld; the documents were found only after at least a fifth search of her office.[8] Judicial Watch was awarded $21,578 in attorney's fees and costs, paid ten days after the court deadline.[8] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] congratulated the organization on the victory.[8]

## Secret Service FOIA denials on the Trump assassination attempt (2024)

Judicial Watch announced that the [[united-states-secret-service|United States Secret Service]] had completely denied multiple Freedom of Information Act requests for documents about the July 13, 2024 assassination attempt on former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[11] On July 16, 2024, the group filed three comprehensive FOIA requests seeking emails, videos, and advance survey security assessments, among other records.[11] The Secret Service refused to produce a single record, citing Title 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A) on the grounds that any potentially responsive records, if they existed, were exempt because disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings, while noting that the cited exemption was not necessarily the only one available.[11]

Judicial Watch said it was pursuing the next steps in preparation for litigation, and reported having more than 25 FOIA and open-records requests pending on the Trump shooting with the Biden administration and with local and state officials and agencies in Pennsylvania.[11] President [[tom-fitton|Tom Fitton]] said, "The Biden Secret Service is in cover-up mode on its inexcusable and epic failure to protect former President Trump and other innocents," adding that promising transparency to [[congress|Congress]] while hiding every possible FOIA record from the American people was "yet another indictment of this corrupt and failing agency."[11]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] praised Judicial Watch for taking on the fight but offered a reality check: the matter was a genuinely ongoing investigation less than a month old, and although the administration appeared to be engaged in a coverup — evidenced by non-responsive testimony across more than four public hearings and a failure to comply with congressional subpoenas — it had legal cover for withholding records from a private entity.[11] She argued that answers would have to come from Congress exercising its full authority in both chambers, describing the dispute as a potential graduate-level examination of the separation of powers.[11]

## Tafari Campbell records release (2023)

## Colorado voter roll consent decree

Among the merits victories catalogued in Badlands Media's review of the [[2020-election-lawsuits|2020 election lawsuits]], Judicial Watch challenged the voter-roll hygiene maintained by Colorado Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]], and the secretary entered a consent decree resolving the case.[17] Under the settlement terms Judicial Watch could not state that Griswold had admitted fault, though the group observed it was hard to imagine another circumstance in which a secretary of state would agree to supply a conservative legal organization with annual voter-roll data updates for five years.[17]

Badlands paired the Colorado decree with comparable [[voter-roll-maintenance|voter roll]] agreements secured against Michigan Secretary of State [[jocelyn-benson|Jocelyn Benson]] and Pennsylvania Secretary [[kathy-boockvar|Kathy Boockvar]], arguing that officials could have used the litigation to demonstrate their rolls were pristine and chose not to.[17]
