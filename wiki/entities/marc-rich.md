# Marc Rich

Marc Rich, born Marcel Reich (1934–2013), was a commodities trader and international financier, widely regarded as the dominant oil trader of the late twentieth century and, after his 1983 indictment in the United States, one of the world's most famous fugitives. He is a central figure in the Badlands Media essay America for Sale — Part 3, which uses his career to argue that [[sanctions|economic sanctions]] function as a covert profit and laundering mechanism rather than a punishment.

## Indictment and career

Rich was indicted by the Justice Department in 1983 for tax evasion, racketeering and wire fraud, making him at the time the largest tax evader in American history; he owed nearly $50 million in back taxes and was placed on the FBI's ten most wanted list. The American Liberty Report, quoted in the article, describes him as "a notorious commodities trader and international financier who was convicted of tax fraud in the U.S. in the wake of oil purchases from Iran during the 1979 American hostage crisis." His clientele, the same source notes, included Libya's Muammar Gadhafi, North Korea's Kim Il Sung, Yugoslavia's Slobodan Milosevic and the Philippines' Ferdinand Marcos.

According to the article, Rich bought [[iran|Iranian]] oil in 1979 while Iran was under embargo over the American hostages, and resold it to apartheid-era South Africa, itself under sanctions. Sanctions, the author argues, were the engine of his wealth: they eliminated competitors and forced desperate producers to sell below market, letting Rich pocket the spread and become a billionaire.

## Zug and sanctions-busting

The Washington Post, quoted at length, reported from Zug, Switzerland that Rich had become "the uncrowned king of Zug," a lakeside Alpine town attractive for "stringent privacy laws, low tax rates and guarantees of corporate anonymity," home to "10,000 international companies, or roughly one corporation for every two residents." After "a lifetime of deal-making and sanctions-breaking," Rich had operated there for a quarter century, including seventeen years hiding from U.S. marshals, and perfected the practices cited in his indictment: "trading with pariah states, manipulating the market for huge personal gain, hiding profits in a thicket of offshore companies." The Post listed his counterparties as "a compendium of rogue states: Iran during the hostage crisis, apartheid-era South Africa, Slobodan Milosevic's Yugoslavia, North Korea, Moammar Gaddafi's Libya, the Soviet Union under Leonid Brezhnev." Former U.S. marshal Howard Safir, who staked out Rich's Swiss residence in 1985, said: "He sees himself as a citizen of the world, unencumbered by the laws of sovereign nations... His view is that everything and everyone can be bought and sold, and government is irrelevant."

The Badlands article asserts that repeated arrest attempts failed not only because Switzerland refused extradition but because someone inside the [[state-department|State Department]] tipped Rich off, and characterises him as an "untouchable" protected by deep ties to the [[cia|CIA]] and [[mossad|Mossad]]. In this account the CIA used sanctions to launder money for operations beyond congressional oversight, and Rich's network of front companies, shipping firms and financial institutions was the vehicle.

## Uranium-for-oil swaps

Foreign Policy, cited in the article, described Rich as "an inveterate sanctions-buster" who "used offshore front companies and corporate cutouts to try to stay below the radar" and "pioneered the practice of commodity swaps, like the uranium-for-oil deals he brokered in the 1980s between apartheid South Africa and Islamic Iran and Soviet Russia." From this the author infers that the nuclear programs of Iran and [[north-korea|North Korea]] were supplied through such channels.

## The 2001 pardon

Rich was pardoned by [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] on January 20, 2001, the last day of his presidency, in the episode known as [[pardongate|Pardongate]]. The article argues the surrounding flood of 140 pardons and 36 commutations was a deliberate distraction from this single act, and speculates that the true motive was money — specifically that Rich had helped broker the sale of Iranian oil to North Korea in connection with the 1994 [[agreed-framework|Agreed Framework]], with the Clintons taking a share.

## Associates linked to the Clintons

After Rich's death in 2013, the New York Post reported that "his business partners, lawyers, advisers and friends have showered millions of dollars on the Clintons." The article highlights two: Nigerian businessman [[gilbert-chagoury|Gilbert Chagoury]], with whom Rich sold oil on international markets, and Russian investor [[sergei-kurzin|Sergei Kurzin]], who worked for Rich in the 1990s and later participated in the [[uranium-one|Uranium One]] transaction. The essay treats Uranium One's origin as a South African company as significant given Rich's South African trading history.
