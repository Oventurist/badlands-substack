# Oliver North

**Oliver North** was the United States official whose Contra resupply network became a defining element of the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra scandal]] of the 1980s.[1]

In the account of Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], North's network provides the link between the covert arming of the [[nicaraguan-contras|Nicaraguan Contras]] and the alleged narcotics operation at [[mena-arkansas|Mena, Arkansas]].[1] The aircraft formerly flown by [[barry-seal|Barry Seal]] — the C-123 used in a [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] sting against the Sandinistas — was by October 1986 tied to North's Contra resupply effort, and its crash in Nicaragua while carrying a load of guns dragged Mena into the scandal's harsh spotlight, as reported by PBS Frontline in 1987.[1]

North's operation existed because [[congress|Congress]] had cut off official Contra funding through the [[boland-amendment|Boland Amendment]], forcing the war's backers toward off-the-books financing.[1]

## Investigation, immunity and dismissal

The [[justice-department|Justice Department]] asked Swiss authorities to assist in an investigation of both North and [[richard-secord|Richard Secord]] in connection with two Swiss bank accounts that may have been used to channel funds to the Nicaraguan contras.[2] North was fired from his [[national-security-council|National Security Council]] post in November 1986 after Attorney General Edwin Meese said he had planned and run the Iran-contra effort without President [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]]'s authorization.[2] A Reagan administration official quoted in the Chicago Tribune said North and Secord pressed contra leader Adolfo Calero to use [[southern-air-transport|Southern Air Transport]] for resupply missions, and the Los Angeles Times described Secord as North's top lieutenant in coordinating arms shipments to Iran and diverting the proceeds to the contras.[2]

North and his superior [[john-poindexter|John Poindexter]], both NSC members, were granted immunity by Congress in order to testify, over the objections of Special Counsel [[lawrence-walsh|Lawrence Walsh]].[2] Convictions won against both men were subsequently overturned on appeal because of those immunity deals, and Joe Lange notes that the two admitted to shredding hundreds of documents and laundering money through Swiss accounts yet walked away "scot-free" — in his view proof that the proceedings were a show trial staged by the [[shadow-government]].[2]

## Central American operations

In "School of Assassins and Dictators," Joe Lange describes North as the White House's covert operations pointman and a central figure in the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra scandal]] that shook the Reagan presidency.[3] [[manuel-noriega|Manuel Noriega]] later claimed it was his refusal to help North provide arms for the Contra rebels in Nicaragua that triggered the U.S. decision to drop him.[3] In Costa Rica, U.S. Ambassador [[lewis-tambs|Lewis A. Tambs]] and other embassy officials maintained close contact with the private supply network North set up from the [[national-security-council|National Security Council]], which used a secret airstrip built by the Panamanian front company [[udall-research|Udall Research]].[3]

North also figures in the El Salvador death squad episode. The Washington Post named him, then an obscure NSC aide, alongside [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]], [[george-hw-bush|Bush]], Jeane Kirkpatrick and Jesse Helms among those responsible for the policy of turning a blind eye to state-sanctioned terrorism.[3] Accompanying Bush to a December 11, 1983 meeting with Salvadoran provisional president Álvaro Magaña, North slipped Magaña a piece of paper with no letterhead or watermark, listing eight military officers and one civilian — close associates of [[roberto-daubuisson|Roberto D'Aubuisson]] — identified as death squad leaders.[3] None was ever tried, but all went into "gilded exile" or dropped out of sight and the killings fell sharply.[3]
