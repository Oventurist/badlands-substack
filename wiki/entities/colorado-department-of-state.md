# Colorado Department of State

The Colorado Department of State (CDoS) is the state agency responsible for administering elections in Colorado, headed by Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]].[1] In 2024 the department became the subject of national scrutiny after BIOS passwords for election equipment were posted publicly on its website.[1]

## The 2024 password breach

An outside investigation conducted by the Baird Quinn law firm on behalf of the Secretary of State's Office found that Griswold and her office violated state security protocols in a manner that allowed the disclosure of election equipment passwords, and concluded that the passwords were "mistakenly, unknowingly and unintentionally" posted online.[1] The BIOS passwords to election equipment in 34 counties were posted online on 21 June but were not discovered by the state until 24 October.[1] Griswold did not disclose the breach until five days later, when the Republican Party made it public.[1] The secretary maintained that the disclosure posed no immediate threat to the state's election system and that all voting equipment passwords were reset before Election Day.[1] The secretary and the office were cited for violating state information security rules, but no repercussions were expected, and the former employee who created the password spreadsheet was not admonished because she had left the office before the material was posted.[1] The breach undermined trust in the state's voting system days before the 2024 election and prompted an ongoing criminal investigation.[1]

## Criticism of the investigation

Badlands Media commentary characterized the report as an unserious work product and argued that the level of incompetence in Colorado amounted to a threat to national security.[1] Among the criticisms raised: no one involved — including the investigator who "cleared" the office — appeared to know about hidden-file capability or the "inspect workbook" function in Microsoft Excel; department staff were violating their own policies, described sardonically as gold-standard ones; and the investigator did not review or audit CDoS actions after 24 October 2024, the date on which the department learned of the breach.[1] The commentary asserted that CDoS was caught hiding a massive breach from the county clerks who had custody of the compromised equipment, told the clerks only after the story broke in the news on 29 October when the Colorado GOP publicized the scandal, and then engaged a hand-picked lawyer-investigator whose scope was limited or possibly directed by the department itself.[1]

The critique further noted that the investigation excluded the cover-up itself — the refusal to communicate with clerks until the story broke, the recorded statement by Deputy Secretary Beall to the Adams County Clerk that clerks were not told in order to avoid a media frenzy, and the question of who made decisions during the cover-up or held the crisis-communications contract.[1] Because CDoS holds legal privilege with the law firm investigating its own office, the commentary questioned whether Beall or the department defined the investigator's scope, whether such direction would be enough to pierce privilege, and whether the adage that the cover-up is worse than the crime is both true and actionable in this case.[1] The Badlands summary concluded that an ongoing cover-up exists in the Colorado state department and that new co-conspirators were being drawn into it.[1]
