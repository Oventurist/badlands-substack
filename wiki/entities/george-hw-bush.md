# George H.W. Bush

George Herbert Walker Bush was a Texas oilman, Director of Central Intelligence, Vice President and President of the United States. In the Badlands Media account, he is the central figure of a multi-generational family enterprise that the author describes as far more corrupt than the Biden family whose dealings the mainstream media was, at the time of writing, finally being forced to acknowledge — a corruption said to involve far more money and a complete disregard for American lives.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Houston and Zapata

Bush made Houston, Texas his home, and the city served as his base of operations going back to the 1950s for what the article characterizes as his CIA front company, [[zapata-offshore|Zapata Offshore]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) In 1953 Bush and J. Hugh Liedtke — later head of Pennzoil — formed Zapata Petroleum Corporation with headquarters in Houston, the same year that [[texas-national-bank|Texas National Bank]] was created through the merger of South Texas National and Union National Bank.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) The article treats the simultaneity as deliberate rather than coincidental: a bank established to service a CIA front company.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Bay of Pigs

Three ships owned by Zapata and financed by Texas National Bank — the *Houston*, the *Barbara J* and the *Zapata* — were loaned by Bush's firm to the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]] for the 1961 invasion of Cuba by exiled Cuban mercenaries, an operation the agency code-named OPERATION ZAPATA.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) Bush always denied being a member of the CIA before becoming its director in 1976, a posture the author summarizes as the family motto: "Admit nothing and deny everything."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) The article argues that the mainstream media has never reported the [[bay-of-pigs|Bay of Pigs]] connection because Bush was "an untouchable of the highest order."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Cuban exile networks

The article holds that Bush had been a leader of clandestine operations since the early 1950s and maintained deep ties to the Cuban exile population recruited by the CIA for the Bay of Pigs and later covert work — a force said to be trained in paramilitary operations, terrorism, assassination and money laundering. It notes that the CIA named its new headquarters after him.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) Several Cuban-American figures in the narrative, including [[miguel-recarey|Miguel Recarey]] and [[armando-codina|Armando Codina]], are presented as products of that network.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Placing Jeb

As one of his last acts as Director of Central Intelligence, Bush is said to have arranged for his son [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]] to be hired by the international division of [[texas-commerce-bank|Texas Commerce Bank]] as a CIA "non-official cover" officer, simply by calling his close friend [[james-baker|James Baker]], whose family founded the bank.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Latin American connections

Bush's circle is linked through the article to the [[salinas-family|Salinas family]] of Mexico — "Intimo Amigos" — and, via [[nafta|NAFTA]], to a claimed expansion of drug trafficking across the southern border intended to increase CIA profits.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) Drug money from the [[gulf-cartel|Gulf Cartel]] ultimately flowed into Texas Commerce Bank in Houston, the city Bush called home.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Director of Central Intelligence (1976)

In 1976 President [[gerald-ford|Gerald Ford]] nominated Bush to be Director of the [[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]] in an attempt to save an agency at the lowest point in its history, under Senate investigation with its "dirty laundry" exposed and its very existence in question.[2] Badlands writer [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] described the appointment as a turning point that would shape the future of the CIA and of the world, and not for the better.[2]

Quoting the Miami Herald, Badlands noted that Bush "loved the CIA" and called it "part of my heartbeat"; he was the only president who ever ran the agency and the last who truly believed in its Cold War code, "Admit nothing, deny everything."[2] During his eleven months as director he spent much of his energy battling senators concluding their year-long investigation, which uncovered assassination plots, secret wars, antidemocratic coups and mind-control experiments using LSD on unwitting subjects.[2] Bush decried the revelations rather than the facts revealed, saying they "devastated the morale of perhaps the finest group of public servants this country has."[2] Lange argued Bush cared more about morale than about the agency's lawbreaking, and that he was installed to make the investigations go away — steering the [[church-committee|Church Committee]] era toward reforms rather than criminal charges.[2]

## Capture of the President's Daily Brief

A CIA.gov biography quoted by Badlands records that on becoming DCI, Bush insisted upon direct access to President Ford, usually editing the [[presidents-daily-brief|President's Daily Brief]] or briefing at National Security Council meetings in order to convey the agency's analysis directly to the president.[2] Ford frequently included Bush in high-level meetings, and Bush brought Agency officers along to brief the president, a tactic that impressed Ford and boosted morale at Langley.[2] As [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]]'s vice president from 1981 he read the PDB avidly, and as the 41st president from 1989 he made a CIA briefing his first order of daily business.[2] Lange treats this institutional capture as Bush's most consequential achievement: it elevated the CIA within the executive branch, gave it influence over every administration since, and accelerated the formation of a [[shadow-government|shadow government]].[2]

## Denials of earlier CIA service

Badlands questioned why the agency named its headquarters after a man who served as director for only eleven months and who consistently denied any CIA connection before 1975.[2] A New York Times report cited by the article described a memo of November 29, 1963 from FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover to the State Department, which stated that the bureau had briefed "Mr. George Bush of the Central Intelligence Agency" on the reaction of Cuban exiles in Miami to the assassination of President John F. Kennedy.[2] The same Nation article, by Joseph McBride, quoted an unidentified source with close intelligence connections saying Bush "started working for the agency in 1960 or 1961, using his oil business as a cover for clandestine activities."[2] A spokesman for the then-vice president, Stephen Hart, said Bush denied any involvement with the agency before Ford named him director, insisting he was an oilman in the early 1960s who ran for the Senate in 1963.[2] Lange concluded that Bush had been a CIA member since at least the early 1950s and was most likely recruited out of Yale.[2]

## Zapata as global drug infrastructure

Citing Wikipedia's HRG Group entry, Badlands recorded that Zapata Petroleum was founded in 1953 by Bush with John Overbey, Hugh Liedtke, Bill Liedtke and [[thomas-j-devine|Thomas J. Devine]], a CIA staffer who resigned that year to work under commercial cover; Devine later accompanied Bush to Vietnam in late 1967 as a "cleared and witting commercial asset."[2] [[zapata-offshore|Zapata Off-Shore]] was formed in 1954 with Bush as president, and in 1959 Bush bought control of it for $800,000.[2] Although it had only a few drilling rigs, the company set up operations in the Gulf of Mexico, the Persian Gulf, Trinidad, Borneo and Medellín, Colombia — locations Lange argued would each become a centre for global drug trafficking, tied respectively to the Mexican cartels, the Mujahideen and [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]], Venezuela, Indochina, and the [[medellin-cartel|Medellín cartel]] of [[pablo-escobar|Pablo Escobar]].[2] The 1967 Vietnam trip is placed alongside the [[secret-war-in-laos|secret CIA war in Laos]] over the opium trade and the [[phoenix-program|Phoenix Program]].[2] In this account Bush is responsible for the CIA's global drug trafficking expansion, whose proceeds funded terrorist groups and proxy wars from the [[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]] onward.[2]

## Alleged role in Watergate and the drug trade

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argues that Bush played a vital role in forcing [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]] out of office through the intentionally botched [[watergate|Watergate]] break-in — highly trained covert [[cia|CIA]] operatives who suddenly became bumbling fools and were easily caught because they were supposed to be caught, so the operation could be pinned on Nixon, who had declared a [[war-on-drugs|war on drugs]].[3] Before becoming Director of the CIA in 1976, Bush was Chief of the US Liaison Office to the People's Republic of China.[3]

Bush was Vice President in 1986 when the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra Affair]] was exposed, an operation funded in part by CIA trafficking of cocaine into Los Angeles.[3] He was also Vice President at the time of the assassination attempt on [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]] — another president who had declared a war on drugs — by a shooter Carlson describes as having close ties to the Bush family.[3] [[lyndon-larouche|Lyndon LaRouche]] was among Bush's persistent critics.[3]

In "Follow The Money," Joe Lange continued his argument that Bush was the architect of the modern [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and [[shadow-government]], adding a financial dimension: the enterprise's dependence on [[money-laundering]] through complicit banks.[5] Lange contends that when congressional testimony by [[ramon-milian-rodriguez]] made [[manuel-noriega]] a liability, the Bush administration responded with a narrative reversal — indicting Noriega, invading Panama to arrest him, and casting Bush as the presidential hero fighting drug traffickers, thereby shielding both Bush and the CIA from trafficking accusations.[5] The hunting and killing of [[pablo-escobar]] under Bush's presidency served the same purpose, while doing nothing to slow the flow of drugs into the United States.[5]

Lange also notes the timing coincidence that [[michael-jon-hand]] and [[bernie-houghton]], the American intelligence figures behind the [[nugan-hand-bank]], arrived in Sydney in 1967 — the same year Bush and his CIA partner at Zapata Offshore traveled to Vietnam.[5] He closes by flagging the Bush family's direct ties to Citicorp, now [[citigroup]], one of the two US banks named by Milian Rodriguez in his congressional testimony.[5]

## Deregulation and the savings and loan crisis

In "Never Let a Crisis Go to Waste," Joe Lange casts Bush as the author of the [[savings-and-loan-crisis|savings and loan crisis]].[7] Reducing regulatory burdens was one of four "pillars" of [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]]'s 1981 economic recovery agenda, and Reagan established the [[task-force-on-regulatory-relief|Presidential Task Force on Regulatory Relief]] with Bush as chairman, calling him a "vigorous leader, talented administrator, and absolutely, no doubt, a superb diplomat" to coordinate the interagency effort to end excessive regulation.[7] Lange asks whether Bush was merely carrying out Reagan's wishes as a loyal vice president or pursuing something much bigger, and answers that putting Bush in charge of revising past regulations opened the door to one of the biggest scandals in United States history.[7]

The central act was the [[deregulation|deregulation]] of banking, which allowed savings and loans to make commercial and unsecured business loans and to invest heavily in commercial real estate with 100 percent financing, under far more lenient standards; combined with the 1980 increase in [[deposit-insurance|deposit insurance]] this removed all risk from thrift operators.[7] Lange argues the real purpose was to create a nationwide [[real-estate-money-laundering|real estate money laundering]] system to fund the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] outside congressional and presidential oversight, and that many writers allege the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] used the thrift deregulation process to pay for covert operations Congress would not support.[7]

Bush's family and associates profited directly. His son [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]] defaulted on a [[broward-federal-savings-and-loan|Broward Federal]] loan and settled for $505,000 while keeping a Miami office building, and his son [[neil-bush|Neil Bush]] presided over [[silverado-savings-and-loan-association|Silverado]]'s $1 billion collapse, after which regulators delayed announcing their takeover until Bush had been elected president in 1988 and the [[white-house|White House]] instructed the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]], the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury]] and banking investigators to lay off.[7] In 1984 Bush personally summoned a thrift regulator to his office and, in front of [[sunrise-savings-and-loan|Sunrise Savings]] chief executive Robert Jacoby, reprimanded her and told her to back off her investigation; Sunrise failed a year later at a cost of $680 million.[7] Lange speculates the intervention protected a CIA operation under a "national security" pretext.[7]

Bush's circle extended across the scandal: [[stefan-halper|Stefan Halper]] and [[harvey-mclean-jr|Harvey McLean Jr.]], both veterans of his 1980 presidential campaign, founded [[palmer-national-bank|Palmer National Bank]] with $2.8 million in seed money from mafia-linked [[herman-k-beebe|Herman K. Beebe]], and the bank funnelled money to [[oliver-north|Oliver North]]'s [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] operation; his fundraiser [[walter-mischer|Walter Mischer]] was a friend of major thrift defaulter [[john-riddle|John Riddle]]; [[robert-corson|Robert Corson]] obtained [[vision-banc-savings|Vision Banc Savings]] with a recommendation from the judge who would head Bush's 1988 Harris County campaign; and Reagan's Comptroller of the Currency [[robert-l-clarke|Robert L. Clarke]] was a Bush colleague.[7] Lange concludes the entire episode was a planned crisis set up and perpetuated by Bush and his shadow government — the biggest bank theft in American history at the time.[7]

## The Gulf War coalition

In "Nothing Is as It Appears," [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues that while Bush was creating the wars of the Middle East he simultaneously presented himself as the region's savior by "coercing" a coalition of 42 countries into removing Iraq's military from Kuwait.[8] US troops made up 73% of the coalition's 956,600 personnel in Iraq, and many coalition governments were reluctant to commit forces, viewing the conflict as an internal Arab affair or resisting an expansion of US influence; they were ultimately persuaded by Iraqi belligerence, offers of economic aid or debt forgiveness, and threats to withhold aid.[8] Lange characterizes those inducements as bribes funded by taxpayers and concludes that Bush's reputation as a statesman rallying the world against the next "Hitler" rested on bribery and coercion.[8]

He finds the debt-forgiveness lever especially revealing, since one of [[saddam-hussein|Saddam Hussein]]'s reasons for invading Kuwait was Kuwait's refusal to forgive Iraqi war debt incurred fighting the [[iran-iraq-war|war against Iran]], a debt owed largely to Kuwait and Saudi Arabia that was crushing the Iraqi economy.[8] Lange asserts that Bush's father helped install Saddam as dictator, backed him against Iran, helped build up his military, and then declined to deter the invasion of Kuwait, concluding that "Bush wanted a war with Iraq" — and that Bush and the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] funded both sides of the Iran–Iraq war.[8] The [[gulf-war|Gulf War]] is thus placed within the broader claim that the Middle East was purposely turned into a war zone for money laundering.[8]

## Control of presidential records

The article devotes extended attention to Bush's handling of his own records. At 11:30 P.M. on January 19, his last full day in office, [[archivist-of-the-united-states|Archivist of the United States]] [[don-w-wilson|Don W. Wilson]] signed an unusual agreement granting Bush "exclusive legal control of all Presidential information," including more than 4,000 computer tapes from the [[national-security-council|National Security Council]] and other White House offices.[8] Three weeks later Wilson resigned to become executive director of the George Bush Center at Texas A&M University, prompting three Democratic senators to seek an ethics investigation.[8] Judge Charles R. Richey found Wilson had violated federal record-keeping law and twice ordered the tapes preserved out of fear that Bush officials would destroy them; on January 20, Bush and archives officials removed 4,000 to 5,000 tapes and the hard drives from 147 NSC computers and about 300 other White House machines.[8] Evidence of [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] crimes, Lange writes, was the least of what Bush had to hide.[8]

[[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] later publicized the improvised storage of Bush's materials in a former bowling alley and adjacent Chinese restaurant kitchen, drawing a defense of his late father from [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]]; Los Angeles Times reporting confirmed that 36 million pages of documents, a million photos and 40,000 objects were being sorted there, including material from Bush's time as vice president, as a Texas congressman and as CIA director, some of it classified and open only to holders of top-secret clearances.[8] Lange links the congressional-era records to Bush's travel to Vietnam with a CIA frontman at the time the [[phoenix-program|Phoenix Program]] was established and the CIA was waging a [[secret-war-in-laos|secret war in Laos]] to protect heroin trafficking, and treats the [[george-bush-presidential-library|Bush presidential library]] as a mechanism for retaining control over self-incriminating releases.[8]

## The 1991 Iran payment precedent

"Panic in DC" treats Bush's presidency as the template for the [[barack-obama|Obama]] administration's later cash transfers to [[iran|Iran]].[9] CBS News reporting cited there notes that the United States paid out $278 million in Iran-related claims from the [[judgment-fund|Judgment Fund]] in 1991, when Bush — a former member and director of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] before serving as Ronald Reagan's vice president — was president.[9] [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] calls this "a key data point connecting a lot of things" and argues the two episodes share the same elements: cash to Iran, drawn from the Judgment Fund, justified by a supposed looming loss at the [[international-court-of-justice|International Court of Justice]], with American hostages in the background — "the same CIA playbook."[9]

New York Times reporting from November 1989 quoted in the article describes the Bush administration as eager to resolve financial disputes with Iran to improve relations in the post-Khomeini era and possibly pave the way for the release of American hostages in Lebanon, with Bush saying at a news conference, "I'd like to get this underbrush cleaned out now."[9] Under the arrangement disclosed at the time, Iran would receive $567 million from a frozen account while the United States retained the $243 million balance.[9] The decision followed unannounced talks with Iranian officials by State Department legal adviser Abraham D. Sofaer at the American-Iranian claims tribunal in The Hague, and the administration notably did not wait for the tribunal to rule against the United States but dispatched Sofaer to accelerate matters.[9] Iran had filed a $12 billion claim over undelivered military equipment.[9]

The article separately alleges that Hezbollah's Latin American drug networks, uncovered decades later by [[project-cassandra|Project Cassandra]], traced back to the 1980s when Bush allegedly helped establish them in the region.[9]

## The Hinckley connection and Watergate

Kennedy]].[10] What the author finds hard to understand is how Bush was never accused of involvement in the Reagan attempt, concluding that the CIA "really is that powerful."[10]

The article also states that [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]] was taken out by the CIA — not by a bullet but by a plot — with the [[watergate|Watergate]] break-ins performed to remove him from office because the agency viewed him as a threat to its power, and adds that Bush Sr. may have had his hands all over the Watergate set-up, from behind the scenes as in the JFK case.[10]

## BCCI, Kuwait and the Gulf War as distraction

"Profiting From War" places Bush at the centre of the [[bank-of-credit-and-commerce-international|BCCI]] scandal, describing the Abu Dhabi-based institution — nicknamed the "Bank of Crooks and Criminals International" — as a CIA bank that became a $20 billion fraud funding both agency operations and individuals.[11] Quoting a 2004 [[los-angeles-times|Los Angeles Times]] opinion piece, the article notes that Bush was the first CIA director to come from the oil industry and the first vice president and president to have either an oil or CIA background, which it says helps explain his persistent bent toward the Middle East, covert operations and rogue banks.[11] In each office he held he encouraged CIA involvement in Iran, Pakistan, Afghanistan and other Middle Eastern countries, and pursued policies that helped make the region the world's primary destination for arms shipments.[11]

As early as 1964, running for the U.S. Senate from Texas, Bush was labeled by incumbent Democrat Ralph Yarborough a hireling of the sheik of Kuwait, for whom his company drilled offshore oil wells.[11] After taking the CIA helm in January 1976 he cemented relations with the intelligence services of both Saudi Arabia and the shah of Iran, working closely with [[kamal-adham|Kamal Adham]], head of Saudi intelligence, brother-in-law of King Faisal and an early BCCI insider.[11] [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues that Bush deliberately started the [[gulf-war|Gulf War]] as a distraction once congressional and criminal investigations into BCCI had begun and the family had become vulnerable to exposure, and that the destruction of [[kuwait|Kuwait]] conveniently destroyed evidence of the family's dealings there.[11]

The article recounts that in 1992 special prosecutor Lawrence E. Walsh asserted Bush had been "in the loop" on multiple illegal acts in the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra affair]], some of whose clandestine arms shipments to Iran were BCCI-financed, and describes his role in [[iraqgate]] as much clearer still.[11] Lange contends Bush was shielded by underlings as an "untouchable" while using BCCI to fund CIA operations across both scandals.[11]

## The family money spigot

The same article describes Bush's four sons — [[george-w-bush|George W.]], [[jeb-bush|Jeb]], [[neil-bush|Neil]] and [[marvin-bush|Marvin]] — as following in the family footsteps, lining up deals with Saudi, Kuwaiti and Bahraini moneymen and cozying up to BCCI, making the Middle East a "family money spigot."[11] Lange casts Bush as "the original Big Guy" taking ten percent or more through his sons' vehicles, with [[harken-energy|Harken Energy]] serving as the family's equivalent of Burisma.[11]

After losing his bid for a second term, Bush joined the Washington-based [[carlyle-group|Carlyle Group]] in 1993, becoming an investor, a member of its Asia Advisory Board and a rainmaker who drummed up investors — among them twelve rich Saudi families including the Bin Ladens.[11] The Washington Post reported in 2002 that Saudis close to defense minister Prince Sultan were encouraged to put money into Carlyle as a favor to the elder Bush; he retired from the firm in October 2003.[11] Also in 1993, Bush travelled to Kuwait with his wife, his sons Neil and Marvin, and former Secretary of State [[james-baker|James A.
