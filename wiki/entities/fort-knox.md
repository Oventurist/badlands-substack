# Fort Knox

Fort Knox is a U.S. Army installation in Kentucky that houses the United States Bullion Depository, a massive and heavily secured national gold reserve.[1]

## 2025 DOGE audit push

On Monday, February 17, 2025, [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] signaled that the [[doge|Department of Government Efficiency]] would look into Fort Knox, writing on X, "Looking for the gold at Fort Knox …"[1] He amplified a post by Senator [[mike-lee|Mike Lee]] of Utah, who said he had been denied access to the installation, and added: "Who is confirming that gold wasn't stolen from Fort Knox? Maybe it's there, maybe it's not. That gold is owned by the American public! We want to know if it's still there."[1]

Senator [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] of Kentucky encouraged the effort, replying "nope. Let's do it," to Musk's question about whether the reserve is reviewed annually.[1] Paul told Fox News the same day that more transparency about Fort Knox was warranted: "I think some of them may not think it needs to be audited all the time, but I think the more sunlight, the better, more transparency, the better. And also, it brings attention to the fact that gold still has value and implicitly, not explicitly, but implicitly, gold still gives value to the dollar."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that an audit of Fort Knox is not bullish for gold over the long term but quite the opposite, because it would demonstrate that without a distributed and verifiable monetary ledger there is no such thing as financial transparency — a pointed nod toward [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]].[1]

## Audit history and the ZeroHedge campaign

Whether the gold at Fort Knox is really there, or has been plundered, has been described as one of the biggest open questions of the past fifty years.[2] The last audit of America's gold stash was conducted on September 23, 1974, when the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|US Treasury]] opened just one of its fifteen vaults at Fort Knox so that politicians and reporters could swarm the site for a two-hour photo opportunity with roughly 6% of the alleged amount held.[2] None of the bars passed around for the cameras were matched to a serial number, assayed or tested for purity, or even verified as US holdings — foreign countries have previously stored gold at Fort Knox as well.[2] Since then there has been no independent verification of the roughly 4,580 metric tons supposedly held by the Treasury, outside of annual "vault seal checks" that do not analyze the gold, seven of which have reportedly been lost.[2]

In February 2025, [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] suggested that [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s [[doge|DOGE]] team — including a staffer known as "Big Balls" — investigating government-wide waste, fraud and abuse might usefully be pointed toward Fort Knox.[2] The suggestion immediately went viral on X, with Senator [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] (R-KY) replying to Musk with "Let's do it."[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that gold was inching toward $3,000 an ounce as she wrote, that stories of gold being repatriated to the United States were appearing in mainstream outlets, and that calls to audit Fort Knox were rising, concluding: "Our national family is as sick as its secrets. Time to come clean."[2]

## Mnuchin's 2017 visit

In August 2017, during [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s first year in office, Treasury Secretary [[steven-mnuchin|Steven Mnuchin]] became only the third treasury secretary to visit the United States Bullion Depository at Fort Knox, Kentucky — an event that caught many observers by surprise because nothing comparable had happened since 1948.[3] Visits to the classified facility, built in 1936, are extremely rare for security reasons; in 1974 the Mint gave a tour to select members of Congress to quell rumors that there was no gold in the facility.[3] The total value of the depository's contents was put at approximately $200 billion, though Mnuchin noted the last count was in 1953.[3]

Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] treats the visit as a deliberate signal, pairing it with a Q post in which the question "Do we have the gold?" was answered "Yes," and arguing that any return to a [[gold-standard|gold standard]] must begin with confirming the gold is there.[3]
