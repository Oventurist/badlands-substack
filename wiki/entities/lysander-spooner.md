# Lysander Spooner

**Lysander Spooner** was a nineteenth-century American legal theorist and essayist whose prolific writings on [[natural-law|Natural Law]] form the subject matter of the Badlands Media programme [[no-treason-podcast|The No Treason Podcast]].[1] A Badlands Media essay published 5 October 2025, "On Nations and Natural Law," presents Spooner as the central authority for recovering a philosophical grounding for American liberty that the author regards as largely absent from modern political discourse.[1]

## Conception of Natural Law

Spooner argued that Natural Law is universal — valid for all time and in all places — because it is woven into the fabric of Creation in the same manner as the phenomena of light or magnetism, and that it can therefore be studied, learned and discerned much as one scientifically contemplates gravity.[1] For this reason he called it the "science of justice."[1] The Badlands essay describes this as a "second tier" of [[first-principles|First Principles]], a logical extension of the ultimate First Principle of God as Creator, since a law of this kind necessarily implies a Law Giver.[1]

In his essay [[natural-law-the-science-of-justice|Natural Law – The Science of Justice]], Spooner defined the subject as "the science of mine and thine — the science of justice," encompassing all of a man's rights of person and property and his rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness.[1] He characterised it further as "the science of peace," the only science capable of stating the conditions under which mankind can live at peace with one another.[1]

## Conditions of peace and the reciprocity of justice

Spooner reduced those conditions to two: first, that each man do toward every other all that justice requires — paying his debts, returning borrowed or stolen property, and making reparation for injuries done to another's person or property; and second, that each man abstain from what justice forbids, such as theft, robbery, arson and murder.[1] From these he derived three fundamental legal rights of a man: to be dealt with honestly, not to be intentionally and undeservingly hurt, and to be given what he is due.[1]

Because justice is reciprocal, Spooner expressed these rights simultaneously as duties, invoking the ancient maxim that the sum of a man's legal duty to his fellow men is "to live honestly, to hurt no one, and to give everyone his due."[1]

## Legal versus moral

Spooner drew a sharp distinction between the legal and the moral: the legal pertains to property rights as informed by Natural Law, while the moral concerns the ethical dimension of social relationships.[1] He did not comment on the source of moral responsibilities, which the Badlands author attributes to his Deistic background — his father was reportedly a Deist — and to a corresponding unwillingness to accept that God the Creator intervened in His creation to bequeath a revealed moral law.[1] The author nonetheless regards the distinction as valid and useful for delimiting the prerogatives of human government, arguing that much of the legal confusion of the present day arises from government straying into the moral sphere when it should be confined to the legal.[1]

## Arguments for the existence of justice

Spooner argued retroductively — reasoning backwards from an observed phenomenon to its simplest explanation — a method the essay compares to CS Lewis's later argument in *Mere Christianity* that mankind's universal impulse to worship is evidence for God, employed some sixty-five years after Spooner.[1] He observed that children grasp the principles of justice long before they can articulate them and independently of external instruction: that one child must not strike or hurt another without just cause, must not assume arbitrary domination over another, and must not by force, deceit or stealth take what belongs to another, and that other children have both the right and the moral duty to assist the injured party.[1] Children learn these things, he wrote, earlier than they learn that three and three are six.[1]

Spooner further argued that men must know sentiments and ideas, no less than material things, before they can know the meanings of the words describing them — one cannot teach the meaning of justice and injustice before the nature of the things themselves is understood, any more than heat and cold or light and darkness.[1] The Badlands author connects this observation to the Apostle Paul's argument in Romans 2:14–16 that Gentiles who do not have the law nevertheless show the work of the law written on their hearts.[1]

Spooner reinforced the case with a series of fifteen "If/Then" propositions, the first two of which hold that if justice is not a natural principle then every appeal and struggle for justice in history has been a struggle for a mere fantasy, and that there could then be no such thing as injustice — all the crimes in the world becoming mere events, like the falling of rain or the setting of the sun.[1] Being part of nature, justice is immutable and can no more be changed by any inferior power than can the law of gravitation or the principles of mathematics; attempts by any man or body of men, "whether calling themselves governments, or by any other name," to substitute their own commands or discretion for justice are an absurdity, a usurpation and a tyranny.[1] Absent such a principle, Spooner concluded, the world would be an "abyss of moral darkness."[1]

## Reception in Badlands Media commentary

The essay treats Spooner as offering both a diagnosis and a remedy: the slow degradation of liberty in the United States is attributed in part to a lost understanding of Natural Law, and its recovery is presented as the means by which "freedom-loving patriots" might swing the pendulum of social change back.[1] Spooner is noted as having had "choice words" for the outcome of the American Revolution, even as the [[founding-fathers|Founding Fathers]] are credited with demonstrating what is possible when enough people approach the creation of a government with the right mindset.[1] The essay also commends the wider libertarian literature, singling out [[murray-rothbard|Murray Rothbard]] as worthwhile reading.[1]
