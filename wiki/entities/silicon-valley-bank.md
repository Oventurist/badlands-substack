# Silicon Valley Bank

Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) was a United States commercial bank whose collapse in March 2023 became the reference point for fears of a broader contagion running through the global banking industry.[1]

## Role in the March 2023 banking stress

In coverage compiled by the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] for Tuesday, March 14, 2023, the failure of Silicon Valley Bank was identified as the trigger event that sent the cost of insuring the bonds of [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse Group AG]] against default to the highest level on record.[1] Reporting cited by the brief, attributed to [[bloomberg|Bloomberg]], framed the SVB collapse as having "sparked concern about broader contagion in the banking industry," linking a domestic American bank failure directly to the deteriorating position of a systemically important Swiss institution.[1]

## Significance

The juxtaposition of the SVB failure with Credit Suisse's simultaneous disclosure of "material weaknesses" in its financial reporting formed the core of the brief's financial segment, in which Badlands contributor [[just-human|Just Human]] warned that a Credit Suisse failure "will be catastrophic to [their] systems."[1] In that framing, SVB functioned less as an isolated institutional failure than as the first visible crack in a wider structure of leverage and counterparty exposure.[1]

## Collapse and insolvency

Silicon Valley Bank was the sixteenth-largest bank in the United States until it abruptly failed and entered insolvency on March 10, 2023.[2] The immediate impetus for the collapse was a $2 billion liquidity loss on bond sales, which caused the institution's stock value to fall more than 60% and triggered a bank run by depositors fearful of losing some or most of their money.[2] Writing at Alt-Market, [[brandon-smith|Brandon Smith]] argued that the bank's shortfalls were not the cause of the crisis but a symptom of a wider liquidity drought he had forecast months earlier, including the approximate timing of the event.[2]

In the days following the failure, the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], the Treasury and the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] intervened to backstop depositors, but regional bank shares nonetheless fell sharply and credit-default-swap spreads widened across global majors.[2] Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] wrote that the fallout from the SVB and [[signature-bank|Signature Bank]] failures was "just beginning," and that despite the Fed's effective bailout the markets continued to tumble because banking is fundamentally "a confidence game."[2] Contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode as evidence that the Fed was caught in a pincer: raising rates would destroy more insolvent institutions, while cutting them would reignite inflation.[2]

## Successor ventures

The collapse of Silicon Valley Bank left a gap in banking services for technology start-ups that later ventures sought to fill.[3] In July 2025 a group of technology billionaires led by [[palmer-luckey|Palmer Luckey]], co-founder of the military contractor [[anduril|Anduril]], was reported to be preparing a United States bank named [[erebor|Erebor]] intended to serve start-ups — including cryptocurrency businesses — in the space SVB had occupied.[3] Reported backers included [[joe-lonsdale|Joe Lonsdale]] of [[8vc|8VC]] and [[peter-thiel|Peter Thiel]]'s [[founders-fund|Founders Fund]].[3]

## March 9, 2023 deposit flight

On March 9, 2023, venture capital firms — including some of the biggest names in the industry — began advising portfolio companies to pull money out of Silicon Valley Bank after the bank announced that morning that it would sell shares to raise capital.[4] Investors feared that enough startups withdrawing at once would leave the institution upside-down on deposits versus demand, a dynamic that can become a self-fulfilling prophecy.[4] Because venture-backed founders routinely park raised capital in SVB accounts, even perceived risk threatened a domino effect.[4] Venture investor Mark Suster publicly defended the bank, saying he believed its CEO's claim that SVB was solvent and not in violation of any banking ratios, and Rob Go of NextView and Bryce Roberts also voiced support.[4] The episode is covered in detail at [[2023-silicon-valley-bank-run|the 2023 Silicon Valley Bank run]].[4]

## Seizure, deposit flight and the bailout question

The California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation reported that, despite the bank being in sound financial condition prior to March 9, 2023, investors and depositors reacted to the announcement of an approximately $1.8 billion loss on a sale of investments and a simultaneous capital raise by initiating withdrawals of $42 billion in deposits on that single day, causing a run on the bank.[5] As of the close of business on Thursday, March 9, the bank held a negative cash balance of approximately $958 million.[5] Despite attempts by the bank and regulators to transfer collateral from various sources, it failed to meet its cash letter with the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], leaving it incapable of paying its obligations as they came due and therefore insolvent.[5] The withdrawal represented roughly a quarter of SVB's deposit base drained in a matter of hours.[5] The bank was seized by federal regulators and taken over by the State of California on Friday, March 10, 2023, making it the largest US bank to crash since the 2008 banking crisis.[5]

On Sunday, March 12, Treasury Secretary [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] appeared on *Face the Nation* and was asked whether a government bailout was on the table for Silicon Valley Bank in the wake of its collapse; she replied that during the financial crisis investors and owners of systemically large banks had been bailed out and that "the reforms that have been put in place means that we're not going to do that again," while adding that regulators were "concerned about depositors and are focused on trying to meet their needs."[5]

Badlands commentary on the episode was divided over intent. [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued the failure could trigger a collapse on the order of 2008 or 1929 and might have been an orchestrated implosion producing economic contagion, pointing to share sales by chief executive [[gregory-becker|Gregory Becker]] and two other SVB executives before the collapse, bonuses paid out before the seizure, and a [[bloomberg|Bloomberg]] report that [[jpmorgan-chase|JP Morgan]] had warned some SVB customers to move their funds on asset-safety grounds.[5] He characterised the result as an industry-driven bank run mounted by Silicon Valley venture capitalists, and suggested the eventual "bailout" could take the form of a move to [[central-bank-digital-currency|central bank digital currencies]].[5] Contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], by contrast, read [[peter-thiel|Peter Thiel]]'s withdrawals as evidence against a planned demolition of the banking system by entrenched interests.[5]

## Trigger of the March 2023 Banking Crisis

ZeroHedge described SVB as the first of many banks to fail when the Federal Reserve's tightening cycle ended "in disaster" in March 2023, triggering a chain of dominoes that culminated in the collapse of [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]] and its emergency sale to [[ubs|UBS]].[6] The cascade prompted the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] to announce coordinated daily U.S. dollar swap lines with all major central banks on March 19, 2023.[6]

## Aftermath and the deposit guarantee debate (March 2023)

The downfall of Silicon Valley Bank and [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]] rattled financial markets and prompted the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury]] and the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] to shore up the banking sector with special loans and guarantees of uninsured deposits at the failed institutions.[7] Economist [[daniel-lacalle|Daniel Lacalle]] told [[the-epoch-times|The Epoch Times]] that the failures were a symptom of a broader problem the central bank could not fix, and that repeated liquidity injections simply create new bubbles.[7] The episode also drove bank lobbying for a temporary blanket deposit guarantee, described at [[2023-proposal-to-guarantee-all-us-bank-deposits|the 2023 proposal to guarantee all US bank deposits]].[7]

## Insider loans before the collapse

Bloomberg reporting relayed by [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] in March 2023 found that SVB's loans to "insiders" — officers, directors and principal shareholders, and their related interests — more than tripled from the third quarter of 2022 to $219 million in the final three months of that year, a record dollar amount going back over 20 years.[8] ZeroHedge argued that insiders had not only been dumping shares at speed but simultaneously loading up on loans from the bank at a scale that "makes a mockery of any regulatory oversight," while noting that the terms, recipients and collateral of the loans would likely never be disclosed.[8] It also observed that if the loans had been collateralized by SVB shares, those shares were now worthless, leaving the loan-heavy C-suite obliged to find cash to repay debts that do not disappear with the bank's liquidation.[8]
