# Commission on Industrial Relations

The **Commission on Industrial Relations** was created under the Congressional Act of August 23, 1912 to study labor conditions and the treatment of workers by major U.S. industrial firms.[1] Badlands Media describes its work as producing the first true glimpse into the reality of foundation influence in American life.[1]

As [[rene-wormser|Rene A. Wormser]] summarized it, the commission, "starting with a study of labor exploitation... went on to investigate concentrations of economic power, interlocking directorates, and the role of the then relatively new large charitable foundations (especially of Carnegie and Rockefeller) as instruments of power concentration."[1]

During the hearings, future Supreme Court Justice [[louis-brandeis|Louis D. Brandeis]] testified on January 23, 1915 that he was seriously concerned about the emerging concentration of power, warning that "when a great financial power has developed... which can successfully summon forces from all parts of the country... there develops within the State a state so powerful that the ordinary social and industrial forces existing are insufficient to cope with it."[1] The commission's director of research, Basil M. Manly, declared that control was "being extended largely through the creation of enormous privately managed funds for indefinite purposes, hereinafter designated 'foundations'."[1]

The commission's report concluded that as regards foundations "created for unlimited general purposes and endowed with enormous resources, their ultimate possibilities are so grave a menace... [that] it would be desirable to recommend their abolition."[1] Badlands Media treats this as a matter of historical fact showing that Congress once formally judged [[tax-exempt-foundations|tax-exempt foundations]] to be potentially destructive to the republic.[1]
