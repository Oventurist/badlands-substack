# Pujo Committee

The Pujo Committee was a special congressional committee formed by Representative [[arsene-pujo|Arsene Pujo]] to investigate the monopoly of [[jp-morgan|J. P. Morgan]] and New York's other powerful bankers, following the creation of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

The article treats the Committee's 1913 findings as direct confirmation that a master plan was consummated that year.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) The Committee stated that it had "unanimously determined that a small CABAL of financiers had gained consolidated control of numerous industries through the abuse of the public trust in the United States."1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

## Findings on the banking trade

The Committee also issued a report on the banking trade at large. It found that no fewer than eighteen different major financial corporations were under the control of a cartel led by J. P. Morgan, George F. Baker, and James Stillman.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) The report singled out individual bankers including [[paul-warburg|Paul Warburg]], Jacob H. Schiff, Felix M. Warburg, Frank E. Peabody, William Rockefeller, and [[benjamin-strong|Benjamin Strong Jr.]]1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) It identified over $22 billion in resources and capitalization controlled through 341 directorships held in 112 corporations by members of the empire headed by J. P. Morgan.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

## Aftermath

Arsene Pujo left Congress in 1913 and had some support in the years that followed, but nothing came of it.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) According to the article, the [[cabal]] squashed the Committee's findings and, because it controlled the media, the report never reached the mass public — tactics the article says remain in use today.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

## Legacy in Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] has invoked the committee's findings to explain the structure of the modern American economy, writing that the Money Trust exposed by the Pujo Committee in the run-up to the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act of 1913]] sought to transform the way business was done by shifting enterprise away from saving and toward lending.[2] In his account the effort succeeded: the vast majority of major businesses now run on loans and credit extension, leaving them exposed whenever credit contracts.[2] He compared the last comparable shift to the aftermath of the [[panic-of-1907|Panic of 1907]], when massive portions of the private sector were bought up by the banker-controlled industrialists of the Money Trust, followed shortly by the rise of the military-industrial complex and of large-scale technocratic changes to society.[2]

In his April 27, 2023 essay "The Economy: Weaponized," Deschamps cited the Pujo Committee of 1912–1913 as the documentary example of one of the principal methods by which bankers eliminate competition: buying out rival banks outright.[3] He framed this within an analogy to a tomato monopoly, in which total domination of the market requires all other producers to close, whether by acquisition, by reputational sabotage, or by capturing the next generation so that the knowledge of how to grow tomatoes is lost entirely.[3]

## Creation and Findings

The Pujo Committee was created as a congressional subcommittee after the [[aldrich-plan|Aldrich bill]] was quickly identified as legislation designed to benefit the bankers, an appearance that rubbed many people — including members of Congress — the wrong way.[4] It was tasked with investigating [[jp-morgan|J.P. Morgan]] along with several other Wall Street bankers, bringing the [[panic-of-1907|Panic of 1907]] full circle politically.[4]

The Committee's report found that Morgan and others, including Rockefeller, [[paul-warburg|Paul Warburg]] and [[jacob-schiff|Jacob Schiff]], had gained control of major manufacturing, transportation, mining, telecommunications and financial markets throughout the United States.[4] Despite these findings, no action was ever taken against the financiers named, and the [[money-trust|money trust]] retained its position as the Glass-Owen Bill moved toward passage.[4]

## Comparison with the Pecora Commission

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] contrasts the 1911 Pujo Commission with the later [[pecora-commission|Pecora Commission]]: although Pujo also exposed Wall Street's abuses of power, the Pecora inquiry was supported by a president, [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]], "who actually cared about the Constitution and amplified Pecora's powers even further."[5]
