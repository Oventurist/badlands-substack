# Derek Chauvin

Derek Chauvin is the former Minneapolis police officer whose murder trial over the death of [[george-floyd|George Floyd]] became, in Badlands commentary, the paradigmatic example of mob influence over a criminal proceeding.[1]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] has described the Chauvin trial as a case in which the mob and its influencers ostensibly poisoned the jury pool and influenced the trial outcome in pursuit of "justice for George Floyd."[1]

In July 2026 she invoked the trial as a direct analogue for the conduct of [[turning-point-usa|Turning Point USA]]-aligned influencers surrounding the prosecution of [[tyler-robinson|Tyler Robinson]], arguing that the tactics were reminiscent of the Chauvin matter and that the apparent aim was to make the public reject any outcome other than a guilty verdict and the death penalty.[1] Her broader position is that mobs deciding judicial outcomes is worse for everyone and for posterity than any individual crime, and that calling balls and strikes on process is the most important job of a journalist in an age of weaponized justice.[1]

## Post-conviction challenge (December 2024)

On Monday, December 16, 2024, a judge ruled that lawyers for the former Minneapolis police officer could obtain tests on preserved samples of [[george-floyd|George Floyd]]'s heart, in the latest challenge to Chauvin's 2022 federal conviction.[2] Chauvin is simultaneously serving a 21-year federal sentence for violating Floyd's civil rights and a 22½-year state sentence for second-degree murder.[2]

In November of the prior year, Chauvin's attorney filed a motion to vacate the federal charges related to Floyd's May 2020 death, with Chauvin asserting he would not have pleaded guilty had he known of the theories of Kansas pathologist Dr. William Schaetzel, who does not believe Chauvin's actions caused the death.[2] Schaetzel suggests Floyd died from a high level of catecholamines — a neurohormone central to the fight-or-flight stress response — or from Takotsubo myocarditis, a heart condition that develops in response to intense emotional or physical experience.[2] The motion also accuses Chauvin's trial attorney, Eric Nelson, of ineffective counsel for failing to inform his client of Schaetzel's theory and failing to test samples of Floyd's heart despite the doctor's recommendations.[2]

## 2023 prison stabbing

On a Friday in late November 2023, Chauvin was stabbed by another inmate and seriously injured at the [[fci-tucson|Federal Correctional Institution, Tucson]], a medium-security federal prison in Arizona that had been plagued by security lapses and staffing shortages.[3] A person familiar with the matter, who was not authorised to discuss details publicly, described the attack to the [[associated-press|Associated Press]] on condition of anonymity.[3] The [[federal-bureau-of-prisons|Bureau of Prisons]] confirmed that an incarcerated person was assaulted at the facility at around 12:30 p.m. local time and said responding employees contained the incident and performed "life-saving measures" before the unnamed inmate was taken to hospital for further treatment and evaluation.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the story sickened her less for the stabbing itself — she said she hoped Chauvin was alright — than for how easily it reset the tribalism afflicting the [[collective-mind|Collective Mind]], noting that one of her friend groups immediately began "ripping each other apart" over whether Chauvin had it coming.[3] She compared the dynamic to the aftermath of October 7, arguing that the central narrative was ramping up tribal rhetoric and demanding that people choose a side, that any disagreement was being treated as a threat to one's life, and that "empathy has been weaponized to the point of insanity."[3] Her conclusion was that the outrage was exactly where its managers wanted it, and that the correct response was to "just say no."[3]
