# Online Safety Bill

The **Online Safety Bill (OSB)** is United Kingdom legislation passed by the UK Parliament in **September 2023** that establishes a regulatory framework for internet services and social media platforms operating in Britain. The bill's full legislative title is *"A Bill to make provision for and in connection with the regulation by OFCOM of certain internet services; for and in connection with communications offences; and for connected purposes."* It was the central legislative instrument discussed in the Badlands Media article *5GW Counter-Offensive – The Censorship Regime and the Road to 2024* (September 27, 2023), which framed it as the institutional foundation for a coordinated censorship offensive ahead of the November 2024 elections.

## Regulatory Framework

The OSB assigns primary regulatory responsibility to the **Office of Communications (Ofcom)** and imposes a compliance-or-fine structure on tech platforms including Facebook, YouTube, and TikTok. According to the Badlands source, platforms that ignore the bill's rules face "humongous fines," and persistent non-compliance could result in a platform being banned outright within the UK. The bill was publicly marketed by the UK government as a measure to make the UK "the safest place in the world to be online."

## Controversial Provisions

The source highlights several provisions it characterizes as vague and censorious. **Section 10** criminalizes the transmission of "harmful, false and threatening communications" without clearly defining what constitutes "harmful" or the criteria for determining "false." **Clause 156** makes it a criminal offense to "send a message" containing false information, while **Clause 157** in the next breath grants immunity to every newspaper, television channel, and streaming service. The source argues that this asymmetry creates a two-tier speech regime in which legacy media enjoy broad protections while alternative and individual speakers face criminal liability.

## The Pretext Argument

The Badlands source argues that the bill's public emphasis on combating child pornography functions as pretextual window-dressing, asserting that 99% of child pornography exists on the deep web outside the bill's purview. The article draws an analogy to the **USA PATRIOT Act**, arguing that evocative framing is used to secure public acceptance for provisions that primarily serve to curtail political dissent. The source predicts that the UK Parliament will use the bill to issue threats to platforms demanding the removal of "effective critic[s] of the state" on the grounds that they are "peddling harmful conspiracy theories."

## International Parallels and Enforcement

The article notes a precedent in **2022**, when the French government demanded that Rumble remove accounts belonging to Russian news sources (Sputnik, RT). When Rumble rejected the demands, France effectively blocked access to the site. The source presents this as a model for how the OSB could be used to remove Rumble from the UK entirely. The article also reports that following the Online Safety Bill's passage, the British government asked big-tech platforms to censor **Russell Brand's** online content, and that some platforms complied.

## Relationship to 5GW Narrative

Within the Badlands corpus, the Online Safety Bill is framed as the legal mechanism through which the quadrennial censorship offensive identified with **5th Generation Warfare** becomes institutionalized in Western democracies. The article connects the bill to the broader activities of the **Trusted News Initiative** and to coordinated pressure campaigns against alternative platforms and communicators.
