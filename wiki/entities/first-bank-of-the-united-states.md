# First Bank of the United States

The First Bank of the United States was the national bank designed by Treasury Secretary [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] as the third pillar of his four-fold solution to the financial ruin of the American republic in the period 1783–1791.[1] Hamilton laid out the institution in his *1790 Report on a National Bank* and defended its constitutionality in his 1791 paper *On the Constitutionality of a National Bank*.[1] A statue of Hamilton stands on the opposite side of the bank building.[1]

## Design and purpose

Hamilton's conception was of national banks subservient to the Constitution and to the General Welfare, in explicit contrast to a system of central banks on the British model that, in [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s account, ensured nation-states would remain forever subservient to the laws of usurious finance.[1] The bank's authority was to be grounded in the powers enumerated in Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution, which also underwrote protective tariffs and internal improvements.[1] Hamilton premised the design on the unification of private profit with the wellbeing of the whole nation, seeking to overcome the dichotomy of state versus individual rights that had plagued so much of philosophy and human history.[1]

Explaining the necessity of the institution, Hamilton wrote that a nation without mines of its own must derive precious metals from others in exchange for the products of its labor and industry, so that "the state of its agriculture and manufactures, the quantity and quality of its labor and industry must, in the main, influence and determine the increase or decrease of its gold and silver."[1] He added that the stagnation of industry was the natural consequence of an inadequate medium of exchange, one that "without the aid of Bank circulation, would in the cases supposed, be severely felt."[1]

## Contested legacy

Among alternative-media networks, Ehret notes, a consensus formed over the years around the thesis that Hamilton was a Rothschild stooge whose national bank was the forerunner to the later [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], and whose writings in the [[federalist-papers|Federalist Papers]] justified the modern deep state.[1] Ehret rejects this reading, arguing that most people who discuss Hamilton — admirers and detractors alike — do not know what they are talking about.[1] The national banking tradition was later broken when [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] killed the [[second-bank-of-the-united-states|Second National Bank]] in 1836, and an attempt to charter a third bank died with President [[william-henry-harrison|William Harrison]] in 1840.[1]
