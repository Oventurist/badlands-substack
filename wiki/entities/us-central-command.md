# U.S. Central Command

U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM) is the American combatant command responsible for military operations in the Middle East, including the waters around the Arabian Peninsula.[1]

On April 19, 2026, CENTCOM said American forces enforced naval blockade measures against the Iranian-flagged cargo vessel M/V Touska as it headed toward an Iranian port.[1] According to the command, the destroyer USS Spruance intercepted the ship in the north Arabian Sea, issued warnings, and then fired into its engine room after the crew did not comply.[1] CENTCOM further said that U.S. Marines subsequently boarded the vessel and that it remained in U.S. custody.[1]

[[iran|Iran]] said it would respond to what it described as U.S. "armed piracy," and Iranian state media indicated Tehran would not take part in a second round of negotiations that Washington had hoped to begin before the ceasefire expired.[1] The interception therefore functioned less as a discrete naval action than as a narrative event inside the broader [[fake-iran-war|Iran war narrative]], arriving immediately after [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] had attempted to mandate de-escalation.[1]

## March 2026: Strait of Hormuz warning and information war

In March 2026, US Central Command warned Iranian civilians to keep clear of ports near the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], stating: "Iranian dockworkers, administrative personnel, and commercial vessel crews should avoid Iranian naval vessels and military equipment."[2] The command said [[iran|Iranian]] naval forces had positioned military vessels and equipment within civilian ports serving commercial maritime traffic, effectively co-locating military targets with commercial shipping.[2]

CENTCOM also engaged directly in the information war, posting on X on March 11, 2026 to rebut an [[islamic-revolutionary-guard-corps|IRGC]] leader's claim that a US F-15 had been shot down south of Tehran: "LIE. No U.S. fighter aircraft have been shot down by Iran. U.S. forces continue to exercise air superiority over vast swaths of Iran. TRUTH."[2] The exchange came as Iran accused US officials of "posting fake news to manipulate markets," a charge CENTCOM mirrored against Tehran.[2]

## January 2026 strikes on ISIS in Syria

On January 10, 2026, US Central Command said US forces, working with partner forces, had conducted large-scale strikes against [[isis|ISIS]] targets in Syria.[4] The operation was reported alongside a wider set of American power-projection moves that week, including the Level 4 travel advisory for [[venezuela|Venezuela]] and pressure on [[cuba|Cuba]] and [[greenland|Greenland]].[4]

## First American fatalities in the Iran campaign (March 2026)

CENTCOM confirmed that three U.S. service members were killed and five others seriously wounded on Sunday, March 1, 2026 during U.S. military attacks on [[iran|Iran]], the first American fatalities of the escalating conflict, and said additional losses were possible before the campaign ended.[5] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] described the effort as "major combat operations" that could continue for weeks; U.S. forces had targeted more than 1,000 Iranian sites in the preceding days as part of [[operation-epic-fury|Operation Epic Fury]], while Iran retaliated with missile and drone attacks on U.S. and allied bases across the Middle East.[5]

## Tower 22 Drone Attack (January 2024)

U.S. Central Command confirmed in a statement on Sunday, January 28, 2024 that three service members were killed and at least 34 injured in a one-way drone attack that "impacted at a base in northeast Jordan."[6] Eight injured service members were medically evacuated for higher-level care, CENTCOM said, and officials expected the number of wounded to rise as personnel sought treatment for symptoms consistent with traumatic brain injury.[6] The strike on the outpost known as Tower 22, near the Syrian border, marked the first time U.S. troops had been killed by enemy fire in the Middle East since the start of the Gaza war; officials said the drone was fired by Iran-backed militants and appeared to come from Syria, though the specific militia responsible remained undetermined.[6]
