# British Columbia

British Columbia is the Pacific province of [[canada|Canada]], and in Matthew Ehret's account the decisive contested territory in the struggle over whether North America's rail systems would connect the United States to Russia across the Bering Strait.[1]

## An isolated and bankrupt colony

Before Confederation, British Columbia was an isolated and bankrupt British outpost on the west coast, separated from Britain's other four colonies on the east coast by 3,000 kilometres of undeveloped wilderness privately owned by the [[hudsons-bay-company|Hudson's Bay Company]].[1] The collapse of the gold rush bubbles in 1858 left the colony in economic despair, and the great majority of its subjects favoured annexation to the United States.[1] Persuading these "fiercely independent" subjects to join Confederation was the third of three tasks Ehret identifies for British strategists in the 1865-1867 period.[1]

## The annexation movement

The 1867 [[alaska-purchase|sale of Alaska]] suddenly made the colony "very hot real estate."[1] On 2 July 1867 — one day after the [[british-north-america-act|British North America Act]] took effect — the first of several petitions was sent to Queen Victoria asking either that the colony's debts and economic woes be relieved by the mother country, or that the Queen grant permission to annex to the USA.[1] American consul to Victoria Allen Francis wrote to the president that "even the colonists claiming most loyalty to the queen, are now urging with great unanimity annexation to the United States as their only salvation."[1] The colony's *Colonialist* newspaper reported that British Columbians "would welcome annexation to the United States to continuing in a state of poverty and wretchedness," claiming to speak the mind of nine out of every ten men in the colony.[1]

The last serious effort was the [[annexation-petition-of-1869|Annexation petition of 1869]], which listed the colony's grievances and appealed to President Grant, declaring that nearly all commercial relations were with the adjoining American states and territories and earnestly desiring "the ACQUISITION of this Colony by the United States."[1]

## Entry into Confederation

Ottawa negotiations began on 7 June 1870 and settled nearly all clauses within weeks.[1] The two biggest impediments were removed by Ottawa's payment of all the colony's debts and [[john-a-macdonald|Sir John A. Macdonald]]'s promise to build a rail line linking the province to Montreal and Quebec "within ten years."[1] British Columbia was admitted as the sixth Canadian province, in arrangements paralleling those in the former Red River Settlement in today's Manitoba.[1] Lack of rail progress by 1878 revived annexation demands, producing Macdonald's National Policy of 1878-1885 and the arrival of the first through train at Port Moody on 4 July 1885.[1]

## Rail development and the northern gap

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] writes that anyone travelling by rail north from the United States can reach only as far as British Columbia, since a 1,000 km gap separates all existing track from Alaska, with the northernmost line ending at Dease Lake — built over fifty years ago under Premier [[wac-bennett|W.A.C. Bennett]] and long derided as "the railway to nowhere."[2] Bennett, premier from 1952 to 1972, fought to make the province more than the colonial underdeveloped region its British rulers had condemned it to be, nationalizing BC Power Corporation and creating the Bank of British Columbia after clashes with Ottawa technocrats over the [[columbia-river-treaty|Columbia River Treaty]].[2] Alaska–British Columbia–Yukon conferences in the 1960s advanced his plan to link the provincial rail system to Alaska's, a connection Ehret ties to the [[alaska-canada-railway|Alaska-Canada Railway]] and the [[bering-strait-tunnel|Bering Strait]] corridor.[2]
