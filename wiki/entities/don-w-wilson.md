# Don W. Wilson

**Don W. Wilson** was the [[archivist-of-the-united-states|Archivist of the United States]], appointed to the office on December 4, 1987 near the end of the Reagan administration and shortly before [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] won the presidency.[1] In that role he controlled all presidential documents generated by both the Reagan and Bush administrations.[1]

## The exclusive control agreement

At 11:30 P.M. on January 19 — Bush's last full day in office — Wilson signed what the New York Times described as an "unusual agreement" giving Bush "exclusive legal control of all Presidential information," including more than 4,000 computer tapes from the [[national-security-council|National Security Council]] and other White House offices.[1] Research groups moved to challenge the agreement on the grounds that it violated two recent federal court orders protecting the files.[1] [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] emphasizes that the agreement covered everything, not merely a defined subset of records.[1]

The tapes at issue contained millions of computer correspondences, significant and trivial, concerning domestic and foreign policy initiatives during the Reagan and Bush years, and had been the subject of a long-running lawsuit between the Bush administration and research groups seeking their preservation.[1] The material was largely classified and had never been publicly described; some of it was considered potentially crucial to historians and investigators reconstructing high-level decision making, much as electronic mail records had supplied the most important evidence in the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] trials of Oliver L. North and John M. Poindexter.[1]

## Resignation and conflict of interest

Three weeks after signing the agreement, Wilson announced he would leave the National Archives on March 31 to become executive director of the George Bush Center at Texas A&M University — the institution attached to the [[george-bush-presidential-library|Bush presidential library]].[1] Three Democratic senators asked the Justice Department and ethics officials to investigate the Archivist over the sequence of events.[1]

Through National Archives spokeswoman Susan Cooper, Wilson said the agreement had been drawn up by lawyers from the Justice Department and the White House and that he had been assured it was "proper and legal."[1] Cooper said she did not know whether Wilson had told those lawyers he was simultaneously under consideration for the presidential library position.[1] Lange characterizes the arrangement as an obvious conflict of interest and quid pro quo.[1]

## Judicial findings

In January, Judge Charles R. Richey of the Federal District Court found that Wilson had violated the law governing federal record keeping by failing to establish guidelines for the preservation of electronic messages.[1] Fearing that Bush officials would destroy the computer tapes, the judge twice ordered all the material preserved.[1] Michael Tankersley, a lawyer at Public Citizen — the organization founded by Ralph Nader and one of the groups that filed the lawsuit — criticized the Wilson–Bush agreement as giving too much control to the former president, saying it "purports to rewrite the records law so that Bush can delay and restrict access."[1] Tankersley added that on January 20, Bush and archives officials removed 4,000 to 5,000 computer tapes as well as the hard disk drives from 147 computers at the National Security Council and roughly 300 other personal computers at the White House.[1]
