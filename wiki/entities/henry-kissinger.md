# Henry Kissinger

Henry Kissinger was the American Secretary of State whose 1974 negotiations with Saudi Arabia created the petrodollar system, and who in Badlands commentary is treated as a principal architect of the postwar globalist network.[1]

The 1973 Yom Kippur War between the Arab states and Israel produced the oil crisis in the West, culminating in the negotiated settlement reached in 1974 by Kissinger with Saudi King Faisal that created the [[petrodollar|petrodollar]]. The deal placed Aramco, the Saudi national oil company, firmly at the top of the industry's food chain, while Iran was forced to remain subservient to the British.[1]

According to [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]], Kissinger immediately resigned from Nixon's cabinet to focus on building the globalist network through the [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]] and the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]], while King Faisal was assassinated a year later by a young, brainwashed nephew. [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]], later National Security Advisor to [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]], organized the Trilateral Commission for his mentor Kissinger and was a high-profile member of the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]].[1]

Badlands commentary in February 2025 on the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]]'s withdrawal from the [[network-of-central-banks-and-supervisors-for-greening-the-financial-system|NGFS]] cited commentator [[warhamster|WarHamster]] naming Kissinger as one of the "Four Horsemen of the Climate Cult," together with [[maurice-strong|Maurice Strong]], [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]] and [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]].[2] The argument traced the climate agenda to [[thomas-malthus|Thomas Malthus]]'s 1798 population theory and characterized it as psychological warfare premised on artificial scarcity.[2]

## Death (November 2023)

Kissinger died on Wednesday, November 29, 2023, at the age of 100; his death was announced by his consulting firm, and no cause was given.[3] Obituary coverage described him as a ruthless practitioner of realpolitik and a cunning, erudite strategist whose transformative diplomatic efforts helped reshape the world.[3]

As Secretary of State he was permanently linked to President [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]], particularly for their work in three areas: extracting the United States from the Vietnam War, opening diplomatic relations with China, and reducing tensions with the Soviet Union.[3] His work with Nixon and President [[gerald-ford|Gerald Ford]] made him the Republican Party's elder statesman on foreign policy for decades afterward.[3] Historian George C. Herring, quoted in "America's Longest War," wrote that "the Middle American professional politician and the German-born Harvard professor" could hardly have differed more in background but shared "a love of power and a burning ambition to mold a fluid world," adding that as loners and outsiders in their own professions they were perhaps naturally drawn to each other.[3]

In 1973 Kissinger shared the Nobel Peace Prize with his North Vietnamese counterpart [[le-duc-tho|Le Duc Tho]] for negotiating the accord ending the Vietnam War, signed January 27, 1973; the Nobel committee said it had "brought a wave of joy and hope for peace over the entire world," though the prize was later characterized as premature for ending a war that kept going.[3]

## Early life and education

Kissinger was born in Germany in 1923 and came to the United States in 1938 as a Jewish refugee fleeing Nazi persecution.[5] He served in the U.S. Army during the Second World War and was afterwards educated at [[harvard-university|Harvard University]], where he became a professor of government and earned an international reputation as an expert on nuclear weapons and foreign policy.[5] He frequently consulted for government agencies, think tanks, and the presidential campaigns of [[nelson-rockefeller|Nelson Rockefeller]] and [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]] before being appointed national security advisor.[5]

## Alleged British Cabal recruitment

Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argued that Kissinger was not an originator of policy but "just a middle-man" whose job was to ensure that his Cabal overlords' agendas were carried out, describing him as a puppet and "an employee of the company."[5] Carlson pointed to Kissinger's 1995 honorary knighthood from [[queen-elizabeth-ii|Queen Elizabeth II]] as evidence that a non-elected American official was being rewarded by a foreign power, and asserted that Kissinger also worked with the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]].[5]

According to Carlson, Kissinger's mentor at Harvard was [[william-yandell-elliott|William Yandell Elliott]], who recruited him into the [[cecil-rhodes|Rhodes]]-funded secret society in 1951; Kissinger then joined the American counterpart of the [[round-table-movement|Round Table]], the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]], in 1956.[5] Elliott's Harvard International Seminar, which Kissinger directed, was described in a 2002 Executive Intelligence Review article as an instrument for extending the Round Table movement worldwide.[5] Carlson further claimed that Kissinger in turn mentored [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]] at Harvard.[5]

Kissinger served as National Security Advisor from 1969 to 1975 and as Secretary of State from 1973 to 1977, spanning the [[watergate|Watergate]] collapse of the Nixon presidency and the succession of [[gerald-ford|Gerald Ford]].[5]

## War crimes allegations

An Al Jazeera commentary published after his death argued that Kissinger was directly responsible, through his actions, for the murders of between three and four million people during his eight years in office between 1969 and 1977, citing Yale historian Greg Grandin's book *Kissinger's Shadow*, and that the bloody policies he promoted paved the way for America's later never-ending wars.[5] Seen as the architect of US efforts to contain the Soviet Union and communist influence, he introduced a "bombs over diplomacy" approach that produced some of the most brutal bombing campaigns in modern history.[5]

The approach was first applied during the [[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]], where Kissinger pushed for the carpet bombing of Vietnam and of neighbouring [[cambodia|Cambodia]].[5] The 1969 assault was approved secretly without Congress being informed, and declassified Pentagon reports state that Kissinger personally approved 3,875 air raids dropping some 540,000 tons of bombs on Cambodia within the campaign's first year; unexploded ordnance continues to kill civilians today.[5] Rather than halting the communists, the bombing facilitated their victory, with the [[khmer-rouge|Khmer Rouge]] going on to commit a genocide of between 1.5 and two million people.[5] Anthony Bourdain's remark that "once you've been to Cambodia, you'll never stop wanting to beat Henry Kissinger to death with your bare hands" was quoted as the popular verdict.[5] Kissinger nevertheless received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1973, a pattern Carlson compared to [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]]'s 2009 prize followed by eight years of drone strikes, contrasting both with the treatment of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[5]

Carlson also rejected the idea that Kissinger's death at age 100 was unremarkable timing, suggesting it occurred in the middle of the Israel–Hamas conflict so that people would examine his history and connections.[5]
