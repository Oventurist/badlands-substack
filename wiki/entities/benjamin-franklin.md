# Benjamin Franklin

Benjamin Franklin was an American statesman, scientist and organiser whose networks are presented in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as the coordinating force behind both the American Revolution and the artistic mission of [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] in England.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Philadelphia networks

Franklin's Pennsylvania Philosophical Society drew in the young Benjamin West within a short time of his emergence as a self-taught painter.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) West's father, who ran a boarding house, was a very close friend of Franklin, and in 1751 or 1752 made his home the first building in the world to install a lightning rod.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) Friends and patrons within Franklin's circle sponsored West's 1769 voyage to France and Italy after seeing his early painting *The Last Days of Socrates*.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) The article argues that official narratives wrongly infer that few points of contact existed between Franklin and West.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Organising the Revolution

Franklin's networks had been planning the conditions for a declaration of independence for decades, a thesis the article attributes to the late historian [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]] in his *How the Nation Was Won: Volume 1 (1630–1757)*.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) The 1759 fall of French Quebec at the [[battle-of-quebec|Battle of Quebec]] removed the French Empire's stranglehold on North America and gave Franklin's networks the room to organise.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) It was understood that Quebec would side with the republican cause and become the fourteenth signatory of the Declaration of Independence; Franklin travelled to Quebec in 1774 to organise its elites and population, and had established Canada's first postal service in 1759 to secure lines of communication and elevate Canadian culture.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) Bribes and other manoeuvres ensured this did not come to pass.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

Franklin and West both understood that the true battle for independence was only beginning after the British admitted defeat in 1781, given top-down imperial policies designed to deny the colonists manufacturing, keep them impoverished, and bar their movement west beyond the Allegheny Mountains — a policy aimed at crushing the Promethean spirit in the colonists.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## The Prometheus of America

Following his 1752 discoveries of what he called the "electric fire from the gods," Franklin became known as "The Prometheus of America."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) West's 1816 painting *Benjamin Franklin Drawing Electricity from the Sky* depicts the key experiment capturing a charge from a thunderbolt, and is described as embodying the Promethean principle with little subtlety.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) Franklin's discoveries are said to have given rise directly to the advances in electrical communication made by [[samuel-morse|Samuel Morse]], achieved with the help of scientists around the Humboldt brothers including Carl F. Gauss, Wilhelm Weber and Ampère.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) Franklin's own great-grandson Alexander Dallas Bache figured prominently in this Göttingen–Philadelphia complex of genius during the nineteenth century.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

Franklin appears in West's unfinished 1783 painting *The Peace of Paris* alongside John Jay, John Adams, Henry Laurens and his grandson [[william-temple-franklin|William Temple Franklin]], whom he was training in statecraft.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) Franklin and West are also described as understanding viscerally the occultism embedded throughout the British imperial elite, including the [[hellfire-club|Hellfire Club]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Recruitment of Casimir Pulaski

Franklin spent most of the period between 1750 and 1780 in Europe engaged in diplomacy.[2] After the Partition of Poland began in 1772, he approached Count [[casimir-pulaski|Casimir Pulaski]], leader of the [[winged-hussars|Hussars]] — then without a country and no longer wearing their iconic wings — and convinced him to bring his horsemen to America to fight the British Empire, some 90 years after the Hussars had relieved Vienna from the [[ottoman-empire|Ottomans]].[2] Pulaski would go on to save the life of [[george-washington|George Washington]] at the Battle of Brandywine and become known as the "Father of the American Cavalry."[2]

## On Colonial Scrip

Franklin is quoted at length in [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]]' essay "Cracks in the Curtain" as the authority on colonial monetary prosperity.[3] Asked how the colonies had come to enjoy such prosperity, he is reported to have replied: "That is simple. In the Colonies we issue our own money. It is called Colonial Scrip. We issue it in proper proportion to the demands of trade and industry to make the products pass easily from the producers to the consumers. In this manner creating for ourselves our own paper money, we control its purchasing power, and we have no interest to pay."[3]

The essay sets the quotation against the [[navigation-acts|Navigation Acts]] imposed by Great Britain before the Revolution, which it says all but crippled the colonies and prompted the colonists to create [[colonial-scrip|Colonial Scrip]] free from the controls of the [[bank-of-england|Bank of England]] — producing what Deschamps calls one of the most economically successful periods in American history.[3] He concludes that the founders understood deeply what [[money|money]] was and how much it had been used to control the colonies.[3]

## Government as a "science of happiness"

Badlands contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] writes that within his many essays Franklin — "the great scientist, inventor and statesman" — explained to the world that government was not a "science of control" or a "science of stability" as many elites of his day and ours wish to assume.[4] Franklin and other leading scientist-statesmen instead held that government is better understood as an applied technology advancing a "science of happiness", whose practical expression, like any technological expression of scientific concepts, is endowed with the seeds of its own self-improvement infused into the design.[4]

Ehret credits this outlook with producing the operating principle of constant self-perfectibility embedded in the American foundational documents of 1776 and 1787, expressed in the seemingly contradictory phrase "a more perfect union" — a construction a logician would call absurd, but which Franklin and his allies, being scientists rather than logicians, knew better than to reject.[4] Government "of, by and for the people" was never meant to become a fixed, crystallized or static machine, since stasis suffocating the creative spirit behind the law would doom a society to decadence, stupefaction and absolute tyranny.[4] Accordingly Franklin and the other authors of the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]] affirmed the right of the people to alter or abolish any form of government that becomes destructive of these ends.[4]

Ehret places Franklin alongside [[friedrich-schiller|Friedrich Schiller]], [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]] and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] as the humanist antidote to the [[transhumanism|transhumanist religion]] promoted by [[yuval-noah-harari|Yuval Noah Harari]], and notes that the material facts of humanity's relationship to nature over several thousand years support Franklin's ideas against the Malthusians and eugenicists.[4]

## Electricity and the Platonic scientific tradition

Ehret situates Franklin within a Platonic tradition of electrical research reaching back to Thales of Miletus and Plato's *The Timaeus*, revived by the English Platonist William Gilbert, the astronomer Johannes Kepler and the physicist Stephen Gray, and which Franklin encountered during his early work in England in the 1720s.[5] His experiments demonstrated that the discharge of thunderclouds is of the same nature as that of a Leyden jar, and he was the first to develop a thorough theory explaining the negative and positive components of this subtle "fluid."[5] The discoveries he made in the 1750–53 period polarised the thinking of Europeans and Americans alike and stimulated an explosion of work by Alessandro Volta, Carl Friedrich Gauss, Wilhelm Weber and André-Marie Ampère, culminating in the [[telegraph|telegraph]] of [[samuel-morse|Samuel Morse]], built with the help of [[joseph-henry|Joseph Henry]] and Franklin's grandson Alexander Dallas Bache.[5] Franklin appears as a painting-within-the-painting in Christian Schussele's 1862 canvas [[men-of-progress|*Men of Progress*]].[5]

## Republican organising and the Franklin legend

Franklin, as ambassador to France during the revolution, orchestrated the 1777 French intervention that tipped the balance against the British Empire, drawing in the young [[marquis-de-lafayette|Marquis de Lafayette]].[5] The Philosophical Society he founded is described as an international republican agency with members across Russia, Europe and the United States, working closely with the [[society-of-the-cincinnati|Society of the Cincinnatus]].[5] Ehret recalls the apocryphal exchange in which Franklin, asked what had been given after the Constitution was established, replied "a republic, if you can keep it," and stresses the conditionality of that clause: the formula is not successful forever, but requires the continual reliving of the ideas that gave birth to the revolutionary spark.[5] A bust of Franklin appears in Morse's 1826 portrait of Lafayette wearing an expression of bewilderment and irony that Ehret says exists on no actual bust of Franklin anywhere on earth, invented by Morse for the message he wished to convey.[5]

## As an early practitioner of propaganda

Badlands contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] invokes Franklin to rebut the notion that there was ever a time in America when propaganda was not being used on its own people.[6] Franklin owned multiple newspapers and used various aliases and pseudonyms to put forth different and opposing talking points, in some cases using them to argue back and forth with himself on the same subject.[6] Carlson jokes that Franklin "may have been the original Fed Boy."[6]

## The Hell-Fire Club allegation

The third instalment of Badlands Media's "Founders & Foundations" series, following entries on [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] and [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]], addresses what it calls the most pervasive slander against Franklin over the past 270 years: his relationship to the [[hellfire-club|Hell-Fire Club]], formed in the early eighteenth century as a central agency of occult operations across the British Empire.[7] The essay concedes that Franklin's involvement with England's Hell-Fire Club during 1728–1730 is a fact, but argues that a much larger story has been lost in the piles of gossip, and that if the accusation were the whole truth one could argue the American Revolution was nothing more than a controlled occult revolution sponsored by the very forces running the British Empire.[7]

Instead the essay presents Franklin as a counter-intelligence agent. Recruited at the age of fifteen in 1721 by the aging [[cotton-mather|Cotton Mather]], Franklin joined the battle against the occult networks in the middle of one of the most intensive campaigns to destroy the Mathers their opponents had ever mounted — a campaign in which his own half-brother [[james-franklin|James Franklin]], to whom he had been unwillingly apprenticed in 1718, stood on the enemy side alongside the Anglican propagandist [[john-checkley|John Checkley]].[7] Historian [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]] argues that when Franklin arrived in London, "a most vital intelligence task would have been to dissect this new form of the beast: the politically powerful networks of the Hell-Fire," a case Lowry lays out in his essay "Who Was the Real Benjamin Franklin" and his 1987 book *How the Nation Was Won vol 1*.[7] The essay's appendix dates Franklin's deployment to England on the counter-intelligence operation against the London Hellfire Club to 1725.[7]

## Doing good against Mandeville

Franklin's worldview stood in direct contrast to [[bernard-mandeville|Bernard Mandeville]]'s Hell-Fire thesis of "Private Vices = Public Virtues."[7] His approach to indiscriminate acts of improvement was highly motivated by his early study of Cotton Mather's 1710 book *Essays to Do Good*, which Franklin described as "an influence on my conduct through life; for I have always set a greater value on the character of a doer of good, than on any other kind of reputation."[7] After returning from England he became a leading republican figure within the networks established by Mather, Jonathan Spotiswood and William Pitt, and was introduced to Gottfried Leibniz and other continental scientists through the vast library of Spotiswood's advisor [[james-logan|James Logan]].[7]

For many years Franklin favoured not revolution but reform of the British Empire, which he held had only recently been hijacked by the Venetian Party faction during the Glorious Revolution of 1688; he believed the republican spirit of Thomas More, Erasmus and Shakespeare could still transform the Empire into something in harmony with humanity's well-being.[7]

## Science, arts and internal improvements

This belief led Franklin to found the British [[lunar-society|Lunar Society]] while acting as representative to Britain in 1757, a group including Matthew Boulton, Josiah Wedgewood and Erasmus Darwin that drove internal improvements, industrial growth and living standards in Britain.[7] His influence across England, France and beyond rested on his reputation as the leading scientist on the planet, whose published discoveries of 1751 into electricity transformed the world and earned him the name "Prometheus of America"; despite having no formal education beyond the age of ten, his genius and humble origins demonstrated that the European belief in aristocratic nobility and hereditary institutions had no foundation in truth.[7] The essay stresses that his years of research into electricity were intimately tied to his concepts of natural law and government that later shaped the crafting of the US republic.[7]

In the arts, his close friend and fellow Philadelphian [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] organised the creation of the [[royal-academy-of-arts|Royal Academy of the Arts]] in 1772 and remained its president until his death in 1830, gaining access to the inner court of George III that provided invaluable intelligence to the young republic.[7]

## Systematic acts of goodness

In the thirteen colonies Franklin created the first fire department (1736) and public library (1731) and founded the [[university-of-pennsylvania|University of Pennsylvania]].[7] Judging that the American population of the 1730s lacked the moral or cognitive fortitude for revolutionary change, he created [[poor-richards-almanac|*Poor Richard's Almanac*]], wrapping moral lessons into poetry, science, astronomy and philosophy, and wrote countless humorous essays under pen names such as Silence Dogood, Martha Careful, Richard Saunders and Anthony Afterwit in the manner of the satirist-republican Jonathan Swift.[7] Following Swift's lead he also argued against British population control in his *Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind*.[7]

Franklin studied Chinese culture and strove to model western reforms on the best principles of Confucianism and the Chinese constitution, publishing the first English translations of Confucius' *Analects* in the colonies in 1750 and writing in 1747 that "Confucius was my example. I followed Confucius."[7] As early as 1729 he codified a banking system tied not to the worship of money or markets but to internal improvements in [[a-modest-enquiry-into-the-nature-and-necessity-of-paper-currency|*A Modest Enquiry into the Nature and Necessity of Paper Currency*]], deriving from his studies of Colbertist dirigism and preceding the American System reports of his protégé Alexander Hamilton in 1790–91.[7] His 1743 *Proposal for Promotion of Useful Knowledge* called for the creation of the [[american-philosophical-society|American Philosophical Society]], part of an international network of collaborators reaching Russia, France, Germany, Prussia, Spain, Italy, India and Morocco in the footsteps of Leibniz.[7]

## Franklin in Canada

Franklin's nation-building efforts extended to [[canada|Canada]], whose first postal service and first newspaper he created.[7] The postal service was established in 1753 at Halifax as part of his overhaul of communications infrastructure in the Americas, with mail services extended to Quebec City and Montreal after the French defeat in the Seven Years' War in 1763; Franklin had been made Post-Master General in 1753, the same year his kite experiment made him an international sensation.[7] Montreal's *Gazette* was founded by the French republican [[fleury-mesplet|Fleury Mesplet]], recruited to counteract the effects of the French feudal system on the cognitive powers of Quebec colonists.[7] Though Franklin campaigned actively to persuade the Quebecois to become a fourteenth member of the united colonies, his mission failed through bribes, acts of treason and short-sighted thinking, and the Quebecois chose submission to the Crown — one of the very few failures Franklin faced in his life.[7]

## Keystones of his life

The essay's appendix credits Franklin with inventing bifocals, the glass armonica, the rocking chair and the lightning rod, discovering the Gulf Stream in 1775, and coining terms such as "battery," "charge" and "conductor."[7] It lists his election to the Philadelphia City Council in 1748, membership of the Colonial Assembly in 1751, appointment as Post-Master General of the Americas in 1752, his 1754 Plan of Union adopted by the Albany Congress, appointment as Pennsylvania ambassador to England in 1757, the Canada Pamphlets of 1760, election to the Continental Congress in 1775, his 1776 commission to Canada, his leadership of the four-man committee that drafted the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]], his appointment as Ambassador to France, and his organising of the Treaty of Alliance with France (1778), the Treaty of Paris (1783) and the U.S. Constitution (1787).[7]
