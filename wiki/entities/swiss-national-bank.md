# Swiss National Bank

The Swiss National Bank (SNB) is Switzerland's central bank and the institution responsible for the country's monetary policy and for acting as lender of last resort to Swiss financial institutions. It entered the news cycle of mid-March 2023 as the emergency backstop behind [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]], one of Switzerland's two globally systemic banks.[1]

## Role in the March 2023 Credit Suisse rescue

On Wednesday, March 15, 2023, Credit Suisse disclosed "material weaknesses" in its financial reporting and announced that it would borrow up to $53.7 billion from the Swiss National Bank, a liquidity facility intended to reassure markets after the collapse of [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]] in the United States.[1] The announcement came late in the day after Credit Suisse shares plunged, and it was accompanied by broader market stress: the Dow Jones industrial average fell by nearly 1 percent and European banking stocks tumbled, contributing to a roughly 3 percent decline in the pan-European Stoxx 600 index.[1]

The SNB's willingness to make a public statement of support was itself treated as significant by commentators. Badlands Media contributor [[absolutetruth1776|AbsoluteTruth1776]] argued that "the fact that the Swiss National Bank even made this statement should be paid attention to," reasoning that officials at the top of the financial system typically understand emerging distress before the public does, and predicting that Credit Suisse would be "the next big domino to fall in the current ongoing banking crisis."[1]

## Interpretation in the Badlands corpus

Within [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary, the SNB intervention was framed less as a technical liquidity operation and more as evidence that the fiat monetary system as a whole was approaching a stress point, with "more and more big banks inching closer to their very own precipice."[1]

## Record 2022 loss

In January 2023 the SNB projected the biggest loss in its 116-year history, an annual loss of about 132 billion francs ($143 billion), more than five times the previous record, according to preliminary results published on Monday, January 9.[2] The largest part of this — 131 billion francs — stemmed from collapsed valuations of its large pile of foreign-currency holdings, accrued through decade-long purchases intended to weaken the franc.[2] As a consequence the Swiss government would not receive a payout from the central bank for 2022.[2]

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presented the SNB result, alongside the loss reported by Sweden's [[riksbank|Riksbank]], as one of two "huge stories" demonstrating that cracks were showing in the [[global-debt-system|global central bank debt system]].[2] He attributed both losses to the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s aggressive rate increases and the resulting strength of the US dollar, arguing that foreign central banks could no longer manipulate the dollar and could only react to Fed policy.[2]
