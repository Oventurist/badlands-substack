# Rodney King

Rodney King was the black motorist whose 1991 beating by LAPD officers, captured on camcorder, became one of the defining media events of late twentieth-century America. He appears in the Badlands Media essay [[a-journey-of-ever-changing-truths|A Journey of Ever-changing Truths]] as the author's principal worked example of how a seemingly settled truth can dissolve under further examination.

## The 1991 beating

Author [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] recounts his own contemporaneous reaction to the footage: appallment at what appeared to be violent, cruel, and racist conduct by officers who had sworn an oath to protect and serve and who "appeared to be trying to kill a man." He allows that the officers may have been driven by job pressures and notes the acknowledged circumstances that King was intoxicated, speeding, and resisting arrest, but concludes that on the basis of their actions the officers "probably shouldn't be cops any longer."

## The provenance-of-the-footage argument

The essay's revisionist turn concerns not the beating but the recording. Carlson observes that he never asked who was filming, or why that person happened to have a camcorder ready to go in an era before cell-phone cameras. He describes the perfectly framed nighttime footage shot from an apartment balcony as "not impossible, but rather unlikely" to have arisen organically, and states elsewhere in the article that he now believes it to be "a staged camcorder recording."

## Aftermath

A year later, four officers tried for the beating were acquitted by a jury, triggering the Los Angeles riots. In the source's reading, the riots erased whatever public sympathy the black community had accumulated after the beating — an outcome the article treats as the operation's actual purpose. King's beating occupies the 1991 slot in the essay's proposed thirty-year cycle of engineered racial flashpoints, sitting between the Watts riots of 1965 and the [[george-floyd|George Floyd]] incident of 2020.
