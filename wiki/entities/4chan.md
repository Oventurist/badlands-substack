# 4chan

4chan is an anonymous English-language imageboard frequently cited in discussions of online radicalization. It was named as a defendant in the civil litigation arising from the 2022 Buffalo, New York, supermarket shooting.[1]

## Buffalo radicalization lawsuit

Plaintiffs alleged that 4chan, together with [[meta-platforms|Meta]], [[google|Alphabet]] and [[reddit|Reddit]], profited from racist, antisemitic and violent content in order to maximize user engagement, and that this content radicalized the then-18-year-old gunman [[payton-gendron|Payton Gendron]].[1] Erie County Supreme Court Justice [[paula-feroleto|Paula Feroleto]] denied a motion to dismiss the suit in March 2024, holding that the complaint stated viable causes of action at that stage of the litigation.[1] The platforms' attorneys had characterized the sites as message boards containing third-party content.[1]

## Origin of the Q drops

4chan is the platform on which the anonymous poster known as [[q-operation|Q]] began posting on October 28, 2017, in the board "Politically Incorrect," or /pol/.[2] In Chapter 1 of [[q-for-dummies|Q for Dummies]], [[paul-fleuret|Paul Fleuret]] describes the board as "the wild west of internet forums," with very little to no moderation and an "anything goes" mindset.[2]

Fleuret argues that 4chan's permissiveness, together with its encrypted and anonymous character, is precisely why the Q team selected it.[2] He adds that the board was full of [[anons|Anons]] — users with a particular talent for digging up information and a particular dislike for the cabal and Satanists — and concludes that in hindsight there was "no better place for Q to begin the mission."[2]

## Board security and the shutdown warning (Drop #112)

Q drop #112, of November 5, 2017, warned that incoming information could trigger an operation to shut 4chan down in an effort to silence the Q postings, and asked readers who owned the boards.[3] Chapter 17 of [[q-for-dummies|Q for Dummies]] answers that while the site's formal owner is not identified, [[ron-watkins|Ron Watkins]] — "Codemonkey" — was in control of the actual boards Q was posting to, and may be who Q was pointing toward.[3] The same drop states that the boards were safeguarded but neither fully secure nor immune from attack.[3]

The chapter restates the rationale for the platform choice: 4chan already hosted a base of people who constantly asked questions and had a talent for solving riddles and puzzles, the boards were uncensored, they were not easy for alphabet agencies to penetrate, and the site offered true anonymity to anyone posting.[3] Drop #115, in which Q confirmed an anon's identification of previously posted coordinates as a restaurant in the Philippines, is presented as a live demonstration of that collaborative research capacity.[3]

Drop #112 also opens with Q's instruction that archiving is important, a point the chapter reads as validated by the subsequent years of platform censorship.[3]

## Precursor anon threads

Chapter 4 of the [[q-for-dummies|Q for Dummies]] series discusses 4chan as the venue for the anon threads that preceded the [[q-operation|Q]] drops.[4] Responding to Q's repeated statements in drop #26 about the unmatched scope of the drops, the author says he can confirm the claim from personal experience, having been present for the FBIAnon, MegAnon, [[pizzagate|Pizzagate]] and HLIAnon threads and others on the board.[4] He judges that some of those threads turned out to be LARPs while FBIAnon and Pizzagate were not, and offers the opinion that some of them were the Q Team testing the waters before the operation proper began.[4]
