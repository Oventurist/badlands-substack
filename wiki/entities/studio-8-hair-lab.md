# Studio 8 Hair Lab

Studio 8 Hair Lab is a Michigan hair salon owned by [[christine-geiger|Christine Geiger]] that became the subject of a state civil rights enforcement action in 2023 after its owner publicly stated that the business would serve only people who identify as men or women.[1]

## The charge

On 15 November 2023 the [[michigan-department-of-civil-rights|Michigan Department of Civil Rights]] charged Geiger and the salon with discrimination, following an investigation into complaints filed over her comments.[1] John Johnson Jr., the department's executive director, told reporters in a briefing that "the truth is, based on a thorough investigation, that Studio 8 and its owner Christine Geiger, openly and repeatedly violated the Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act."[1]

The [[elliott-larsen-civil-rights-act|Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act]], implemented in 1977, prohibits discrimination on the basis of certain characteristics including religion; it was expanded in 2023 by the Michigan legislature and Democratic Governor [[gretchen-whitmer|Gretchen Whitmer]] to cover gender identity, enshrining a 2022 Michigan Supreme Court interpretation.[1]

## Geiger's statements

The complaints arose from social media posts. In July, Geiger wrote on Facebook: "If a human identifies as anything other than a man/woman please seek services at a local pet groomer. You are not welcome at this salon. Period."[1] She also said that salon workers might refer to customers as "hey you" if they requested a particular pronoun, and in another post stated that "LGB are more than welcome" while transgender people were not.[1]

## Commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the salon owner had simply made clear that her business was not a good fit for customers for whom pronouns are important, and that the enforcement action's real object was to ensure she would not be permitted to decline "the pronoun game."[1] The commentary situated the case within a broader pattern of [[lawfare|lawfare]], drawing an explicit comparison to Colorado baker [[jack-phillips|Jack Phillips]], who had spent more than eleven years in litigation and whose Supreme Court victory was followed by renewed proceedings in his home state.[1] In that framing, lawfare has two aims — punishing opponents and chiseling away at the contours of the law — with the process itself functioning as the punishment for defendants who struggle to find counsel willing to take their cases.[1]
