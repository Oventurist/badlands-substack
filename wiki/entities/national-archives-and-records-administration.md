# National Archives and Records Administration

The National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) is the U.S. federal agency responsible for preserving and providing access to government records, including the records of departing presidential administrations.[1]

NARA figured centrally in the July 2026 disclosures by [[chuck-grassley|Sen. Chuck Grassley]] and [[ron-johnson|Sen. Ron Johnson]] regarding former special counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]].[1] According to their joint report, the text messages from 44 members of [[us-congress|Congress]] reviewed by Smith's team had been sent to senior [[white-house|White House]] officials during the final weeks of [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s first term, and came from White House records subpoenaed by Smith's prosecutors and turned over by the National Archives in August 2023.[1] The Archives' role as custodian and producer of those records made it the conduit through which congressional communications entered the special counsel's holdings.[1]

## Biden pseudonym email records

NARA disclosed that it possessed approximately 5,400 records containing email pseudonyms used by [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] during his tenure as vice president.[2] The figure emerged in a letter to the [[southeastern-legal-foundation|Southeastern Legal Foundation]], which had filed a Freedom of Information Act request in June 2022 covering three pseudonymous accounts: robinware456@gmail.com, JRBWare@gmail.com, and Robert.L.Peters@pci.gov.[2] NARA wrote that it had "identified approximately 5,138 email messages, 25 electronic files and 200 pages of potentially responsive records that must be processed" in order to respond.[2] The episode, covered at [[biden-pseudonym-emails|Biden pseudonym emails]], underscored the Archives' role as gatekeeper of vice-presidential records sought through FOIA litigation.[2]

## Role in the Biden classified documents matter

NARA was the agency that first moved to recover records associated with [[joe-biden]] in late 2022, and its correspondence with Congress became a principal source of public information about the [[biden-classified-documents-scandal|classified documents scandal]].[3] When NARA contacted Biden's personal counsel on November 3, 2022 to arrange pickup of boxes from the [[penn-biden-center]] in Washington, D.C., it was informed that attorney [[patrick-moore]] had moved other boxes to his law firm in Boston.[3]

The agency notified the [[department-of-justice]] Office of Inspector General on November 4 that the documents had been relocated, retrieved the material on November 9, and secured it at the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library in Boston.[3] Acting Archivist [[debra-steidel-wall]], responding to a February 24 letter from Senators [[ron-johnson]] and [[chuck-grassley]], disclosed for the first time that NARA staff had retrieved nine boxes from Moore's Boston office.[3]

## 2024 unsealed emails in the classified documents case

Documents unsealed in April 2024 in [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s classified documents case were reported to show that the Biden [[white-house|White House]] colluded with NARA and the [[fbi|FBI]] to construct a case against the former president.[4] One email from NARA's general counsel to the national archivist discussed drafting a letter to Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] concerning "missing Trump records," and subsequent emails revealed coordination between NARA and the Biden White House counsel's office over the handling of those records.[4] Trump's defense argued the emails indicated coordination in targeting him since 2021, while Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] had sought to block discovery on the NARA–White House communications; the same trove disclosed the FBI code name [[plasmic-echo|Plasmic Echo]] for the records investigation.[4]

## January 2023 letter to former presidents and vice presidents

In January 2023 the National Archives formally asked former presidents and vice presidents to re-check their personal records for classified documents or other presidential records, in the wake of classified material discovered at the homes of former President Donald Trump, former Vice President Mike Pence and President Joe Biden over the preceding year.[5] The Archives sent a letter on Thursday, January 26, 2023 to representatives of former presidents and vice presidents from the last six administrations covered by the [[presidential-records-act|Presidential Records Act]] - from Ronald Reagan's White House to the present - asking them to ensure that material thought to be personal did not "inadvertently" contain presidential records required by law to be turned over.[5] Representatives for Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Barack Obama and the late George H. W. Bush had all previously said that every classified record had been turned over to the Archives upon leaving office.[5] Badlands contributor Just Human noted that the sweep put all of them "on the record denying having any classified records," and predicted those claims would prove erroneous.[5]

## Final JFK records release (March 2025)

On the evening of Tuesday, March 18, 2025, the National Archives began releasing the last trove of documents on the assassination of President John F. Kennedy, pursuant to an executive order signed by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in January requiring unredacted files in the case to be made public.[6] Trump estimated that 80,000 pages would be unsealed, telling reporters, "You got a lot of reading... I don't believe we're going to redact anything."[6]

Historians combing the material did not expect many ground-breaking revelations, and experts noted it was unclear how much of what the Archives released was genuinely new, since many of the documents had previously been made public in partially redacted form.[6] Prior releases had already covered hundreds of thousands of JFK documents, with some withheld on national security grounds.[6]

## Role in the Pence classified documents matter (2023)

In a letter dated January 22, 2023, [[mike-pence|Mike Pence]]'s lawyer Greg Jacob informed the National Archives that [[fbi|FBI]] agents had collected documents from Pence's Indiana home on January 19 at the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]]'s request, with Pence's agreement.[7] Pence's legal team later argued that because the documents went directly to the Justice Department rather than first to the Archives, the government had "bypassed the standard procedures" that normally govern the recovery of presidential and vice-presidential records.[7]

## America First Legal FOIA disclosure (October 2023)

In October 2023 [[america-first-legal|America First Legal]] announced that its Freedom of Information Act lawsuit against the National Archives had produced counts of correspondence between then-Vice President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s office and his family's business entities: 19,335 emails with [[rosemont-seneca-partners|Rosemont Seneca]], 4,243 with [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]], 1,751 with [[james-biden|Jim Biden]] and 3,738 with Jim's Lion Hall.[8] Badlands commentary stressed that these were official records held by the Archives and framed the volume as inconsistent with claims of a wall of separation between the vice president and his relatives' enrichment schemes.[8]

## Dismissal of the Archivist (February 2025)

On February 7, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] dismissed [[colleen-shogan|Colleen Shogan]], the Archivist of the United States, as part of a broader effort to upend Washington institutions.[9] The archivist is responsible for overseeing government records and heads the National Archives, an agency Trump had repeatedly criticized since it alerted the Justice Department to his handling of classified documents in early 2022.[9] Badlands commentator Chris Paul linked Shogan to financier [[david-rubenstein|David Rubenstein]], noting she had been Senior Vice President and Director of the [[rubenstein-center-for-white-house-history|Rubenstein Center for White House History]], and floated an explicitly speculative theory that privately controlled historical centers might house records moved out of NARA.[9]

## Historical custody controversies

The agency is headed by the [[archivist-of-the-united-states|Archivist of the United States]], a presidential appointee confirmed by the Senate who is responsible for safeguarding and making available all permanently valuable federal records, including the original Declaration of Independence, Constitution and Bill of Rights, and who carries extensive duties regarding the preservation of presidential papers and materials.[10] Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues that the office's second function — making records available to the public through presidential libraries, museums and [[freedom-of-information-act|FOIA]] responses — is the one past presidents have worked hardest to neutralize.[10]

The clearest example he cites is [[don-w-wilson|Don W. Wilson]], who on [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]]'s last full day in office signed an agreement granting the outgoing president "exclusive legal control of all Presidential information," including more than 4,000 computer tapes from the [[national-security-council|National Security Council]], and then resigned three weeks later to run the [[george-bush-presidential-library|Bush presidential library]].[10] Judge Charles R. Richey found that Wilson had violated federal record-keeping law by failing to establish guidelines for preserving electronic messages, and twice ordered the material preserved out of concern that Bush officials would destroy it.[10]

Lange also recounts the agency's later posture under [[david-ferriero|David Ferriero]], who described the Archives as custodian of a 10-billion-item trove and framed his role as oversight of every agency's records practices.[10] The article notes that NARA had fought the [[george-w-bush|George W. Bush]] administration over access to records following the September 11, 2001 attacks and was accused by The New York Times of needing "spine-stiffening" in its dealings with the White House, and that in 2003 [[sandy-berger|Sandy Berger]] removed and destroyed classified memos on terrorist plots taken from the Archives.[10] The Archives publicly refuted Trump's claims that [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] had transported over 30 million pages of documents to a former furniture store in Chicago, saying the agency itself was responsible for relocating unclassified documents to a secure federal facility there.[10]

## Referral preceding the Mar-a-Lago search

NARA's referral to the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] was the proximate trigger for the [[fbi|FBI]]'s August 8, 2022 [[mar-a-lago-raid|search of Mar-a-Lago]], a referral that [[alpha-warrior|Alpha Warrior]] characterised as "tinged with political maneuvering."[11] The redacted affidavit supporting the warrant recounted the agency's attempts through 2021 to recover presidential materials from [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], efforts met by what the government described as boxes shuffling in and out of the estate.[11] Trump's defenders responded that the [[presidential-records-act|Presidential Records Act]] of 1978 — the statute NARA administers, governing the ownership, custody and management of presidential records — placed his handling of the materials within the law.[11]
