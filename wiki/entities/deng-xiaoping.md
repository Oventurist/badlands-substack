# Deng Xiaoping

Deng Xiaoping was the Chinese leader who announced the "opening up" of China in 1978 and who, in Badlands contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s account, is best understood as a disciple of Premier [[zhou-enlai|Zhou Enlai]] rather than as a convert to Western free-market doctrine.[1] Ehret argues that China's leadership under Zhou and Deng held a far longer-term strategic perspective than their Western partners — above all [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] — assumed at the time.[1]

## Break with dialectical materialism

Deng is credited in the article with breaking from the radical Marxist/dialectical-materialist ideology prevalent among China's intelligentsia by redefining "labor" so that it was no longer confined to purely material constraints, elevating the concept to the domain of mind.[1] Deng is quoted as urging that "several thousand of our most qualified personnel within the scientific and technological establishment" be selected and given conditions allowing undivided attention to research, with allowances and subsidies for those in financial difficulty, and that the party cultivate "an atmosphere of respect for knowledge and respect for trained personnel," concluding: "All work. Be it mental or manual, is labor."[1]

The article treats this reformulation as decisive, since dialectical materialism's premise — that all qualitative reality is causally shaped by quantitative properties — reduces labor to physical measurables inside a closed world of finiteness and diminishing returns, leaving no room for soul, mind, ideas, freedom, justice or dignity.[1]

## Role in the Four Modernizations

Deng carried forward Zhou Enlai's [[four-modernizations|Four Modernizations]] program, which was elaborated at the July 6, 1978 [[state-council-of-china|State Council]] Forum on the "Principles to Guide the Four Modernizations" and informed by the international fact-finding delegations of economist [[gu-mu|Gu Mu]].[1] Under this framework China accepted revenue from foreign exports while slowly building the foundations for what Ehret calls a genuine renaissance — learning skills, leapfrogging technologies and acquiring means of production the West had pioneered.[1] Ehret contrasts Deng's nationalist reading of the Four Modernizations with the divergent, information-society reading advanced by [[zhao-ziyang|Zhao Ziyang]].[1]
