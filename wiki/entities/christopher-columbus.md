# Christopher Columbus

**Christopher Columbus** is the Genoese navigator conventionally credited with the European discovery of the Americas. In part five of [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]]'s [[the-werewolf-game|Werewolf Game]] series, the standard Columbus narrative is treated not as history but as a cover story concealing far older transatlantic contact.[1]

## Role in the ancient German argument

The essay's central claim — the [[ancient-german-language-thesis|ancient German language thesis]], that all the peoples of the world once spoke ancient German — is described as directly defying the mainstream narrative that the Americas remained undiscovered by Europeans until Columbus supposedly committed "a navigational blunder of jaw dropping proportions."[1] If Aztec and ancient German share a common vocabulary, the author reasons, the two hemispheres must have been in contact long before 1492.[1]

## Objections raised

The author argues that the Columbus story is very hard to believe once a few simple questions are asked.[1] By the time Columbus set sail, he notes, astronomy and sea navigation had already existed for centuries; India lies on the opposite side of the world from the Americas, yet Columbus is said to have landed in America by accident.[1] He asks how many navigational errors would have had to be made daily, and for how many months such errors would have had to compound, for that outcome to occur.[1]

He further asks whether Columbus failed to notice that the constellations overhead were not in the expected positions, and concludes that a wealthy, learned sailor would not have been capable of so large a mistake.[1] If Columbus was who history says he was, the author writes, it is far more likely that he sailed to the Americas intentionally — assuming any such voyage happened at all — and that the entire concept of a "discovery" was a psyop intended to cover up something else.[1]

Anticipating the objection that no motive for such a cover-up is apparent, the author concedes that the reason is not definitively known, and rests instead on the argument that the mainstream narrative has glaring holes in it, which he considers sufficient grounds to investigate further.[1] The broader picture he draws is of nations that have passed through periods of relative isolation but that once all spoke the same language.[1]
