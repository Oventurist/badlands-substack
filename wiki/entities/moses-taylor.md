# Moses Taylor

Moses Taylor was a New York merchant and banker who was installed on the board of directors of City Bank of New York — later [[citibank|Citibank]] — by [[john-jacob-astor|John Jacob Astor]] after Astor's rescue of the bank during the [[panic-of-1837|Panic of 1837]].[1] Described in the sources cited by Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] as "an importer of Cuban sugar," Taylor played the central role in transforming the bank into a national financial institution with international connections.[1]

## Sugar, slavery, and industrial investment

The commodity Taylor specialized in was sugar produced in Cuba by enslaved Africans smuggled into the Spanish colony in violation of international bans on the trans-Atlantic slave trade.[1] Taylor used sugar profits to help the bank survive a series of economic downturns and to purchase mines, railroads, and real estate.[1] Every company he invested in was then required to use City Bank for its financial transactions, giving the bank what Lange characterizes as a monopoly position over the American sugar trade and a compounding hold on industry.[1]

## Civil War and federal charter

During the Civil War, Taylor negotiated a federal charter for the institution — which became the National City Bank of New York — in exchange for loans financing the Union war effort.[1] Lange presents this as another instance of the same template: the bank financing the federal government in wartime, with war proving highly profitable for the financial elite.[1]

## Tammany Hall and legacy

After the war Taylor became a member of [[tammany-hall|Tammany Hall]], served on a commission that whitewashed the corrupt Tweed Ring which controlled New York City politics, and invested with William "Boss" Tweed in a series of business ventures.[1] When Taylor died in 1882 he was heavily invested in the company that would become Con Edison and was one of the wealthiest men of the nineteenth century, leaving an estate reportedly worth $70 million — roughly $1.6 billion in present-day dollars.[1] Lange argues that Taylor's rise was possible because Citibank was already entangled with, and protected by, the political class.[1] After Taylor's death control of the bank passed to the Stillman family, including [[james-stillman|James Stillman]].[1]
