# Republican National Committee

The Republican National Committee (RNC) is the national governing body of the Republican Party, responsible for party organization, fundraising and, increasingly in the post-2020 period, election-related litigation.[1]

In Arizona the RNC joined Attorney General candidate [[abe-hamadeh|Abe Hamadeh]] in a lawsuit filed shortly after the November 8, 2022 general election contesting the results. The court dismissed the action as prematurely filed, since under Arizona law results may not be contested until they have been certified — certification occurring on Monday, December 5, 2022 and opening a five-day filing window that Hamadeh subsequently used to refile.[1]


## 2026 election litigation
The RNC brought the challenge to [[mississippi|Mississippi]]'s mail ballot law that the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] rejected 5-4 in June 2026, leaving in place the state's practice of counting absentee ballots received up to five business days after Election Day if postmarked on or before it.[2]

The committee, joined by Douglas County Clerk [[sheri-davis|Sheri Davis]] and Representative [[jeff-crank|Jeff Crank]], also filed suit in Denver District Court against [[colorado|Colorado]] Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]], challenging state guidance under the [[uocava|Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act]] that lets some citizens abroad register in Colorado without ever having lived there.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] questioned the RNC's record as an advocate for voters and argued the suit's filing on the eve of the Colorado primary all but guarantees its failure.[2]

## 2024 leadership change

In February 2024, chairwoman [[ronna-mcdaniel|Ronna McDaniel]] announced she would formally resign after the March 5 Super Tuesday primaries, weeks after former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] asked her to step down and issued recommendations for changes within the committee.[3] Trump proposed that North Carolina GOP chair Michael Whatley take over as chairman, with his daughter-in-law Lara Trump and campaign senior adviser Chris LaCivita also receiving leadership positions.[3] McDaniel said she would step aside at the RNC's Spring Training on March 8 in Houston "to allow our nominee to select a Chair of their choosing," citing the party's tradition of undergoing change once a nominee has emerged.[3]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the RNC establishment as "brazen, elitist, self-interested and utterly wasteful," providing "zero value to Republican voters," and argued that the national party apparatus needed "an application of round-up to destroy the roots."[3] She suggested that electing [[scott-presler|Scott Presler]] — whom she called "mini-Ronna with better hair" — would finish the organization off.[3]

## "Ministry of Truth" framing

The RNC dubbed the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]]'s [[disinformation-governance-board|Disinformation Governance Board]] the "Ministry of Truth," a nod to George Orwell's novel *1984*, and characterized the board's working definition of disinformation as "any speech we don't like."[4] The label became a durable part of Republican messaging against the board, which was heavily criticized by the GOP and never held a meeting.[4]

## 2024 Nevada mail-ballot lawsuit

In June 2024 the RNC, together with [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s campaign and the [[nevada-republican-party|Nevada Republican Party]], filed a lawsuit against the state of Nevada over its practice of counting mail-in ballots received the day after an election that bear no postmark.[5] The committee explained that under state law mail ballots are required "to be postmarked on or before Election Day," and said it had discovered that Nevada election officials routinely counted non-postmarked mail ballots received after Election Day in violation of that law.[5] Chairman [[michael-whatley|Michael Whatley]] said the practice "creates a concerning and potentially fraudulent impact on Nevada's elections" and that the suit was intended "to ensure that Nevada law is followed and promote election integrity in the Silver State."[5]

## Michigan election integrity lawsuit

On March 28, 2024, the RNC filed an election integrity lawsuit against Michigan Secretary of State [[jocelyn-benson|Jocelyn Benson]], alleging that she had worked to covertly undercut the state's absentee voting safeguards.[6] At issue was a memorandum Benson sent to election officials ahead of the state's presidential primary encouraging them to apply a "presumption of validity" to signatures on absentee ballot requests, which the RNC contended violates the state constitution's signature matching requirement.[6] The suit also challenged a Benson rule granting officials broad leeway over signature discrepancies, which the committee described as permitting verification based on "mere speculation."[6]

## Departure of chief counsel Charlie Spies (May 2024)

In early May 2024 the committee parted ways with chief counsel [[charlie-spies|Charlie Spies]] roughly two months after he took the role; a source familiar with the move told NBC News he had been "pushed out."[7] Spies had been hired in March 2024 to lead the committee's legal efforts during the leadership overhaul that followed Trump's endorsement of [[michael-whatley|Michael Whatley]] as chairman, a change that effectively reshaped the RNC in Trump's image.[7] RNC spokesperson Danielle Alvarez attributed the exit to workload, saying Spies had approached Chief of Staff Chris LaCivita about time-commitment conflicts and that it was agreed he could not perform the role full time while maintaining obligations to his law firm.[7] A campaign official had earlier described Spies as "the best at what he does," someone who "knows the FEC loopholes better than anyone," but also a "gun for hire," and reporting noted he was not a fan of the former president.[7]

## Arizona proof-of-citizenship petition (August 2024)

In August 2024 the RNC asked the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] to reinstate an Arizona law requiring proof of citizenship for voter registration, which an appeals court had blocked.[8] The petition directly challenged a nearly twenty-year-old precedent — the [[purcell-principle|Purcell principle]] — that cautions against last-minute federal court actions capable of causing "voter confusion," and the committee requested a ruling by August 22 in the battleground state weeks before the presidential election.[8]

## Pre-election litigation blitz (September 2024)

By late September 2024 the RNC said it was involved in more than 120 lawsuits across 26 states, a legal blitz widely reported as laying the groundwork for election challenges in November.[9] The suits were concentrated in swing states such as Arizona, Michigan, Nevada and Pennsylvania, and critics — including voting rights groups and the Harris campaign — accused the party of scheming to sow distrust in elections so it could cry foul upon losing, while Republicans maintained the litigation was intended to restore faith in elections by ensuring people do not vote illegally.[9]

Badlands commentary credited the committee for preparing in advance and apparently learning from its lack of readiness in 2020 and 2022, while cautioning that election [[lawfare]] is usually decided on procedural technicalities rather than merits, per Robert Barnes's formulation that such claims are "Not Ripe in Spring, No Standing by Summer, Laches by Fall, and Moot by Winter."[9] See [[2024-republican-election-litigation-campaign]].[9]
