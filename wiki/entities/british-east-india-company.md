# British East India Company

The **British East India Company** (EIC) was an English, later British, chartered trading corporation that acquired territorial and military power in India and was formally dissolved by an act of Parliament in 1873.[1] Badlands Media describes it as the most powerful corporation of all time, noting that at the time of its dissolution its private army was twice the size of the British Army.[1]

## Alleged transformation into MI6

Badlands commentary advances the thesis that the EIC did not simply disappear but transformed itself into [[mi6|MI6]], observing that the company was dissolved in the same year, 1873, that the War Office created the body that would become known as British Intelligence.[1] The reasoning offered is that corporate espionage long predated the notion of a state intelligence agency, which is comparatively recent in historical terms, and that the EIC had already mastered the craft.[1]

## Proxy warfare and color revolutions

The company is also credited in this account with cultivating the modern practice of proxy war and color revolution during the 1730s, while competing with the French East India Company for influence in India.[1] The French are described as the first to conceive of "buying" the support of local warlords and turning their armies against the British, but British diplomats proved more cunning and better funded, out-manoeuvring their rivals and taking control of more than half of India.[1] Badlands invokes this history to explain the large English-speaking Indian population in London as a product of specific imperial circumstances, unlike migration patterns elsewhere in Europe.[1]

## The company's economists

Badlands contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] identifies [[john-stuart-mill|J. S. Mill]] and [[david-ricardo|David Ricardo]] as British East India Company economists whose "dismal science" produced the pseudo-scientific [[law-of-diminishing-returns|law of diminishing returns]].[2] That doctrine presumed a deterministic devaluation of land over time as rents increased under a "law of exploitation" of the unfit by the "more fit", and was refuted by [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]]'s economic advisor [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]] in *Unity of Law* (1872).[2]

Ehret argues that the closed-system theories advanced by all British Imperial economists were the basis both of Marx and Engels' theory of class struggle — which ignored the anti-imperial American economic school — and of the [[club-of-rome|Club of Rome]]'s 1968 neo-Malthusian [[limits-to-growth|limits-to-growth]] revival, later absorbed into the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] via the 1973 [[davos-manifesto|Davos Manifesto]].[2] He also attributes the turbulence of the US republic between 1787 and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]]'s 1963 murder in large measure to the subversive hand of London-centered deep state operations.[2]

## Role in the opium trade

During the eighteenth century, with English domestic manufacturing falling behind and a trade deficit with China draining British silver reserves, [[king-george-iii|King George III]] mandated the East India Company to begin shipping large amounts of opium from British-controlled Bengal, India, into China.[3] The policy was intended both to reverse the trade deficit and, in the account given in [[dope-inc-book|*Dope, Inc.*]], to create a country of drug addicts who would be more easily controlled.[3] In 1715 the company had opened its first Far East office in the Chinese port city of Canton.[3]

The traffic expanded rapidly: 5,000 chests of opium per year in 1801 rose to more than 30,000 chests by 1836, and by the 1830s the trade balance with China had reversed to eight to one in Britain's favor — the imbalance that precipitated the [[opium-wars|Opium Wars]].[3]
