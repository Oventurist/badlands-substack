# Robert Conrad Jr.

Robert Conrad Jr. is the director of the Administrative Office of the United States Courts, the administrative arm of the federal judiciary. In December 2025 he intervened publicly on behalf of U.S. District Court Judge [[james-boasberg|James Boasberg]] amid congressional scrutiny of the [[arctic-frost|Arctic Frost]] investigation.[1]

## Defense of Boasberg in the Arctic Frost controversy

Conrad stepped in to defend Boasberg over the latter's signing of gag orders that concealed subpoenas targeting members of Congress during the investigation conducted by the Federal Bureau of Investigation and Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]].[1] Conrad claimed that the chief judge for the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia often signed gag order requests essentially without independent scrutiny when those requests came directly from the Department of Justice.[1]

In a letter addressed to Senator [[chuck-grassley|Chuck Grassley]] of Iowa, Conrad revealed that DOJ subpoena requests typically lack identifying details about the subject of the subpoena, and argued on that basis that Boasberg would not have known the identities of the targets.[1] It remained unclear whether the same circumstances applied to U.S. District Court Judge [[beryl-howell|Beryl Howell]], who also approved a number of Jack Smith's Arctic Frost subpoenas targeting Republican lawmakers.[1]

Conrad said he could not address specific subpoenas and gag orders because the underlying materials remained sealed, but offered insight into the general practices that prevailed during Arctic Frost.[1] His letter responded to demands from Grassley, Senator Ron Johnson of Wisconsin, and Representative Jim Jordan of Ohio, who had sought an explanation from Boasberg regarding the one-year gag orders that prevented telephone companies from informing Republican lawmakers in 2023 that their records had been subpoenaed.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands Media commentary framed the episode as an inter-branch clash, arguing that Congress objected loudly only because the surveillance touched its own members, and that Boasberg — standing on the independence of the judiciary — declined to submit to congressional questioning.[1]
