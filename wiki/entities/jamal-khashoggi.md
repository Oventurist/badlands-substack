# Jamal Khashoggi

**Jamal Khashoggi** (also rendered Jamaal Khashoggi) was a Saudi journalist whose 2018 killing became one of the defining international scandals attached to Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] (MBS).[1] The mainstream account holds that MBS ordered the murder; the [[cia|CIA]] publicly blamed MBS and the Saudi kingdom for the killing.[1] Khashoggi was a nephew of the arms dealer Adnan Khashoggi, and Badlands commentary has argued he was "not even a fraction of the gangster that his Uncle Adnan was," which is offered as the reason he went into journalism.[1]

## Alternative theories

A theory popularized by researcher John Cullen holds that Khashoggi was killed in vengeance for allegedly orchestrating a failed assassination attempt on MBS during the October 2017 Las Vegas shooting.[1] [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] has publicly advocated a competing thesis for years: that MBS did not kill Khashoggi and was instead set up by the CIA and [[mossad|Mossad]] in order to ostracize him from the international community.[1] His argument rests on the claim that MBS represents a new era in the Middle East and would not risk his reputation and reform agenda to eliminate a figure less significant than mainstream media portrayed; capturing Khashoggi and coercing a public confession, he argues, would have been the more dynamic move than "guaranteeing the truth dies with him."[1]

## Epstein email

The 2026 release of the [[epstein-library|Epstein Library]] revived the dispute when an October 12, 2018 email between [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] and Anas Alrasheed surfaced, in which Epstein asserted that MBS had been set up and speculated that [[mohamed-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Mohammed bin Zayed]] of Abu Dhabi was responsible for the betrayal.[1] GhostofBasedPatrickHenry rejected the MBZ hypothesis on the grounds that MBZ had advocated for MBS's elevation to Crown Prince and that MBZ, his ambassador Youssef Al Otaiba and the UAE bore the brunt of ridicule when the CIA blamed MBS for the killing — meaning the Emirates had as much reputationally to lose as the Saudis.[1] He nonetheless welcomed the fact that the leak created "social permission" to indulge alternative theories, remarking on the strangeness that a private Epstein email was required to grant it.[1]

## Badlands framing of the narrative

Reporting on the October 2024 EU–GCC summit in Brussels noted that Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] showed little evidence of his former standing as a political pariah following Khashoggi's assassination in 2018, and that EU diplomats claimed to have heard "nothing about this" when asked whether members had raised concerns.[2] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that the Khashoggi narrative had been a psyop designed to alienate MBS and to undermine his capacity to act as a friend and ally of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[2]

### Timing relative to the first FII summit
Badlands commentary noted that the inaugural Future Investment Initiative summit in October 2018 was shadowed by the Khashoggi affair, which had been launched weeks earlier and which the commentary characterized as a psychological operation directed against Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman.[4] In that reading, the resulting controversy gave many would-be attendees pause over whether the young Saudi leader truly represented a new chapter in Arab history — an outcome described as likely part of the intent of the operation.[4]

## Legacy in US-Saudi Relations (2025)

Khashoggi's murder continued to define the reference point for shifts in US-Saudi relations. In May 2025, coverage of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s Riyadh visit contrasted the warm reception given to Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] with the crown prince's difficulty four years earlier in securing a meeting with then-President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], who had vowed to make Saudi Arabia a "pariah" over its role in the journalist's killing.[5] At the [[saudi-us-investment-forum|Saudi-US Investment Forum]], Trump made no mention of the Kingdom's human rights record, which had previously been a sticking point in the relationship.[5]

## Badlands reassessment of responsibility (2024)

Coverage of the April 2024 disclosure that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had spoken with Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] revived the Khashoggi killing as a point of attack, with New York Times reporters [[maggie-haberman|Maggie Haberman]] and [[jonathan-swan|Jonathan Swan]] noting that Trump had defended the crown prince against the murder allegations.[6] Badlands Media contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] responded that those allegations came solely from the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]], without evidence, trial or official statement — only anonymous comments — and recalled that candidate [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] had vowed in 2020 to treat the crown prince as a "pariah," which the commentator said matched precisely what the CIA and the [[state-department|State Department]] had demanded of world leaders after the scandal.[6]

The commentary argued outright that the Saudis did not kill Khashoggi, describing him as a prolific scumbag but a relative nobody next to the crown prince, who by the time of the killing had already consolidated control of the Saudi government and had little to gain from fulfilling the stereotype of a violent and vindictive Arab while rebranding the region.[6] Because the CIA's insistence on Saudi guilt was, in the writer's view, propaganda, and because Khashoggi's death benefited the agency's objectives more than it benefited the crown prince, the commentary concluded that the CIA itself killed Khashoggi.[6]

## Legacy in the 2025 MBS Washington visit

The 2018 killing of Khashoggi, a Washington Post opinion columnist and Virginia resident who was dismembered in the Saudi Consulate in Turkey in a killing US intelligence officials concluded Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] had ordered, resurfaced during the crown prince's November 2025 visit to Washington.[7] Rep. [[eugene-vindman|Eugene Vindman]] called on President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to release the transcript of a phone call with the crown prince that took place in the aftermath of the assassination, saying the call "was about the murder of an American resident — a Virginia resident — and a Washington Post reporter."[7] The Washington Post noted that many tech and Wall Street executives who had shunned the kingdom after the killing attended the November 18 [[white-house|White House]] dinner in the crown prince's honor.[7]


## Treatment in "Profiting From War"

Joe Lange's "Profiting From War" identifies Jamal Khashoggi as the son of the arms dealer and [[bank-of-credit-and-commerce-international|BCCI]] investor [[adnan-khashoggi|Adnan Khashoggi]], who helped [[central-intelligence-agency|the CIA]] arm Iran and the Contras.[8] The article asserts that Khashoggi was himself a CIA operative wanted in Saudi Arabia on criminal charges he was avoiding by staying abroad, and that he "made the mistake of going to a Saudi Arabian consulate."[8] It frames his killing as an act of war against an enemy rather than the murder of a journalist, and argues that CIA-controlled media used his death to attack Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] and to drive a wedge between him and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[8]

Citing [[rolling-stone|Rolling Stone]], the article records that Trump, asked repeatedly whether he believed bin Salman ordered the killing, answered "He says he didn't do it," while the CIA had concluded that he did; intelligence reporting indicated fifteen Saudi agents flew to Istanbul in October 2018, murdered Khashoggi inside the consulate, dismembered his body and removed it in plastic bags.[8] Members of both parties advanced measures to hold bin Salman accountable, including a resolution labeling him complicit.[8] Secretary of State [[mike-pompeo|Mike Pompeo]] said there was no "direct reporting" of guilt and Defense Secretary [[james-mattis|James Mattis]] said there was no "smoking gun."[8] In 2019 bipartisan majorities in the House and Senate passed legislation to block or limit weapons sales to Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Jordan after the administration used an emergency declaration to conclude an $8.1 billion arms deal without congressional approval, but the bills never became law because Trump vetoed them and lawmakers could not override.[8]
