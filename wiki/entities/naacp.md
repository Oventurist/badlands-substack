# NAACP

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) is one of the best-known civil-rights organizations in the United States.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Its Colorado/Montana/Wyoming Area Conference was the lead plaintiff in [[naacp-v-useip|*NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.*]], filed in the [[us-district-court-of-colorado|United States District Court for the District of Colorado]] on March 9, 2022.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

The conference, joined by the [[league-of-women-voters-colorado|League of Women Voters of Colorado]] and [[mi-familia-vota|Mi Familia Vota]], asked the court to find [[useip|USEIP]] and individually named activists liable for voter suppression, intimidation, coercion, and threats under Section 11(b) of the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]] and the [[ku-klux-klan-act-of-1871|Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The claims failed: Judge [[charlotte-sweeney|Charlotte Sweeney]] ended the trial early on partial findings, holding that the plaintiffs introduced no evidence that could remotely be perceived as intimidating or threatening, and awarded costs to the defendants.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

Defendants characterized the litigation as an exercise in [[lawfare]] that attached the stigma of the Ku Klux Klan to lawful political activity, describing the court's findings as a damning rebuke of both the civil-rights organizations and their lawyers.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Fulton County Rule 41(g) motion (2026)

In February 2026 the NAACP and allied NGOs filed an emergency motion in the US District Court for the Northern District of Georgia seeking court-imposed restrictions on how the [[fbi|FBI]] and [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] may use election materials seized from [[fulton-county|Fulton County]] in January.[2] Brought under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(g), the filing did not directly challenge the validity of the search warrant; instead the plaintiffs argued that Fulton County voters hold protectable privacy and constitutional interests in their personal voter data — Social Security numbers, dates of birth, driver's license numbers and ballot-related information — and therefore qualify as "persons aggrieved" under the rule.[2]

The motion asked the court to confine federal use of the seized materials to the criminal investigation described in the warrant, expressly prohibiting use for national voter roll programs, immigration enforcement, unrelated civil enforcement, data sharing with other agencies, compilation of voter databases, and public disclosure.[2] The plaintiffs further sought access controls limiting review to personnel assigned to the investigation, a detailed inventory of seized materials, disclosure of who had accessed the records, and a protective order governing storage and handling.[2] The organizations asserted both individual standing on behalf of Fulton County voters and associational standing, arguing that the seizure undermines voter trust and diverts organizational resources.[2]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the filing as surreal, describing it as voter-registration NGOs begging a federal court to stop the federal government from checking their work using federal records in federal elections, and argued that opposing the roughly 83 percent of Americans who support voter verification makes sense only if that position is an existential threat to get-out-the-vote organizations.[2]

## Federal case against Ashe in America

In the April 7, 2026 Badlands Brief, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] referred to a federal case brought against her by the NAACP, disclosing that she fired her attorneys and proceeded pro se after concluding that her lead attorney's objectives and incentives were not aligned with her own.[3] The decision followed a conversation in which the attorney described a case of his in which a criminal conviction was overturned but the innocent prisoner received nothing beyond release, which she cited as emblematic of a justice system that offers no restitution for government-caused harm.[3]

## Illinois migrant remarks controversy (2023)

In December 2023, Illinois NAACP president [[teresa-haley|Teresa Haley]] apologized "to anyone who may have been hurt or offended" after video recorded at a statewide meeting captured her calling migrants rapists and "like savages," remarks she made while objecting to migrants receiving shelter and clothing when Black people in need had long gone without such assistance.[4] Illinois branch presidents said they "unanimously supported" her leadership.[4] In her written apology Haley restated the organization's mission of achieving "equity, political rights, and social inclusion by advancing policies and practices that expand human and civil rights, eliminate discrimination, and accelerate the well-being, education, and economic security of Black people and all persons of color."[4]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described the NAACP as "one of the key narrative power projection vectors of the cultural arm of the Deep State," arguing that it operates behind "a permanent shield of victimhood," and grouped the episode with the simultaneous troubles at [[harvard-university|Harvard]] as part of a broader Marxist circular firing squad visible only to observers watching multiple institutional theaters at once.[4]
