# Francis Marion

Francis Marion was an American Revolutionary War partisan commander, popularly remembered as the "Swamp Fox," and the subject of Part 1 of [[patriot-chronicles|The Patriot Chronicles]] by [[ghost-of-patrick-henry|Ghost of Patrick Henry]].[1]

## Treatment in The Patriot Chronicles

The introduction to the series closed with a teaser headed "Beware of the Swamp…" that previewed the Marion installment without naming him directly.[1] It described how, long before the emergence of the novel [[deep-state|Deep State]], the swamp belonged to "another apex predator" — a phantom leading a host of hardened war veterans who came to personify the teachings of Sun Tzu and forever changed the Art of War.[1] Armed with nothing more than muskets and their wits, these patriots were said to have stopped the most powerful global empire of all time in its tracks at the most critical moment in the [[american-revolution|American Revolution]], and in doing so the phantom became a legend.[1] The author invited readers who assumed they already knew the story to reconsider, before directing them to Part 1.[1]

The introduction was illustrated with William Washington's painting "Marion and His Men in the Swamp," reinforcing the guerrilla-in-the-wetlands imagery around which the installment was built.[1]

## Early life and character

Marion was born in 1732 — the same year as [[george-washington|George Washington]] — with one leg shorter than the other, a birth defect that in the wilderness of 18th-century South Carolina could easily have been a death sentence, and little was expected of him.[2] He was nevertheless an ardent man who learned to move with quickness and, despite a small frame, thrived as both warrior and farmer.[2] His first great test came at fifteen, when he sought a life at sea as a deckhand on a small merchant vessel in the Caribbean.[2] On his first voyage a whale attacked and sank the ship so quickly that the crew escaped in a rowboat without food or water; after three days adrift they ate the raw flesh of the cabin dog that had swum to them from the wreckage, preserving and drinking its blood in lieu of water, which bought them another week.[2] The captain and first mate lost their minds and dove into the sea, the remaining men died of exposure, and a flock of seagulls feeding on the corpses drew a passing vessel; the boy was pulled from the pile of decay, having used the bodies to shield himself from the sun and the birds.[2] The article treats the episode as proof that Marion was a survivor who found resolve when all hope seemed lost.[2]

A French Huguenot by descent, Marion served five years as a commissioned officer in the [[continental-army|Continental Army]], rising by hard drilling of the men and diligent construction of coastal fortifications in anticipation of a British return to the southern theater.[2]

## Break with the Continental Army, 1780

In the spring of 1780, with rumor spreading that [[henry-clinton|Sir Henry Clinton]] had landed an enormous army thirty miles south of Charleston, Marion found himself at a downtown cocktail party surrounded by flamboyant officers in powdered wigs and tights who seemed wholly unconcerned by the approaching danger.[2] When the host locked the doors, Marion concluded that the Continental Army was not the outfit he had believed it to be, excused himself upstairs, found an unlocked window and jumped, severely breaking his ankle — a fitting metaphor, the article suggests, for the pivotal choice he had just made.[2] With the help of his longtime friend and confidant Buddy, a former slave he had freed, he hobbled out of town just as the British took control, and the two spent months evading capture in the backcountry while seeking out hardened militants who shared their firebrand worldview.[2] Buddy, whose given name was Oscar, appears as Marion's faithful valet in John Blake White's painting of the general sharing a meal with a British officer.[2]

## A merry band of misfits

On 25 July 1780 roughly twenty motley militia volunteers rode into General [[horatio-gates|Horatio Gates]]'s camp near Buffalo Ford, North Carolina — some white, some black, with a Catawba Indian or two and a few soldiers barely in their teens — their attire so "burlesque" that the Continentals struggled not to laugh.[2] At their head was a small man with fire in his eyes, in a tattered Continental uniform, walking with a pronounced limp yet moving with quickness.[2]

Marion's recruits were found in pubs, taverns, churches and rice fields: mostly Scotch-Irish veterans of the French and Indian War who had fought the Cherokee and knew irregular warfare firsthand, along with half-breed Catawba Indians, freemen, highborn Scotsmen, lowborn Englishmen, Irishmen, Huguenots, Poles and all manner of outcasts and go-betweens.[2] The article frames the central question of the "social experiment" — a phrase it attributes to intellectuals such as [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] — as whether a French Huguenot could rouse and lead an army of lowborn Scotch-Irish, hereditary enemies of the English Crown, against the greatest empire of all time, and whether these longtime rivals could become greater than the sum of their parts.[2] As a seasoned drill instructor Marion trained them to ride and shoot from horseback and to fight with the saber, since most carried only a few rounds and the Kentucky long rifle could not take a bayonet; sabers being scarce, he foraged discarded mill-saws and had blacksmiths reforge them in the forest.[2]

## The Swamp Fox

Left alone deep behind enemy lines after the [[siege-of-charleston|fall of Charleston]] and the [[battle-of-camden|Battle of Camden]] had removed every organized American force from South Carolina, Marion operated from [[snows-island|Snow's Island]] and similar refuges, functioning as what the article calls the American Robin Hood and the apex predator of the marsh.[2] Despite total British control of the region, the empire could neither move supplies nor reliably communicate between commanders, because of one man.[2] For this he is recognized in the article as the father of modern [[guerrilla-warfare|guerrilla warfare]] and the progenitor of U.S. Army Special Forces, specifically the 75th Ranger Regiment and the Green Berets.[2]

His first encounter with [[banastre-tarleton|Banastre Tarleton]] was a cat-and-mouse affair: Marion laid an ambush at Nelson's Ferry, Tarleton pulled back and spread false rumors of retreat to Camden, then hid four hundred men and cannon at the plantation of the deceased Brigadier General Richard Richardson, pretending to burn the house to bait a night attack.[2] Marion nearly took the bait but was warned by Richardson's son, a thirty-nine-year-old militia major and former Charleston prisoner of war who risked his life slipping past Tarleton, and withdrew into the supposedly impenetrable swamp at Jack's Creek.[2] After seven hours of fruitless pursuit at full gallop Tarleton gave up with the exclamation that made the legend: "Come my boys! Let us go back, and we will soon find the Gamecock" — [[thomas-sumter|Thomas Sumter]] — " As for this damned Swamp Fox, the devil himself could not catch him!"[2]

## Leadership, psywar and clemency

What distinguished Marion, the article argues, was less his battlefield tactics than his conduct off the battlefield, toward his own men and the enemy alike.[2] He kept a constant pulse on morale and planned actions around it — attacking when spirits were high, regrouping and resupplying when they were low, and sending men home to check on their families when anxiety consumed them — and though he lacked charisma or charm he understood people as few commanders ever have.[2] Operating without logistical support from any government or military authority, he depended entirely on the hearts and minds of the men around him.[2]

By dispersing his small force across an enormous area he induced [[charles-cornwallis|Cornwallis]] to vastly overestimate his strength, a psychological effect deepened when Marion seized a wagon of the general's personal writings and effects.[2] He ordered his men to use their first shot from cover against British officers riding in file, telling the British in parlay that so long as they burned homes and harmed women he would keep assassinating their leadership.[2] Employing the [[fabian-strategy|Fabian strategy]], he contained Cornwallis in South Carolina long enough for the French fleet to reinforce Washington in Virginia.[2] His greatest guerrilla victory came at [[battle-of-parkers-ferry|Parker's Ferry]] on 30 August 1781, and at [[battle-of-eutaw-springs|Eutaw Springs]] that September his militia front line fired seventeen volleys per man.[2]

A famous anecdote — recorded in Samuel Weaver's army pension application and painted by John Blake White, the only artist to depict Marion who actually knew him — has a young British officer brought blindfolded into the swamp camp to parlay, finding men lounging in the shade, reading to one another, playing music on makeshift instruments and playing sports while saddled horses grazed.[2] Invited to dine on roasted potatoes served on plates of tree bark, with Marion joking that "hunger is the best sauce," the aristocratic officer was so overwhelmed that on returning he resigned his commission and departed for home, "declaring his conviction that men who could with such content endure the privations of such a life, were not to be subdued."[2]

Marion's notorious good treatment of prisoners became a strategic asset at the close of the war: Tory militia leaders who saw the writing on the wall surrendered to him specifically because they knew he would not hang them, and universally asked that their wives and children — many in hiding — be protected.[2] Throughout the conflict he continued to see the humanity in the Tories, recognizing that these neighbors would remain neighbors after independence, and though some of his own men defected it was always Marion whom Tory defectors sought out as a true leader of men.[2] His nephew and adopted son [[gabriel-marion|Gabriel Marion]] fought at his side from the age of fourteen.[2]

## Modern application

The Patriot Chronicles presents Marion as "the founding father" of the series and draws explicit contemporary lessons: that Americans face the same two-front war as colonial South Carolina — a civil war between neighbors and an existential struggle against a subjugating oligarchy — and must make quick peace with one rival in order to vanquish the greater enemy.[2] Readers are urged to become partisan guerrillas in the swamps of cyberspace, waging [[fifth-generation-warfare|5GW]] counterinsurgency with counter-narratives, ridicule and humor.[2] The article's principal sources are John Oller's *The Swamp Fox*, W. D. James's *Swamp Fox*, W. G. Simms's *Life of Francis Marion*, and the first-person biography by [[mason-locke-weems|Mason Locke Weems]], without which, the author writes, the Swamp Fox might have gone unnoted in history.[2]
