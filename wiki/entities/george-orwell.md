# George Orwell

George Orwell was the British author of the novel *1984*, published in 1949, which [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] describes as shockingly accurate in its prediction of the future.[1] In the February 5, 2024 Badlands Media essay "Control Through Language," Carlson recounts hearing another Badlands Media contributor read from the novel on air, which inspired him to re-read it; he had first read it roughly five years earlier, at a time when he already considered himself "awake."[1]

## Claimed insider knowledge

Carlson argues that Orwell's predictive accuracy could not have been the product of imagination alone, and that Orwell must have been privy to information and plans unavailable to the rest of humanity.[1] He places Orwell in the same social circles as fellow British writers [[h-g-wells|H.G. Wells]], [[aldous-huxley|Aldous Huxley]], Huxley's brother [[julian-huxley|Julian Huxley]], and the birth-control campaigner [[margaret-sanger|Margaret Sanger]] during the first two decades of the twentieth century, and states that at least some — if not all — of these figures supported [[eugenics|eugenics]].[1]

From this Carlson infers that the [[cabal|Cabal]]'s long-term plan was shared with these authors, and that their fiction amounted to a disclosure of that plan.[1] He suggests Orwell's timeline ran a few decades early because something or someone obstructed the plan, naming Ronald Reagan as "a stick in the bicycle wheel" of the [[deep-state-cabal|Deep State Cabal]] who set it back decades.[1]

## The novel's themes in Carlson's reading

Carlson summarizes *1984*'s protagonist Winston as a worker for the government media whose duties include rewriting history to match each new official narrative, destroying documents and books so that earlier narratives are "memory-holed" as though they never occurred.[1] He highlights a cafeteria scene in which Winston speaks with a colleague responsible for compiling a new dictionary containing progressively fewer words, on the theory that eliminating words limits thought — the basis for Carlson's broader argument about [[control-through-language|control through language]].[1]
