# Abraham Lincoln

**Abraham Lincoln** was the sixteenth president of the United States. In [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s essay "Avoiding WWIII," Lincoln appears as one of the "wiser anti-slavery voices" of American history and as the president who tied national survival to large-scale infrastructure development.[1]

## Anti-slavery statesmanship

Ehret lists Lincoln alongside [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], John Jay, [[john-quincy-adams|John Quincy Adams]], Charles Sumner, William Seward and [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]] as figures repeatedly subverted by an anglophile [[deep-state|Deep State]] parasite class that ran both Wall Street in the north and the southern slave power.[1] He is also counted among the five presidents who made serious efforts to revive America's nationalist system and died in office — [[william-henry-harrison|Harrison]] in 1841, [[zachary-taylor|Taylor]] in 1850, Lincoln in 1865, Garfield in 1880 and McKinley in 1901.[1]

## Rail and the Civil War

Despite the loss of the national bank in 1836, Ehret writes, a general orientation toward scientific and technological optimism still animated the American character.[1] By 1860 some 30,000 miles of rail had been laid, and a new quantum leap followed Lincoln's decision to devote considerable resources during the height of the Civil War to the Trans Continental Railway, built between 1863 and 1869.[1] With that project completed and the Civil War resolved — largely, in Ehret's telling, through the intervention of the Russians — rail growth and industrialization exploded, reaching over 93,000 miles by 1880 and 163,000 miles by 1890.[1]

During the Civil War the British provided weapons, warships, logistical support, intelligence hubs in Canada and funding to the rebels, nearly forcing Lincoln to fight a war on two fronts: one against the south and one against the British Empire.[1] Frederick Douglass, unlike radical abolitionists who advocated dissolving the union, recognized the higher historic fight at hand.[1]

## Carey and the American System

Badlands contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] notes that Lincoln's economic advisor was [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]] (1793–1879), a champion of American industrial growth and a leading anti-Malthusian.[2] Carey refuted the "dismal science" of the [[british-east-india-company|British East India Company]] economists [[john-stuart-mill|J. S. Mill]] and [[david-ricardo|David Ricardo]] and their [[law-of-diminishing-returns|law of diminishing returns]], demonstrating in *Unity of Law* (1872) that technological progress made unproductive lands more productive and that the power to support life increased in a non-zero-sum system of mutual cooperation.[2] Ehret places this principle of self-perfectibility in science, technology and statecraft in a line running from [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] and the founding documents of 1776 and 1787 through Lincoln's presidency to [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]].[2]

## Contested legacy

Ehret observes that Lincoln has become the cause of serious arguments among patriots: some citizens love him as an angelic savior of America while others hate him as a wicked tyrant, even celebrating his 1865 murder to this day, and he contends that both extremes of this narrative war have been duped.[3] He argues that 150 years of revisionist historians have obscured the real Lincoln and the true nature of the Civil War as a British-run operation to undo the revolution of 1776.[3]

## Whig and American System commitments

Lincoln was always an opponent of slavery and always situated himself in the traditions of the American System of [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]], describing in 1832 a policy he enacted thirty years later: "My politics are short and sweet, like the old woman's dance. I am in favor of a national bank. I am in favor of the internal improvement system, and a high protective tariff."[3] In 1859 he described himself as "an old Henry Clay tariff whig" who had made more speeches on that subject than on any other and had not since changed his views.[3]

His commitment to the Hamiltonian system of national banking was showcased in his 1840 campaign speeches promoting [[william-henry-harrison|Harrison]]'s Whig presidency and the restoration of the bank cancelled by [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] four years earlier.[3] American System Now president Nancy Spannaus writes that the 1840 campaign was successful in that Harrison was elected and a bill establishing a new National Bank was passed by the incoming Congress, but that the move was aborted when the President died of a mysterious illness and his vice-president, John Tyler, took the Jacksonian position of vetoing the legislation.[3] From his period in Congress, where he became a leading ally of [[john-quincy-adams|John Quincy Adams]] and played a leading role in opposition to the unjust U.S.–Mexican War, Lincoln committed himself to ending not only systems of slavery but all hereditary power structures internationally, which he understood to be inextricably connected.[3] In an 1858 debate with [[stephen-douglas|Judge Douglas]] he called the contest "the eternal struggle between these two principles – right and wrong – throughout the world," between "the common right of humanity" and "the divine right of kings."[3]

## Wartime finance

Beyond the dangers of secession, Lincoln had to contend with Wall Street financiers and anglophile families who worked tirelessly to sabotage his ability to acquire funds to execute the war, and loans from Wall Street and international banking houses were granted only at excessive interest rates of 20 to 25 percent.[3] In January 1862 the New York Associated Banks presented an ultimatum to the Treasury demanding massive direct taxation, a monopoly on marketing U.S. debt, suspension of the sub-treasury laws, and withdrawal of all government-issued paper currency.[3] Lincoln instead revived the [[american-system-of-political-economy|American System]] through a three-step operation: the Banking and Currency Acts of 1862 and 1863, the [[national-bank-act-of-1863|Bank Act of 1863]], and the issuance of [[greenbacks|greenbacks]] under the [[legal-tender-act-of-1862|Legal Tender Act of 1862]], supplemented by [[five-twenty-bonds|5:20 bonds]] organized by [[jay-cooke|Jay Cooke]].[3] By the beginning of 1865, $450 million in greenbacks made up over half of all currency in circulation.[3]

## Assassination and subversion

Ehret attributes Lincoln's murder to Confederate intelligence operations run through British Canada, and writes that the subversion of his American System began quickly thereafter.[3] Rather than the full reconstruction of the defeated South that Lincoln had planned, a new war was waged against the greenbacks by the [[city-of-london|City of London]] and Wall Street, culminating in the [[specie-resumption-act|Specie Resumption Act]]; his vice-president [[andrew-johnson|Andrew Johnson]] declared in his 1865 inaugural address that "free trade with all the markets of the world is the true theory of government."[3]

## Bering Strait telegraph and Russian solidarity

In December 1864 Lincoln delivered a State of the Nation address to Congress outlining federal support for extending telegraph lines through the Bering Strait, noting that the proposed overland telegraph between America and Europe by way of Bering's Straits and Asiatic Russia "has been undertaken, under very favorable circumstance," and that most South American states had signalled readiness to cooperate in constructing lines tributary to that "world-encircling communication."[5] Russian Alaska was sold to the United States only three years later, explicitly around the notion of facilitating this program.[5]

Having just endured the [[american-civil-war|Civil War]], the United States had much reason to trust [[alexander-ii|Czar Alexander II]]'s Russia, which had intervened to protect the Union against aggressive intervention by English and French imperial powers supporting secession.[5] In 1863 Alexander II sent Russian battleships to New York and San Francisco as a message of solidarity with Lincoln and a threat to the imperial forces of Europe.[5] The article states this fact has been erased from American history books but remains better understood in Russia, where a statue in Moscow depicts Alexander II and Lincoln — described as two martyred patriots and great emancipators — holding hands.[5]

Lincoln's friend [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]], the first governor of Colorado, later carried this vision forward in his 1890 book [[cosmopolitan-railway|The Cosmopolitan Railway]].[5]
