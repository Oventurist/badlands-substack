# Ferdinand Pecora

Ferdinand Pecora was an Italian-American lawyer from New York whose work as chief counsel to the U.S. Senate Committee on Banking and Currency in 1932–1934 exposed the criminal practices of Wall Street's largest banks and made possible the banking reforms of the early New Deal.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes him as the central figure in an "often-forgotten war on Wall Street, which changed the course of human history."[1]

## Early life and legal career

A first-generation Italian-American, Pecora was forced to quit high school after his father was injured, taking work to support his family.[1] He later found employment as a clerk in a law firm and worked his way through law school, passing the bar in 1911.[1] His reputation for incorruptibility earned him the animosity of powerful New York financiers, who ensured that his successes in prosecuting brokers never resulted in his elevation to Attorney General — a post he was denied despite having shut down more than 100 illegal brokerage houses that speculated on fraudulent securities during the depression.[1]

## Chief counsel of the Banking and Currency Committee

The Committee on Banking and Currency, established in 1932 under the leadership of Senators Peter Norbeck and George Norris, was a relatively impotent body when it began.[1] That changed when Norbeck called in Pecora to lead its investigation in April 1932.[1] Within days of accepting the Washington post as chief counsel — for the meager salary of $250 per month — Pecora was granted broad subpoena powers to audit banks and compel the most powerful men in America to testify.[1]

In his first two weeks he made headlines by auditing the books of major Wall Street banks and summoning [[charles-e-mitchell|Charles Mitchell]], the pro-fascist president of [[national-city-bank|National City Bank]] who was then preparing to advise [[benito-mussolini|Benito Mussolini]].[1] Mitchell's expensive defense team could only watch as the financier admitted to short-selling his own bank's stock during the depression, scamming depositors with purchases of Cuban junk debt, and avoiding taxes for years.[1] Mitchell resigned in shame, followed days later by New York Stock Exchange chairman Richard Whitney, who left the court in handcuffs.[1] Similar displays of corruption were extracted from the heads of Kuhn Loeb, Chase Bank and [[brown-brothers-harriman|Brown Brothers Harriman]].[1]

Unlike the earlier 1911 [[pujo-committee|Pujo Commission]], which had also exposed Wall Street's abuses of power, the [[pecora-commission|Pecora Commission]] enjoyed the backing of a president who amplified its powers.[1] When [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]] was warned that supporting Pecora's exposures would hurt the economy, he replied, "they should have thought of that when they did the things that are being exposed now."[1] Roosevelt then encouraged Pecora to take on [[jp-morgan-jr|John Pierpont Morgan Jr.]][1]

## Aftermath and legacy

The hearings gave Roosevelt the political support needed to impose sweeping reforms in his first hundred days, including [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]] banking separation, the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation]], the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]] — on whose body Pecora was appointed commissioner in 1934 — and the [[reconstruction-finance-corporation|Reconstruction Finance Corporation]].[1] Pecora's ally Senator Burton Wheeler declared that "the best way to restore confidence in our banks is to take these crooked presidents out of the banks and treat them the same as we treated Al Capone."[1]

In 1939 Pecora published *Wall Street Under Oath: The Story of our Modern Money Changers*, warning that "under the surface of the governmental regulation of the securities market, the same forces that produced the riotous speculative excesses of the 'wild bull market' of 1929 still give evidence of their existence and influence. Though repressed for the present, it cannot be doubted that, given a suitable opportunity, they would spring back to their pernicious activity."[1] He added that "had there been full disclosure of what has been done in furtherance of these schemes, they could not long have survived the fierce light of publicity and criticism. Legal chicanery and pitch darkness were the bankers' stoutest allies."[1]

Ehret argues that a 21st-century revival of Pecora's method — paired with a restored Glass-Steagall — is the necessary remedy for the present financial system.[1]
