# Gulbuddin Hekmatyar

**Gulbuddin Hekmatyar** was an Afghan warlord backed by the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] among the [[mujahideen|Mujahedeen]] fighters during the Soviet occupation of [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] in the 1980s, and a known drug lord whose heroin empire supplied much of the world's market.[1]

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] places Hekmatyar at the centre of the narcotics dimension of [[operation-cyclone|Operation Cyclone]].[1] Afghan opium production surged during this period, with CIA-allied warlords controlling poppy-growing regions such as Helmand and Nangarhar; heroin was processed in laboratories along the Afghanistan-Pakistan border, often under the oversight of Pakistan's [[inter-services-intelligence|ISI]], and trafficked globally.[1] Because the agency's priority was defeating the Soviets rather than policing narcotics, drug activity by allies was largely ignored or tolerated as a byproduct of the war effort, if not encouraged.[1]

## Ties to Hafizullah Amin

[[paul-fitzgerald|Paul Fitzgerald]] and [[elizabeth-gould|Elizabeth Gould]] report in [[valediction-three-nights-of-desmond|Valediction: Three Nights of Desmond]] that Hekmatyar, a CIA asset, interfaced closely with his nominal enemy, Afghan President [[hafizullah-amin|Hafizullah Amin]], in an effort to bring global heroin production into Afghanistan.[2] The two men were in truth united as members of the same Ghilzai tribe, which had long sought to assert dominance over Kabul.[2]

## The CIA's heroin proxy in Afghanistan

"Who Is The Real Enemy?" describes Hekmatyar as the man the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] handpicked to run its heroin operation in [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] under the cover of "freedom fighter" and the [[cold-war|Cold War]].[3] He joined the Muslim Youth organization as a student in the early 1970s, where he was known for an Islamic radicalism rejected by much of the organization, and spent time in [[pakistan|Pakistan]] before returning to Afghanistan when the [[soviet-afghan-war|Soviet–Afghan War]] began in 1979 — at which point the CIA began funding his rapidly growing [[hezb-e-islami|Hezb-e Islami]] [[mujahideen|mujahideen]] organization through the Pakistani [[inter-services-intelligence|ISI]].[3] He received more CIA funding than any other mujahideen leader during the war.[3]

In the late 1980s Hekmatyar and his organization used CIA-supplied funds and weapons to begin trafficking opium and later to manufacture heroin, establishing themselves among the leading heroin suppliers in the Middle East; given the CIA connection, this became a subject of diplomatic embarrassment for the U.S. foreign service.[3] He first came to public attention in the 1980s after murdering a fellow student at Kabul University, and his prominence in Afghanistan's underworld let him leverage more money and guns from the Americans and the Saudis than any of the fifteen jihadist groups fighting the Soviets.[3]

Known as the "Butcher of Kabul," Hekmatyar spent 1992–96 raining rockets and artillery shells on the city during the post-Soviet scramble for spoils, a period in which as many as 50,000 people were slaughtered and Kabul fell from a metropolis of two million to perhaps 500,000 survivors.[3] Badlands Media notes he was listed as a terrorist at the United Nations, a designation removed as part of a later peace deal between Hekmatyar and the government of Ashraf Ghani brokered through the High Peace Council.[3]

The article argues that his protected heroin operation, funded through Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, brought in billions for [[george-hw-bush|Bush]] and the [[shadow-government|shadow government]], and that WhoWhatWhy identified him as the most notable of [[bank-of-credit-and-commerce-international|BCCI]]'s drug clients, emerging under General [[muhammad-zia-ul-haq|Zia]]'s protection as a dominant figure in the expanded Afghan heroin traffic.[3] Afghan opium production rose from 200 metric tons in 1980, the first full year of U.S. support for Hekmatyar, to 1,980 metric tons in 1991.[3]
