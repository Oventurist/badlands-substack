# Eli Lilly

Eli Lilly is a major American pharmaceutical company.[1]

## Acquisition of Fauci-linked mRNA startup

In 2026 reporting by [[natalie-winters|Natalie Winters]] described Eli Lilly as buying, for up to $1.55 billion, a stealth mRNA vaccine startup that [[anthony-fauci|Anthony Fauci]] had quietly joined in 2023. According to that account, the Biden administration had previously awarded the startup more than $28 million to develop vaccines for viruses with pandemic potential.[1]

The reporting circulated in the aftermath of Fauci's Senate hearing on Covid origins, where he invoked the Fifth Amendment, and was cited by Badlands commentary as part of a broader pattern of pandemic-era financial entanglement.[1]

## Texas kickback lawsuit (2025)

On August 12, 2025, Texas Attorney General [[ken-paxton|Ken Paxton]] sued Eli Lilly, alleging the company bribed and illegally induced medical providers to prescribe its most profitable drugs, including the GLP-1 medications Mounjaro and Zepbound used for weight loss and diabetes treatment.[2] Paxton said "Big Pharma compromised medical decision-making by engaging in an illegal kickback scheme," and his office described the suit as building on earlier actions against drug manufacturers for fraud and abuse.[2] Badlands host Ashe in America argued the case could set multiple precedents beyond Texas, contending that the bribing of physicians to push pills is nationwide and reflects a "sick care" model designed to keep patients as customers, and expressed hope the litigation would reach discovery.[2]

## "Spear of Justice" Analysis (2025)

An August 2025 Badlands Media essay framed the Texas suit against Eli Lilly as a strike on a "revenue artery" of entrenched power rather than a routine consumer-protection action.[3] The piece described the company's alleged "free nurses" program as marketing dressed as patient support that functioned in operational terms as an inducement pipeline pushing high-margin GLP-1 prescriptions through providers at scale.[3] It argued the decisive exposure would come at discovery, when pharmacy benefit manager contracts, hospital agreements, and insurer arrangements would enter the record.[3] Invoking the prisoner's dilemma, the author contended Eli Lilly faced only losing options: settle and lock in the precedent that inducements are prosecutable, or fight and risk having its internal playbook dragged into daylight.[3] The essay linked the case to broader systemic effects, reasoning that weakening pharmaceutical revenue also weakens the industry's capacity to fund lobbying, sponsor research narratives, and buy media influence.[3]
