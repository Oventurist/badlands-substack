# National Academy of Design

The National Academy of the Arts of Design was an artists' institution founded in New York by [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]], who served as its president from its founding until 1845, with the painter, playwright and historian [[william-dunlap|William Dunlap]] as co-founder and vice president.[1] It was created in direct opposition to the [[american-academy-of-fine-arts|American Academy of Fine Arts]] run by [[john-trumbull|John Trumbull]] and financed by [[robert-livingston|Robert Livingston]], and in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] it is treated as the institutional embodiment of a republican aesthetic in the United States.[1]

## Founding and conflict

Morse and Dunlap "lost no time launching a direct war" with what they regarded as the Anglophile dictatorship of Trumbull's Academy, denouncing its joint-stock structure in which artists were governed by stockholders ignorant of the arts.[1] Their new organisation immediately attracted talent: students who had felt stifled in the older New York body flocked to it, seeking freedom of expression, the ability to earn a living while showing deeper creative work, and a school in which they could teach the next generation and transmit their skills.[1] At its inception the Academy counted thirty of America's best talents, including [[thomas-cole|Thomas Cole]], Asher B. Durand, Frederick Agate and Martin E. Thompson.[1]

An attempted compromise, under which artists could buy onto the board of directors for $100, collapsed when Trumbull limited artist representation to two seats; Morse and Dunlap withdrew and the dispute moved into a series of public debates.[1] Morse's rebuttal of Trumbull's case for aristocratic patronage carried the day, and according to [[pierre-beaudry|Pierre Beaudry]] Trumbull was forced to resign by popular consent while the rival academy disintegrated within months.[1]

## Principles

Beaudry framed the outcome in terms drawn from [[friedrich-schiller|Friedrich Schiller]]'s lectures on universal history, as the difference between "studies for bread" and "studies for truth": what conferred the right to vote in the Academy of Fine Arts was a share in stocks, whereas what conferred the right to vote in the National Academy of Design was a share in principles.[1] This, Beaudry wrote, is how the difference between the British system and the American system was established at the National Academy of Design, the two proving irreconcilable.[1]

Morse's own 1827 discourse before the Academy stated that "we have taken the English Royal Academy for our model, as far as the different circumstances of form of government and state of taste will admit" — an apparent paradox for an anti-monarchist republican that the article attributes to Morse's training in England under [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] and to be resolved in a promised second part.[1]

## Legacy

The Academy became an engine for an entirely new school of arts, out of which emerged the [[hudson-river-school|Hudson River School]] of painters including Cole, Albert Bierstadt and Frederic Edwin Church.[1]
