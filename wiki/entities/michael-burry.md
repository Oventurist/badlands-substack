# Michael Burry

Michael Burry is an American investor best known as the "Big Short" trader who anticipated and profited from the collapse of the United States housing bubble in 2008, and who by 2025 had become one of the most closely watched skeptics of the artificial-intelligence equity boom.[1] He is the founder of [[scion-asset-management|Scion Asset Management]], the vehicle through which he has placed his most widely publicized bets.[1]

## Public profile and market warnings

Burry has a long-established pattern of periodically deleting his account on X before reemerging with terse, cryptic warnings; his 2025 return was marked by the message "Sometimes, we see bubbles."[1] Commentators treated the post as an unusually blunt statement from an investor whose reputation rests on identifying mispriced optimism, and it was read alongside his earlier 2023 "Sell" call.[1]

Days after the bubble post, Scion's 13F disclosure revealed that roughly 80 percent of the firm's put positions were concentrated in two of the market's leading momentum names, [[palantir|Palantir]] and [[nvidia|Nvidia]].[1] Burry subsequently clarified the scale of the Palantir wager on X, stating that he had purchased 50,000 option contracts at $1.84 each — each contract representing 100 shares — for a total outlay of roughly $9.2 million, rather than the $912 million notional figure that had circulated online.[1] The contracts conveyed the right to sell Palantir shares at $50 in 2027.[1]

## Deregistration of Scion

In November 2025, Scion Asset Management terminated its registration with the Securities and Exchange Commission, an action reported as Burry effectively "rage-quitting" the market and liquidating the fund.[1] The move came within roughly a week of the 13F disclosure and the bubble warning, and was framed in coverage as the culmination of a long-running argument that valuations had detached from underlying fundamentals.[1]

## Cultural legacy and commentary

Burry's 2008 trade was dramatized in Michael Lewis's book *The Big Short* and Adam McKay's film adaptation, in which he was portrayed by Christian Bale — a process that [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described as turning the investor into a "narrative artifact" whose public legend may bear only a passing resemblance to the man.[1] In that reading, Burry's exit is an ouroboros: the legend created by the market's storytelling apparatus turns around to call the market's bluff.[1]

Burning Bright argued that Burry's bearishness makes sense only under "the old rules of earnings and fundamentals," rules held to have lapsed in 1971 when [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]] severed the dollar's link to gold and converted markets into a narrative system in which perception outweighs production.[1] The commentary situated Burry's exit within the broader decoupling of the equity market from the physical economy tracked in the [[multipolar-margin-call|Multipolar Margin Call]] framing of the [[donald-trump|Trump]]–[[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] contest, contending that the real economy might be primed either for a surge or a controlled correction but that the stock market's "fractured glass" would not reveal which.[1]
