# Katie Porter

Katie Porter is a former Democratic member of the U.S. House of Representatives from California and a candidate for governor of California.[1] In October 2025 she became the subject of a widely circulated media controversy after attempting to end a televised interview during questioning about Trump voters in her state.[1]

## 2025 CBS News interview controversy

In a sit-down interview with CBS News correspondent Julie Watts that aired on Tuesday, October 7, 2025, Porter was asked what she would say to the roughly 40 percent of California voters who had voted for [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] and whose support she would need to win.[1] Porter responded, "How would I need them in order to win, ma'am?"[1]

Asked whether she believed she would win the other 60 percent of California voters, Porter said, "In a general election? Yes. If it is me versus a Republican, I think I will win the people who did not vote for Trump."[1] When Watts asked what would happen if the general election pitted Porter against another Democrat — a plausible outcome under California's top-two primary system — Porter replied, "I don't intend that to be the case," arguing that she had already built support through name recognition.[1]

Porter grew visibly irritated as Watts pressed the line of questioning, characterizing the interview as "unnecessarily argumentative."[1] Watts responded that [[cbs-news|CBS News]] had asked the other candidates in the race the same question.[1] Porter then said, "I don't want to keep doing this. I'm gonna call it. Thank you," while looking off camera, effectively ending the interview.[1] Video of the exchange spread rapidly online.[1]

## Badlands commentary

The [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] treated the episode as a case study in candidate fragility, noting that Porter is running to be the chief executive of what commentators describe as the fifth largest economy in the world.[1] Contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that Porter first delegitimized 40 percent of the state's electorate by appearing offended at the suggestion that she would need Trump supporters to win, a posture that might itself generate more Trump support in California.[1] Ashe further argued that Porter, realizing her mistake, pivoted into a complaint about the interviewer's conduct, and that when Watts noted the questions were standard across the field, Porter shifted to claiming the follow-ups were inefficient.[1]

In the Badlands framing, the interviewer was fair and Porter was trapped, sinking further into "rhetorical quick sand" as she tried to escape.[1] The commentary concluded that in an election decided by critical-thinking voters the exchange would be politically fatal, but that because Badlands contributors regard American elections as unreliable, Porter would nonetheless likely win the Democratic nomination.[1]
