# Paul Nitze

Paul Nitze was an American financier and national security strategist who led the [[committee-on-the-present-danger|Committee on the Present Danger]].[1] In 1950 he used that organisation to spearhead the passage of [[nsc-68|NSC-68]], the policy document that first justified the notion that the United States should maximise its build-up of nuclear warheads on the supposition that it stood in a moral equivalent of war with Russia.[1]

Throughout the 1960s, saner forces pushed back against Nitze's Committee, producing the nuclear test ban treaty, the Open Skies Treaty, the Space Treaty and other trust-building measures, and the 1972 [[salt-i|SALT]] agreement extended those mechanisms.[1]

Nitze's Committee was revived in 1976 through the new think tank [[team-b|Team B]], where Nitze and his neoconservative colleagues deployed what [[paul-fitzgerald|Paul Fitzgerald]] and [[elizabeth-gould|Elizabeth Gould]] call vast data-crunching sophistry to overwhelm the incompetent second-rate minds assigned to defend the official National Intelligence Estimate.[1] Alongside [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]], [[paul-wolfowitz|Paul Wolfowitz]], [[richard-pipes|Richard Pipes]], [[richard-perle|Richard Perle]] and [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]], Nitze held that the détente-era push toward trust and cooperation with Moscow had to stop.[1]
