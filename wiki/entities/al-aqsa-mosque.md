# Al-Aqsa Mosque

Al-Aqsa Mosque is the Islamic holy site standing, together with the Dome of the Rock shrine, on the raised plateau in Jerusalem's Old City known to Jews as the Temple Mount.[1] It sits at the center of disputes over proposals to construct a [[third-temple|third Jewish temple]] in Jerusalem.[1]

## Contested site

According to Jewish tradition, the ashes of a perfectly red heifer are needed for the ritual purification that would allow a third temple to be built in Jerusalem, and radical Jewish groups hold that such a temple must be constructed on the Temple Mount plateau where Al-Aqsa and the Dome of the Rock stand today.[1] Advocates of the [[red-heifer|red heifer]] ritual say the slaughter of the animals on the Mount of Olives would allow Jewish people to be purified so they could perform rites and worship on the site of Al-Aqsa Mosque.[1] Some believe the sequence will herald the arrival of the messiah and possibly the end of the world.[1]

In August 2024, a group of religious Israelis, identified as activists of the [[temple-institute|Temple Institute]], were pictured practising the red heifer ritual in front of the mosque and the Temple Mount.[1] Badlands commentary noted that the construction of the new temple organized by the Temple Institute would require the destruction of Al-Aqsa Mosque.[1]

## Ben-Gvir synagogue proposal (August 2024)

In an interview with Army Radio in late August 2024, Israeli National Security Minister [[itamar-ben-gvir|Itamar Ben-Gvir]] called for a synagogue to be built atop the Al-Aqsa compound, Islam's third holiest site, and argued that Jews should have unrestricted access to pray there.[2] The remarks triggered immediate controversy, with some Israeli groups condemning them as needlessly inflammatory and unrealistic, and drew outrage from [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] at a moment when Saudi-Israeli normalization talks were already stalled by the Gaza war.[2] Commentators noted that constructing a synagogue or a third temple on the site — which sits on the Temple Mount above the Western Wall — would require destroying the mosque and could provoke uprisings across the Islamic world large enough to trigger a broader regional war.[2]

Badlands Media reported that Ben-Gvir had repeatedly led "prayer groups" onto the compound grounds in violation of longstanding rules barring Israeli prayer and protest at the site, restrictions rooted in past attacks by Israeli extremists such as Meir Kahane and Baruch Goldstein.[2]
