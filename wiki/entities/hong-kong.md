# Hong Kong

Hong Kong is a territory on the southern coast of China that came under British control as a direct result of the [[opium-wars|Opium Wars]].[1]

## Acquisition by Britain

Following the first Opium War, the British Navy compelled China to accept British opium imports, pay for opium that Chinese authorities had confiscated, and cede sovereignty over several offshore islands — the mechanism by which Britain came to control Hong Kong.[1]

## Role in the narcotics economy

After the second Opium War of 1860, the [[hsbc|Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation]] was founded in the territory and, according to [[dope-inc-book|*Dope, Inc.*]], served as the central clearinghouse for Far Eastern financial transactions tied to the opium and heroin black market.[1] Hong Kong also hosted British opium refineries which, alongside those in Shanghai, supplied narcotics into the United States during [[prohibition|Prohibition]].[1] Agents of the organized crime bosses [[meyer-lansky|Meyer Lansky]] and [[arnold-rothstein|Arnold Rothstein]] made their first trips to the Far East in the 1920s to buy heroin from the British.[1]

## British rule and the 1997 handover

Britain controlled Hong Kong for over one hundred and fifty years, apart from a brief Japanese occupation during the Second World War, and the territory did not fully return to Chinese rule until 1997.[2] Conversations between the Chinese and the British over its future did not begin until the 1970s and 1980s, culminating in the [[sino-british-joint-declaration|Joint Declaration]], under which Hong Kong would keep a high degree of autonomy for 50 years under the slogan "[[one-country-two-systems|One country, two systems]]" — an arrangement ratified by the [[united-nations|United Nations]] and supported by the United States.[2]

Hong Kong was never fully democratic: from 1982 to 1997 it moved from having no elected government to a fully elected Legislative Council, though not entirely by geographical constituencies, and after 1 July 1997 it regressed toward its pre-democracy condition as appointed provisional bodies replaced elected ones.[2] Press freedom, speech freedom, the right to a fair trial and freedom of assembly nonetheless persisted, and these factors largely made the territory a prime financial hub for international business.[2]

## Erosion of autonomy after 1997

Those freedoms are nonexistent today, and Badlands Media described Hong Kong as an unprecedented case in which a city of more than 7 million people had its rights stripped away in a remarkably short period; promised 50 years of autonomy, it received less than half of that.[2] Beijing's 2010 reversal on universal suffrage triggered the [[umbrella-movement|Umbrella Movement]], a 79-day occupation of the financial district that was the largest act of civil disobedience in the territory's history to that point, and a proposed extradition law later provoked the [[2019-hong-kong-anti-extradition-protests|2019 anti-extradition protests]] in which two million people marched.[2] The [[chinese-communist-party|CCP]] answered with the [[hong-kong-national-security-law-2020|National Security Law]], which was used to jail [[jimmy-lai|Jimmy Lai]], shutter [[apple-daily|Apple Daily]] and place overseas activists such as [[samuel-chu|Samuel Chu]] of the [[hong-kong-democracy-council|Hong Kong Democracy Council]] on fugitive lists.[2] [[human-rights-watch|Human Rights Watch]] reported that police and courts had become "tools of Chinese state control," while [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] was said to be presiding over the most oppressive period in China's history since Mao's Cultural Revolution.[2]
