# Nicholas Murray Butler

Nicholas Murray Butler was an American academic who served as president of [[columbia-university|Columbia University]]. Badlands Media identifies him, alongside the newspaper mogul [[william-randolph-hearst|Randolph Hearst]], as one of the two figures most responsible for bringing the idea of [[technocracy|Technocracy]] to American public attention in the early 1930s.[1]

## Endorsement of technocracy

During the depths of the economic depression, Butler publicly endorsed a new economic system being planned by scientists and engineers. The system was touted as a cure for all of America's economic woes and was intended to replace capitalism and free markets outright. It was called "Technocracy."[1]

Butler's endorsement, from the platform of a leading Ivy League university, gave the movement intellectual respectability. It also attracted the attention of Hearst, who published a series of articles on technocracy that generated widespread curiosity and intense debate among intellectuals and the general public.[1] Badlands frames this episode as the origin point of an idea that would later be developed by [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]] into the concept of the "technetronic society," and carried into the present by figures such as [[bill-gates|Bill Gates]], [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]] and [[yuval-noah-harari|Yuval Noah Harari]].[1]

## Expulsion of Technocracy and the 1933 task force

Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] records that as [[technocracy-inc|Technocracy Inc.]] faced its first crisis in early 1933, with leading members departing over its dictatorial proclivities, pressure was placed on [[columbia-university|Columbia University]] president Nicholas Murray Butler to expel [[howard-scott|Howard Scott]]'s group, which occurred on 18 January 1933.[2] Quoting the researcher Carl Teichrib, Ehret notes that in the same month Butler announced the creation of a special task force to examine technological progress in the nation, naming to it Edmund Day of the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]], the Fabian socialist [[walter-lippmann|Walter Lippmann]], Alvin Johnson of the [[new-school-for-social-research|New School for Social Research]], Benjamin Anderson of Chase National Bank, and professors from Yale, Harvard and the University of Chicago.[2] Butler explained that "the inquiry will be directed in particular to the technique of production and the technique of exchange… for the service of society," with the price-based economy and its adequacy for "social welfare" as the topic of choice — in other words, Ehret argues, technocracy under institutional sponsorship.[2]
