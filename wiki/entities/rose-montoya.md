# Rose Montoya

Rose Montoya is an American transgender social-media influencer and activist who became the subject of national controversy in June 2023 after appearing topless in a video filmed at a [[white-house|White House]] event.[1]

## The June 2023 White House pride event

Montoya attended a White House LGBT "pride" celebration on Saturday, June 10, 2023, and posted a TikTok video from the event in which Montoya went topless, covering the nipples with both hands, as a voice on the recording asked, "Are we topless at the White House?"[1] Another individual in the same display appeared with visible scarring from breast-removal surgery.[1] The video circulated widely and drew immediate criticism.[1]

## Ban from the White House

On Tuesday, June 13, 2023, the White House confirmed to the New York Post that Montoya had been barred from the facility.[1] A representative for the residence said the "behavior is inappropriate and disrespectful for any event at the White House" and that it was "not reflective of the event we hosted to celebrate LGBTQI+ families or the other hundreds of guests who were in attendance."[1] The ban was notable in part because Montoya had previously been photographed with President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[1]

## Commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] argued that the episode exposed the arbitrariness of the administration's standards rather than a return to decorum, contrasting the swift sanction of Montoya with the administration's tolerance of what he characterized as far more consequential conduct, including allegations surrounding the president's son.[1] He framed Montoya's apparent belief that the display would advance trans rights as a miscalculation that instead forced the White House to draw a line it had not previously drawn.[1]

## Comments on corporate Pride pullback

Days after the White House ban, Montoya was quoted by the Guardian lamenting the corporate retreat from Pride marketing: "I've spoken to a lot of my trans friends and colleagues, and we've all noticed less brands seeking partnerships and smaller budgets for Pride campaigns. It's disappointing."[2] The remarks were made in the context of reporting that [[starbucks|Starbucks]] had asked workers in at least 21 states to remove Pride decorations, following the [[target|Target]] and [[bud-light|Bud Light]] boycotts.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] highlighted the juxtaposition of Montoya's White House ban with her complaint about Starbucks "backing off rainbow communism," treating the corporate pullback as evidence that consumer boycotts were outweighing the purchasing power of Pride-aligned consumers.[2]
