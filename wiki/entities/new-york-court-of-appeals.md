# New York Court of Appeals

The New York Court of Appeals is the highest court in the state of New York. On Thursday, April 25, 2024, the court threw out [[harvey-weinstein|Harvey Weinstein]]'s 2020 rape conviction, finding that the trial judge had unfairly allowed testimony against Weinstein based on allegations that were not part of the charges in the case, and ordering a new trial.[1]

## The Weinstein ruling and its reception

The decision shocked and disappointed women who had celebrated the historic gains of the [[metoo-movement|#MeToo]] era, and left those who had testified in the case bracing for a retrial against the former movie mogul.[1] Weinstein, then 72, remained in prison because of a separate 2022 rape conviction obtained in Los Angeles, but the New York ruling was widely described as reopening a painful chapter in America's reckoning with sexual misconduct by powerful figures — an era that began in 2017 with a flood of allegations against Weinstein.[1] #MeToo advocates emphasized that the ruling rested on legal technicalities rather than any exoneration of Weinstein's conduct, and argued that the original trial had irrevocably moved the cultural needle on attitudes toward sexual assault.[1]

## Badlands commentary: discretion and the Trump parallel

Badlands Media writer Ashe in America framed the ruling as a case study in [[judicial-abuse-of-discretion|judicial abuse of discretion]], arguing that broad discretionary rulings can compound into abuse of power when their net effect is manifest injustice — which, in her reading, is precisely the foundation of the appellate decision in Weinstein's favor.[1] She drew an explicit parallel to the New York cases against [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]], contending that judges including [[lewis-kaplan|Kaplan]], [[arthur-engoron|Engoron]], and [[juan-merchan|Merchan]] had exercised broad discretion in an effort to "get Trump," and that the same activists who built the #MeToo movement would now have to defend against Trump the very discretionary abuses they decried in Weinstein's case.[1] The commentary conceded that Weinstein appeared to be "a monster" who deserved imprisonment while insisting that even monsters retain due process rights, and that trying cases in the court of public opinion eventually gives way to reality — here, seven years after the initial allegations and four years after conviction.[1]
