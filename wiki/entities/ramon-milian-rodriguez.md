# Ramon Milian Rodriguez

Ramon Milian Rodriguez is a Cuban-born American accountant who became the chief money launderer for the Colombian [[medellin-cartel]] and later testified before Congress that he had also worked for the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]].[1] Badlands Media contributor Joe Lange treats him as the central figure linking the agency's covert finance apparatus to the cocaine trade.[1]

## Background and recruitment

According to a 1988 Frontline investigation, Milian Rodriguez was recruited in 1972 by [[manuel-artime]], the CIA's handpicked Cuban commander of [[brigade-2506]], who arranged CIA training for the "brilliant young accountant."[1] Lange stresses that Milian Rodriguez was a Cuban-American, part of a pattern in which Cuban exiles — among them [[felix-rodriguez]] — occupied key roles in the covert apparatus he describes as a [[shadow-government]].[1]

Milian Rodriguez told Frontline that Artime "ran covert operations out of Miami for the CIA," that the operation was "very large" and "very active, all over Central and South America," and that Artime "was in charge of, among other things, the [[watergate|Watergate]] burglars." Asked whether he had laundered money for the Watergate team, he answered that he made payments for the burglars, adding: "I start out in life in one scandal and I've ended it in another."[1] After Watergate, he said, Artime's Miami group was disbanded.[1]

## Cartel laundering

The New York Times reported in February 1988 that Milian Rodriguez, then 36 and serving a 43-year sentence for money laundering, testified under oath to a Senate Foreign Relations subcommittee that he had laundered billions of dollars in drug profits through Panama's banking system with the help of General [[manuel-noriega]].[1] He estimated a "ballpark figure" of $320 million to $350 million paid to Noriega for his services, at commissions of between 1 and 10 percent depending on the transaction.[1]

In hundreds of trips to Panama he moved roughly $11 billion in drug profits from secret safehouses around the United States run by the Medellin cartel, through Miami to Panama. He said he personally earned $2 million to $3 million a month and, toward the end of his career, was laundering $200 million a month.[1] In exchange for his commissions, Noriega provided the use of Panama's airports, banking system and security officers, and supplied information on the identity of American drug agents, radio frequencies, and the schedules of Coast Guard and US Navy surveillance vessels.[1]

Beyond Panama, Milian Rodriguez testified that he laundered money through Curaçao, Switzerland, Hong Kong and the Bahamas — a set of jurisdictions Lange connects to the [[nugan-hand-bank]] and [[castle-bank-and-trust]] networks built by CIA financier [[paul-helliwell]].[1] He also described dealing with people who worked for US banks but were not on the official employment rolls, naming Citicorp — now [[citigroup]] — and [[bank-of-america]].[1]

## Contra funding and CIA payments

The Washington Post reported in June 1987 that Milian Rodriguez had told Congress in secret testimony that he funneled nearly $10 million from the cartel to the Nicaraguan Contras through former CIA operative [[felix-rodriguez]], with the money delivered in several installments after his own 1983 arrest and passed in Central America to couriers Rodriguez selected.[1]

The media watchdog FAIR reported that in February testimony before the Senate Subcommittee on Narcotics, Terrorism and International Operations, Milian Rodriguez acknowledged laundering more than $3 million for the CIA after his 1983 indictment on drug charges — and that this admission was not reported by the New York Times, the Washington Post, or the three major networks, all of which covered the hearings.[1] He also said he had been trained in money laundering by men whose names he never learned and later delivered cash to the families of Watergate burglars.[1]

## Interpretation

Lange argues that the consistent media framing of Milian Rodriguez as merely "the Medellin cartel's accountant," omitting his CIA recruitment and training, is itself evidence of narrative management — and that the more accurate question is whether he was working for the cartel or for the agency.[1]
