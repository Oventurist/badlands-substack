# Józef Retinger

Dr. Józef Hieronim Retinger (d. 1960) was a Polish scholar, publicist and self-described "international political activist" who is widely regarded by the researchers cited in [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]]'s "The Men Behind the Curtain" series as the "father of the Bilderbergers."[1] DeLarme describes him as a Polish socialist and a founder of the European Movement after the Second World War who had access to some of the leading power brokers of the twentieth century.[1]

## Introduction to the American establishment

Retinger was first ushered to America by Averell Harriman, then U.S. ambassador to England and a member of the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]].[1] During that trip he is said to have visited prominent citizens including [[david-rockefeller|David]] and [[nelson-rockefeller|Nelson Rockefeller]], [[john-foster-dulles|John Foster Dulles]] and then [[cia|CIA]] Director [[walter-bedell-smith|Walter Bedell Smith]].[1] Earlier he had formed the [[american-committee-on-united-europe|American Committee on a United Europe]] with future CIA Director and CFR member [[allen-dulles|Allen Dulles]], then CFR Director George Franklin, CIA official Thomas Braden, and [[william-donovan|William Donovan]], former chief of the [[office-of-strategic-services|Office of Strategic Services]].[1]

## Freemasonry, the Vatican and the Jesuits

Retinger was a Freemason with a reputation as a *éminence grise* — a powerful decision-maker or adviser operating behind the scenes in a non-public or unofficial capacity.[1] As a Catholic he was viewed by many as an agent of the Vatican, acting as liaison between the Pope and the Father-General of the Jesuit order.[1] From his days at the Sorbonne he believed in greater European unity in both military and economic terms, and his interest in Jesuit guidance manifested early: he proposed to Premier Georges Clemenceau a plan to unite Eastern Europe by merging Austria, Hungary and Poland into a tripartite monarchy under the guidance of the Jesuit order.[1] Clemenceau, doubtful of the Vatican-inspired plan, rejected it outright, and the episode labelled Retinger thereafter as a Vatican agent.[1]

## Wartime and postwar career

After the outbreak of the Second World War Retinger became principal adviser to the Polish government-in-exile formed after the invasion of Poland.[1] Afterwards he co-founded the European Movement, which DeLarme says led to the establishment of the [[european-union|European Union]] — a goal he argues had been pursued for centuries by sub-groups hidden within [[freemasonry|Freemasonry]] and the [[knights-templar|Templars]].[1] Retinger was the initiator and architect of the informal Bilderberg conferences of 1952–54 and served as their permanent secretary until his death in London in 1960; the British Labour foreign secretary Denis Healey, a founding member, described the meetings as Retinger's "brainchild."[1] His stated purpose was to "stimulate understanding between Europe and the US as the Cold War developed by bringing together financiers, industrialists, politicians and opinion formers."[1]

His ambitions extended beyond Europe: through several trips to Mexico he played a key role in creating a trade union movement in the 1920s, and having gained the Mexican government's trust he convinced it to nationalise U.S. oil interests, conducting secret negotiations with Washington on Mexico's behalf.[1] In the 1950s his high-profile career let him build contacts among high-ranking military officials and political leaders.[1]

## Political philosophy

Retinger's aim, as DeLarme presents it, was to unite the world in peace under the control of supernational, powerful organizations that would be immune from short-term ideological conflicts between governments.[1] He considered a country's dominant economic ideology insignificant, believing differences could be brought into line by multinational organizations dictating economic and military policy and thereby creating a union and bond between nations.[1] His left-wing background gave him access to leaders of newly born socialist and communist states, while his church background opened dialogue with the middle ground of international relations.[1] Knowing that control of world affairs required U.S. participation, he campaigned for the creation of an "Atlantic Community" that would make Europe's development an important political aim for American politicians and prevent their retreat into isolation.[1]

At his funeral in 1960 Sir Edward Bedington-Behrens recalled: "I remember Retinger in the United States picking up the telephone and immediately making an appointment with the President, and in Europe he had complete entrée in every political circle as a kind of right acquired through trust, devotion and loyalty he inspired."[1] Together with Lord [[victor-rothschild|Victor Rothschild]] he encouraged [[prince-bernhard|Prince Bernhard]] to create the [[bilderberg-group|Bilderberg Group]], and he continued participating in its meetings until his death.[1]
