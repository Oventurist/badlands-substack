# Marquis de Lafayette

The Marquis de Lafayette was a French nobleman who fought for the American Revolution and later became the pivotal, and in Badlands Media commentary tragically irresolute, figure of French republicanism.[1] [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] treats him as a man caught between two worlds whose failure to break with aristocracy shaped two centuries of history.[1]

## The American Revolution

Lafayette was famous for bringing the French army to the aid of the American revolution, an intervention orchestrated by [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], then ambassador to France, whose networks in the intelligentsia enabled the 1777 alliance that tipped the balance against the British Empire and proved a deciding factor in the war's outcome.[1] Lafayette was only eighteen at the time and offered his services even before the Franco-American alliance was formalised.[1] Though a member of the nobility, he shared the disdain of many younger nobles for the decadence and immorality of an oligarchical culture living off the parasitical exploitation of the majority, and had absorbed the republican principle that all people are made in the living image of God.[1]

## Failure in 1789

Returning to France to extend the revolution by ending the stranglehold of hereditary institutions, Lafayette did as well as could be expected in the early stages of 1789, but retained a loyalty to aristocratic forms he could never fully break.[1] Offered the popular demand to become president after the tennis court oath, he refused, attempting instead an impossible fusion of republicanism and monarchism alongside King Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette.[1] The result, in Ehret's account, was that the monarchy was destroyed in favour of the Jacobin Terror, in which allies of Franklin such as Antoine Lavoisier and Jean-Sylvain Bailly were beheaded; Lafayette kept his own head only by fleeing France and spending four years in an Austrian Habsburg dungeon.[1] The Napoleonic Wars and the [[congress-of-vienna|Congress of Vienna]] restoration followed as downstream consequences.[1] He had written to his wife in 1792 that "you know that my heart would have been Republican if my reason had not given me a nuance of royalism."[1]

## The American tour and the second chance

As one of the youngest soldiers of the war of independence, Lafayette became the last living American Revolutionary leader by 1826, touring the United States from July 1824 to September 1825 in preparation for something global intended to culminate in 1830–32.[1] He was a leading figure in the International Society of Cincinnati and the Philosophical Society founded by Franklin as an international republican agency with members across Russia, Europe and the United States, and served as president of the European branch of the [[society-of-the-cincinnati|Society of the Cincinnatus]].[1] In America he organised for the presidency of John Quincy Adams, who won by a single vote, tipping the balance toward the nationalists against the Wall Street and Tammany Hall interests, and met for several days with William Lyon Mackenzie, later leader of the 1837–38 Rebellion of the Patriots of Upper Canada.[1]

## 1830 and the balcony

The Three Glorious Days of riots — the article dates them to July 1829 — brought the repercussions of the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 and the 1815 Congress of Vienna home to roost, and Lafayette again positioned himself to become president of a republican France by removing the Bourbon king Charles X.[1] According to the French historian and foreign minister Gabriel Hanotaux, Lafayette told the Duke of Orleans on the morning of the decisive speech, "You know that I am a republican and that I consider the Constitution of the United States as the most perfect that ever existed," but concluded that "what the French people need today, is a popular monarchy, surrounded by republican institutions, completely republican," to which the prince replied, "That is precisely what I intend to do."[1] Instead of declaring a republic before crowds that included [[james-fenimore-cooper|James Fenimore Cooper]] and [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]], Lafayette endorsed the Duke of Orleans as king; he was made head of the National Guard and swiftly dismissed, and the new French king immediately allied with the British Empire.[1]

## Morse's portrait

Morse was commissioned in 1826, through Cooper, to paint Lafayette, and embedded visual ironies in the result: ominous dark clouds overwhelming the sunlit sky, busts of Franklin and [[george-washington|George Washington]] regarding the Marquis as if in judgment, and an ambiguity between motion and stasis in his posture — feet aligned for movement, right hand fixed on a column.[1] Ehret reads this as a deliberate compression into one figure of the motion-versus-stasis contrast Raphael used between Plato and Aristotle in *The School of Athens*, expressing Lafayette's unresolved dual devotion to aristocracy and republicanism.[1] The republican intelligentsia hoped he might resolve the paradox in old age, but by the time of the painting it was apparent he did not have it in him.[1] Lafayette, Cooper and Morse all lived several doors from one another in Paris, spending evenings working, planning and playing music together, with Morse on flute and Cooper on piano.[1]
