# Alfred Herrhausen

Alfred Herrhausen was the chairman and president of [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]] who, in the closing years of the Cold War, promoted a strategic vision for a productive zone of advanced development spanning Europe and Russia.[1] In Badlands Media's account of the post-Cold War period, Herrhausen is presented as one of the central figures of a brief window in which East–West relations might have been reorganized around industrial and scientific cooperation rather than confrontation.[1]

## Role in the 1988–1992 window

By 1988 it had become increasingly clear that the system of mutually assured destruction was coming to an end, as the rigid economic systems of the Soviet bloc proved incapable of introducing the technological innovations to the civilian economy needed to avert a general breakdown.[1] Back-channel discussions had been arranged over several years between leading figures of the Gorbachev administration, their American counterparts in the Reagan administration, and German industrial leaders led by Herrhausen as Deutsche Bank chairman.[1]

Leading American statesmen affiliated with the Reagan administration joined Herrhausen in calling for billions of dollars in development aid to assist the Soviet Union in embracing industrial capitalism and democracy.[1] These figures worked to present development plans involving billions of dollars of promised investment into the modernization of all sectors of the Soviet economy, premised on large-scale infrastructure and industrial growth.[1]

## Assessment

The article characterizes Herrhausen and his collaborators as "anti-Malthusian statesmen" who may not have fully appreciated the forces they were challenging, but who nonetheless worked to end the Cold War not by crushing Russia into oblivion but by building a new synergy of industrial and scientific cooperation between East and West.[1] The story of these plans is recounted in the autobiography of American University in Moscow's Edward Lozansky and in the 2008 [[schiller-institute|Schiller Institute]] documentary *The Lost Chance of 1989*.[1]

Instead of the cooperative outcome Herrhausen envisioned, the 1990s brought what the article describes as a bloodied Russia "swimming with sharks," subjected to [[shock-therapy|Shock Therapy]] overseen by the [[imf|IMF]], the [[city-of-london|City of London]] and Washington policy circles.[1]
