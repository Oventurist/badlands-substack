# Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) is the United States federal body responsible for coordinating disaster response and long-term recovery, operating under the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]].[1] Badlands Media coverage in December 2025 treated the agency as an institution under active reform pressure, following persistent criticism of its performance in recent domestic disasters.[1]

## Office of Response and Recovery

FEMA's Office of Response and Recovery directs federal disaster deployments and the agency's long-term recovery efforts. Senior FEMA officials have described leadership of that office as the agency's "second most important role," because the position governs the speed and effectiveness of federal support delivered after a disaster.[1] In December 2025 the Department of Homeland Security confirmed the appointment of [[gregg-phillips|Gregg Phillips]] to lead the office.[1]

## Criticism and reform pressure

FEMA has drawn sustained criticism from within the populist right, particularly over its handling of Hurricane Helene relief in North Carolina. Phillips, before his appointment, traveled to North Carolina after the storm and publicly accused the agency of failing affected communities, faulting FEMA's contracting operations and arguing that outside volunteer relief groups had repeatedly filled gaps left by the agency.[1] Phillips subsequently cited a "meaningful and productive" call with President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and then–acting Administrator David Richardson as the source of renewed optimism that reforms were possible.[1]

## National alerting role

FEMA jointly administers the nationwide emergency alert system with the [[federal-communications-commission|Federal Communications Commission]], and the two agencies plan and execute periodic nationwide tests of it.[2] On October 4, 2023, they conducted the [[2023-nationwide-emergency-alert-test|2023 nationwide emergency alert test]], which pinged cell phones across the United States and sent alerts to radios and televisions — the third nationwide test of the wireless emergency alert system and only the second directed at all cellphones.[2] The agencies said the purpose was to ensure the systems "continue to be effective means of warning the public about emergencies, particularly those on the national level," and the test message itself explained that no actual emergency was under way.[2] FEMA had previously run nationwide wireless tests in October 2018, as a "presidential alert," and in August 2021.[2]

## Funding shortfall during the 2024 hurricane season

On Wednesday, October 2, 2024, Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] told reporters aboard Air Force One that FEMA could meet immediate needs but did not have enough funding to make it through the hurricane season.[3] The agency was being stretched as it worked with states to assess damage from [[hurricane-helene|Hurricane Helene]] and delivered meals, water, generators and other critical supplies; the storm had struck Florida the prior week before plowing through several Southeastern states, flooding towns and killing more than 160 people.[3] "We are meeting the immediate needs with the money that we have. We are expecting another hurricane hitting," Mayorkas said. "FEMA does not have the funds to make it through the season."[3] He was not specific about how much additional money would be required, but his remarks underscored concerns voiced by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and some lawmakers that [[congress|Congress]] might need to pass a supplemental spending bill that fall to help states with recovery.[3] Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30, with most hurricanes typically occurring in September and October.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that Mayorkas was "claiming poverty for FEMA, a massive agency with a massive budget," and treated the shortfall as evidence of interconnected failures: mismanagement of the U.S. border, the reallocation of disaster-relief funding to offset the resulting budget shortfall, and the consequent lack of resources to manage Hurricane Helene.[3] She placed these alongside an energy crisis, the supply-chain crisis surrounding the [[2024-east-and-gulf-coast-dockworker-strike|port strike]], and Middle East escalation as components of a deliberate [[polycrisis|polycrisis]].[3]

## Trump's January 2025 criticism

In an interview with Fox News's Sean Hannity on Wednesday, January 22, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] suggested he was considering the agency's future, saying he planned to have a "big discussion very shortly" about FEMA because he would rather see the states handle their own problems.[4] Trump said FEMA had not done its job for the last four years and "gets in the way," citing the agency's response to [[hurricane-helene|the devastating hurricane in North Carolina]] the previous year.[4] The remarks came days before Trump's first trip of his second term, scheduled for Friday, January 24, 2025, to visit victims of flooding in North Carolina and of the [[january-2025-los-angeles-wildfires|ongoing wildfires]] in Los Angeles — disasters that many believed had been worsened by government incompetence.[4]

## Alleged bribe offer in the Ramirez case (2021–2023)

In January 2023 The Western Journal reported that [[ernest-ramirez|Ernest Ramirez]] said FEMA officials had contacted him after the death of his 16-year-old son Ernesto, who died on April 24, 2021 — five days after receiving a COVID-19 vaccine — from complications related to an enlarged heart.[5] According to Ramirez, agency officials asked him to change his son's listed cause of death to "COVID" and offered financial assistance in exchange; he refused and went public.[5]

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] wrote that there is likely no greater "red pill" for a parent than the untimely death of a child caused by government, corporate media, regulatory agencies and medical professionals lying about an experimental drug, calling FEMA's alleged offer of hush money to a grieving father "the cherry on top."[5] He predicted that bereaved families would grow more galvanized to speak out through 2023 until "the dam will burst."[5]

## 2025 Israel boycott certification requirement

In August 2025 the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] reversed a policy that had required U.S. cities and states to rebuke boycotts of Israeli companies in order to receive federal disaster funds, deleting the language from the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] website.[6] Reuters reported that the requirement applied to at least $1.9 billion in funds covering search-and-rescue equipment, emergency manager salaries, and backup power systems across eleven agency grant notices.[6] After DHS posted on X that no such requirement existed, the post was annotated with a Community Note clarifying that the conditions had only just been removed and had previously been [[dhs-israel-boycott-certification-requirement|FEMA policy]].[6]

## Funding strain during the 2024 hurricane season

As [[hurricane-milton|Hurricane Milton]] bore down on Florida in October 2024, less than two weeks after [[hurricane-helene|Hurricane Helene]] flooded communities across the Southeast, reporting described the federal government as potentially nearing a collapse of its capacity to assist with major disasters.[7] FEMA's disaster relief fund, which pays for repair and rebuilding, and FEMA's flood insurance program — along with the [[small-business-administration|Small Business Administration]]'s loans to stricken businesses and homeowners — were all described as possibly within weeks of running out of cash, based on remarks by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] and insurance analysts.[7] FEMA nonetheless sought to assure the public that it had enough money to meet its "life-saving" responsibilities for both storms.[7] Lawmakers of both parties urged Congress to return to Washington before the November election to approve additional disaster funding, but House fiscal conservatives balked and Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] said he had no plans to recall members.[7] Florida Governor [[ron-desantis|Ron DeSantis]] said during the same period that he was in contact with FEMA officials while preparing the state for Milton.[7]
