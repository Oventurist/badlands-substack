# Adam Rubenstein

Adam Rubenstein is a former opinion-section editor at [[new-york-times|The New York Times]] who, in February 2024, published a lengthy essay in [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] describing what he characterized as pervasive ideological bias and activism inside the newspaper.[1]

## The Atlantic essay

Rubenstein's essay was framed as pulling back the curtain on the paper and its alleged bias in news coverage, and followed similar accounts from other departed Times figures including [[bari-weiss|Bari Weiss]] and Rubenstein's former colleague James Bennet.[1] The piece described a newsroom culture so ideologically homogeneous that Rubenstein's passing remark that he liked Chick-fil-A sandwiches drew condemnation from shocked colleagues.[1] More broadly, he described an environment in which staff routinely rejected conservative viewpoints, subjected conservative contributors to additional demands and editing, and resisted working on such pieces at all.[1]

## Role in the Tom Cotton op-ed controversy

As an opinion editor, Rubenstein was involved in the 2020 controversy over the publication of Senator [[tom-cotton|Tom Cotton]]'s op-ed arguing for the possible use of the National Guard to quell violent rioting around the White House.[1] Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]], writing about the essay, called the episode one of the lowest points in the history of modern American journalism, noting that Times reporters and columnists denounced the column as historically inaccurate and politically incendiary, claimed that Cotton was endangering them, and insisted the paper could not platform advocates of political violence — while a year later the paper published a column by an academic who had previously suggested there was nothing wrong with murdering conservatives and Republicans.[1]

Rubenstein observed in the essay that on January 6, 2021, few people at the Times remarked on the fact that liberals were cheering the deployment of National Guardsmen to stop rioting at the Capitol — the very thing Cotton had advocated months earlier.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] read the account as evidence of the transformation of the news media through what he called Neo-Marxist ideological subversion, describing it as the evolution of [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]] into a more dangerous form.[1] Where Mockingbird inserted conscious agents and contracted friendly assets into media organizations, Esler argued, decades of ideological subversion have now produced journalists who serve the same deep-state ends out of conviction rather than orders, including a growing cohort philosophically opposed to objective reality itself.[1]
