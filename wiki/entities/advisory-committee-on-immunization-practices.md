# Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP)

The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices is the federal body whose guidelines set routine U.S. vaccine recommendations, and whose revisions under the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration prompted countervailing state legislation in 2026.[1]

## 2026 recommendation changes and state response

ACIP recently reduced its routine vaccine recommendations from 17 diseases to 11.[1] In response, New York Governor [[kathy-hochul|Kathy Hochul]] signed a bill on May 15, 2026 requiring health insurers to cover all vaccines recommended by the New York State Commissioner of Health, explicitly decoupling state coverage policy from ACIP guidance.[1] A companion measure expanded pharmacist authority to administer COVID-19 vaccines to children ages 2 to 18, and the package preserved state authority over school immunization schedules and newborn vaccination protocols.[1]

## Court stay of the replacement panel, March 2026

In March 2026, a federal judge in Boston, Brian Murphy, stayed Health Secretary [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]]'s replacement of the committee's membership as part of a ruling halting portions of his broader vaccine policy overhaul.[2] The underlying lawsuit, brought by the [[american-academy-of-pediatrics|American Academy of Pediatrics]] and other medical organizations, targeted Kennedy's decision the previous year to remove all seventeen members of the committee and appoint new experts to the panel, which helps determine vaccine recommendations and the insurance coverage that follows.[2]

The judge's order stayed the new advisers' appointments and any votes on further policy changes, though the panel was still permitted to hold a meeting already scheduled for that week.[2] The suit also challenged steps taken by the [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|Centers for Disease Control and Prevention]] on January 5 that cut the list of routinely recommended childhood vaccines to eleven and lowered recommendation status for rotavirus, influenza, and hepatitis A.[2]

## June 2025 mass dismissal

In June 2025 Health and Human Services Secretary [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]] fired all 17 experts on the committee, announcing the decision in a *Wall Street Journal* op-ed arguing that "a clean sweep is needed to re-establish public confidence in vaccine science."[3] The removal drew immediate criticism from former White House COVID-19 response coordinator [[ashish-jha|Ashish Jha]], who called Kennedy's stated reasoning "a series of nonsense" and the move "a step in the wrong direction," while noting that the panel's members determine "what vaccines, if any, are going to be available to the American people."[3] Attention then turned to whom Kennedy would appoint in their place.[3]

### Replacement appointments and criticism

Commentary published in mid-June 2025 noted that most of the seventeen dismissed members had been appointed only the previous year to terms running through 2028, meaning Kennedy would have had to remove at least some of them to affect the panel's composition, and that President Joe Biden had set a clear precedent for removing supposedly bipartisan and nonpartisan advisory-board members.[4] Critics warned Kennedy that restoring public trust in federal public-health decisions required hiring "truly fair-minded, science-driven folks — not anti-vax crackpots," and argued that his agenda risked endangering the wider [[donald-trump|Trump]] reform drive.[4] The same commentary conceded that vaccine recommendations had plainly been politicized during the Biden years — alleging that [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|CDC]] and [[food-and-drug-administration|FDA]] officials covered up negative side effects of COVID-19 vaccines and prompted senior career officials to resign over findings that everyone should be vaccinated and revaccinated.[4] It assessed several of the eight new ACIP members Kennedy announced later that week as solid scientists, singling out biostatistician Martin Kulldorff, a co-author of the anti-lockdown [[great-barrington-declaration|Great Barrington Declaration]], and pediatrician Cody Meissner, who had previously served on the panel.[4]

## 2025 private coalition to bypass ACIP

Following Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s dismissal of all sitting ACIP members over conflict-of-interest concerns, pharmaceutical companies, pharmacists, and medical associations with longstanding ties to vaccine manufacturers including Merck, Sanofi, and Pfizer formed a private coalition aimed at bypassing the committee, according to Washington Post reporting summarized in June 2025.[5] The unnamed coalition, which also includes state health officials and a new advocacy group, is working with the University of Minnesota's Vaccine Integrity Project to develop a "nongovernmental vaccine system."[5] Its plan involves ordering vaccines directly from manufacturers and following recommendations issued by medical associations rather than ACIP.[5]
