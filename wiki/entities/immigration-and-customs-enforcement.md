# Immigration and Customs Enforcement

**Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE)** is the interior immigration-enforcement agency of the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]], and one of the principal beneficiaries of the June 2026 border-security funding package.[1]

## Funding and operations, 2026

The immigration and border-security legislation signed by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] provides tens of billions of dollars in additional funding for DHS, ICE, and [[customs-and-border-protection|Customs and Border Protection]] through the remainder of his term, with roughly $70 billion in additional immigration-enforcement funding allocated over three years.[1]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] reported that ICE and Homeland Security Investigations were surging into sanctuary jurisdictions as part of the campaign against [[child-trafficking|child-trafficking]] networks, and that new resources for child-exploitation investigators were being funded.[1] He characterised the administration's posture as treating "the heroes of ICE and Border Patrol as exactly that," while moving to locate missing children and prosecute the networks that exploited them.[1]

## Minneapolis shooting and backlash, January 2026

On January 7, 2026, federal immigration agents shot and killed 37-year-old Renee Nicole Good during an immigration enforcement operation in south Minneapolis, triggering protests the following day.[2] Demonstrators gathered near the Bishop Henry Whipple Federal Building, which houses ICE offices, where confrontations with law enforcement occurred; authorities reported the use of crowd-control measures including pepper spray and partial highway closures, though no arrests were announced by local officials.[2] Additional demonstrations and solidarity actions were reported in other US cities.[2] The [[fbi|Federal Bureau of Investigation]] assumed control of the shooting investigation, and Minnesota investigators claimed the FBI was blocking their access to information.[2]

Minnesota Governor [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]] issued Executive Order 26-01 placing the [[minnesota-national-guard|Minnesota National Guard]] on state active duty, and the order's text accused federal immigration officials of surging masked agents into the state, indiscriminately arresting people off the street, intimidating residents, and causing the loss of life.[2] Badlands commentators read the resulting protest wave as a manufactured "George Floyd 2.0" moment and a distraction from the [[minnesota-somali-fraud-hearings|Minnesota Somali fraud hearings]].[2]

## Operation Metro Surge and Senate scrutiny (2026)

ICE actions in Minnesota under [[operation-metro-surge|Operation Metro Surge]], conducted alongside [[customs-and-border-protection|CBP]], produced clashes in Minneapolis and St. Paul in which two U.S. citizen protesters — Renee Good on January 7, 2026, and Alex Pretti on January 24 — were fatally shot.[3] Homeland Security Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]] defended the operations before the [[senate-judiciary-committee|Senate Judiciary Committee]] on March 3, 2026, blaming "violent protesters," while Democrats accused officers of excessive force and objected to the victims being labeled "domestic terrorists."[3] Despite the bipartisan criticism directed at departmental leadership, immigration enforcement in the field continued unimpeded.[3]

## Minneapolis-St. Paul operations (January 2026)

In the Minneapolis–St. Paul area tensions surged over federal ICE deportation operations targeting criminal illegal aliens in the sanctuary-city region, exacerbated by the fatal shooting of Renee Good, who was killed by an ICE agent after allegedly attempting to run over a federal officer with her vehicle.[4] The incident sparked ongoing anti-ICE riots and protests, compounding an already volatile situation involving left-wing activism, potential organized pressure campaigns by nonprofits, and federal law enforcement responses.[4]

A notable flashpoint occurred when dozens of left-wing activists stormed [[cities-church|Cities Church]] in St. Paul during a Sunday service, apparently targeting a pastor believed to be linked to ICE's local field office operations.[4] The broader federal response included the deployment of 3,000 ICE agents to the area, with 1,500 troops on standby, amid warnings of escalating left-wing violence in Democrat-run cities.[4]

## January 2026 Minneapolis shooting

In mid-January 2026, days after the death of Renee Nicole Good — the 37-year-old fatally shot on January 7 while attempting to ram an ICE agent with her car — an ICE agent shot and wounded a Venezuelan national during a traffic stop in north [[minneapolis|Minneapolis]], according to federal officials.[5] Authorities said the man attacked the agent with a shovel and that the shooting was in self-defense; he was hospitalized and reported in stable condition.[5]

Protests continued in Minneapolis the following day and federal vehicles were looted during the unrest, prompting President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to say he was considering invoking the [[insurrection-act|Insurrection Act]] in response to the situation in Minnesota.[5] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the two shootings as establishing "the rules of the game" — that one cannot ram law enforcement with a car, and cannot attack officers with shovels — and argued that federal officers being attacked and federal property destroyed with state acquiescence met the definition of insurrection.[5]

## Minneapolis shooting and the January 2026 protest wave

In January 2026 an ICE agent in Minneapolis fatally shot [[renee-nicole-good|Renee Nicole Good]] during a confrontation in which she struck the agent with her car; ICE officials said the agent acted in self-defense, and video of the incident was reported to confirm the sequence of events.[6] The shooting triggered anti-ICE protests across the United States organized under the banner "ICE Out For Good" by [[indivisible|Indivisible]] and related groups, with organizers claiming more than 1,000 events nationwide and large gatherings in Washington, D.C., Philadelphia, and Minneapolis, where Representative [[ilhan-omar|Ilhan Omar]] joined thousands of demonstrators.[6] Roughly 500 people protested outside a Home Depot in Boca Raton, Florida, where one person was arrested on battery charges, and protests in Los Angeles, Austin, and Houston also produced arrests.[6]

Badlands commentary compared the mobilization to the 2020 George Floyd protests, characterizing the 2026 wave as a similar narrative operation but "more shrill and unhinged."[6]

## Operation Metro Surge (January 2026)

In early January 2026 the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] deployed approximately 2,000 federal agents from ICE, Customs and Border Protection and Homeland Security Investigations to Minneapolis and St. Paul under [[operation-metro-surge|Operation Metro Surge]], with President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] ordering an additional 1,000 CBP agents that would bring the total to roughly 3,000 personnel.[7] White House deputy chief of staff for policy [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] told Fox News that agents have federal immunity in the conduct of their duties and that anyone who lays a hand on them or obstructs them — including city or state officials — is committing a felony and could face prosecution for criminal conspiracy against the United States or against ICE officers.[7]

## Removal of Noncitizen Voters (2026)

In June 2026 the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] issued guidance directing ICE to open deportation proceedings against noncitizens who vote illegally in American elections or who falsely claim United States citizenship.[8] The directive authorizes ICE attorneys to seek removal on the strength of evidence of illegal voting or a false citizenship claim even before any criminal conviction is obtained, relying on grounds already contained in the [[immigration-and-nationality-act|Immigration and Nationality Act]].[8] Commentators at [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] treated the policy as a concrete first step toward election integrity, anticipating that left-leaning nongovernmental organizations would sue to block removals of noncitizens accused of diluting American votes.[8]

## Lakeville hotel service denial (January 2026)

In January 2026 agents from ICE and the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] conducting deportation operations in Minnesota had reservations at a Hampton Inn by Hilton in Lakeville canceled, with hotel staff citing the guests' "immigration work."[9] DHS condemned the cancellations on X as a "coordinated campaign" to impede law enforcement, and [[hilton-worldwide|Hilton]] subsequently terminated the property's franchise agreement after finding the franchisee was not meeting brand standards.[9]

## Denver Executive Order 152 and public opinion (2026)

On February 26, 2026, Denver Mayor [[mike-johnston|Mike Johnston]] signed [[denver-executive-order-152|Executive Order 152]], banning ICE and other federal immigration agents from using city-owned or city-controlled property for immigration enforcement operations without a judicial warrant, subpoena, or court order, and barring city agencies from sharing databases or technology with the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] absent legal compulsion.[10] The order also directed local police to protect peaceful protesters, identify ICE agents with body cameras when tactically safe, render aid to anyone harmed, and intervene in cases of excessive force, including potentially detaining federal agents; DHS called the order "legally illiterate."[10]

A [[reuters|Reuters]]/Ipsos nationwide survey published around the same period found that 60% of respondents believed ICE agents had overstepped in their enforcement actions, with high disapproval among Black and Hispanic respondents, while 63% of Democrats said ICE should be abolished, up from 44% in 2018, and 30% opposed abolition.[10] The same survey found 61% of U.S. adults supported the goal of deporting unauthorized immigrants.[10]

## New Jersey sanctuary litigation (February 2026)

In February 2026 the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] sued [[new-jersey|New Jersey]] and Gov. [[mikie-sherrill|Mikie Sherrill]] over [[new-jersey-executive-order-12|Executive Order No. 12]], which bars ICE from conducting secure arrests of criminal illegal aliens in nonpublic areas of state property, including correctional facilities.[11] Federal officials argued the order obstructs immigration enforcement and interferes with efforts to detain and remove individuals in custody, resulting in the release of removable individuals including those convicted of aggravated assault, burglary, and drug and human trafficking offenses.[11]

## Cannabis site operations and the Los Angeles response (July 2025)

In July 2025 ICE participated with Customs and Border Protection in coordinated operations at two licensed cannabis cultivation sites in Carpinteria and Camarillo, California, where about 200 suspected illegal aliens were detained and 10 minors were found.[12] The [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] said officers had to "dodge literal bullets from rioters" who appeared to have been forewarned of the operation, and described the children as rescued from potential exploitation, forced labor, and human trafficking.[12]

In response to the wider enforcement campaign, Los Angeles Mayor [[karen-bass|Karen Bass]] announced cash assistance for families affected by ICE raids and issued an executive order directing all city departments to refuse to assist the federal government with immigration enforcement, citing the "Angeleno card" model used by her predecessor [[eric-garcetti|Eric Garcetti]] during the pandemic.[12]

## Memphis Safe Task Force (September 2025)

Immigration and Customs Enforcement was named among the federal agencies composing the [[memphis-state-task-force|Memphis Safe Task Force]], established by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order of 15 September 2025 to address violent crime in [[memphis|Memphis]], Tennessee.[13] The task force, which also drew on the [[fbi|FBI]], the [[drug-enforcement-administration|DEA]] and the [[us-marshals-service|U.S. Marshals Service]], was described by Trump as a replica of his August 2025 crackdown in Washington, D.C.[13]

## GlobalX deportation flights

ICE contracted the airline [[globalx|GlobalX]] to deport alleged migrant gang members to El Salvador, including roughly 130 Venezuelan migrants flown on March 15, 2025.[14] A U.S. federal judge ruled on May 1 that the reasoning behind those deportations was illegal, and the hacker collective [[anonymous|Anonymous]] subsequently defaced a GlobalX subdomain in protest.[14]

## El Paso border riot detainers (2024)

After the [[texas-department-of-public-safety|Texas Department of Public Safety]] booked about 220 people on riot charges following a stampede that overwhelmed [[national-guard|National Guard]] troops along the Rio Grande, 39 were processed for release to ICE and remained jailed on immigration detainers, according to the El Paso County Sheriff's Office.[15] Court officials noted that undocumented migrants would stay jailed where a federal immigration hold blocked release, even after a magistrate judge ordered the riot defendants released on their own recognizance.[15] See [[el-paso-border-riot-case]].[15]

## Arrest of a Delta pilot at San Francisco (July 2025)

On Saturday, July 26, 2025, federal agents arrested a [[delta-air-lines|Delta Air Lines]] pilot aboard Delta Flight 2809 moments after it landed at San Francisco International Airport from Minneapolis at around 9:35 p.m.[16] Roughly ten federal agents, including officers from [[homeland-security-investigations|Homeland Security Investigations]], boarded the full aircraft before passengers could deplane; one passenger described how they "stormed the cockpit, cuffed the co-pilot, arrested him, walked him down the aisle, and ushered him off the plane."[16] The reason for the arrest remained unclear at the time of reporting.[16]


## IRS taxpayer data agreement (2025)

In April 2025 ICE concluded an [[irs-ice-taxpayer-data-sharing-agreement|agreement]] with the [[internal-revenue-service|Internal Revenue Service]] allowing immigration officials to request protected tax records in support of deportations, disclosed in a partially redacted court filing on Monday, April 7, 2025.[17] Under its terms ICE officials can ask the IRS for information about people who have been ordered to leave the United States or whom they are otherwise investigating, though the filings stated no information had yet changed hands.[17] Because federal law tightly controls taxpayer information — shielding home addresses and earnings even from other agencies — IRS officials warned during negotiations that the arrangement could be illegal, and several top officials, including the acting commissioner, prepared to resign over it.[17]

## Appearance in the 2025 DHS deportation video

A [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] video posted on September 23, 2025 to promote deportation efforts showed ICE officials detaining immigrants, intercut with comedian [[theo-von|Theo Von]] saying, "I heard you got deported, dude. Bye."[18] Von said he had not approved his inclusion and asked for the video's removal, and later reported receiving online harassment and an offer of security from a "high government official."[18]

## Exposure of undercover agents in San Jose, 2025

On January 26, 2025, [[kcbs-740-am|KCBS 740 AM]] — a radio station described as owned by [[george-soros|George Soros]] — publicly disclosed the locations of ICE agents operating undercover in San Jose, California.[19] Host [[bret-burkhart|Bret Burkhart]] detailed the types of unmarked vehicles used and the specific locations of agents, citing updates posted on social media by the [[rapid-response-network|Rapid Response Network]], an organization opposing deportation efforts.[19] Reporting noted that the broadcast might have endangered the agents' safety by potentially exposing their covert operations.[19]

## Detention of Mahmoud Khalil (March 2025)

ICE agents detained [[mahmoud-khalil|Mahmoud Khalil]], a pro-Palestinian activist and [[columbia-university|Columbia University]] graduate, on Saturday, March 8, 2025, prompting a federal judge to temporarily block his deportation while weighing the petition challenging the arrest.[20] Several hundred protesters massed in Lower Manhattan on the Monday to demand his release, with organizer Marian Osman saying he "was arrested for speaking out against genocide" and that "political speech is protected speech in this country."[20] After President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] posted that Khalil would not be the last pro-Palestinian demonstrator arrested, Osman said protesters would not be intimidated.[20] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the case should proceed through the courts to clarify whether First Amendment protections extend to green-card holders accused of advocating violence, citing deportability and inadmissibility provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1227 and § 1182 and the Smith Act at 18 U.S.C. § 2385.[20]

## Suspension of congressional facility access (2025)

In October 2025, federal immigration authorities said they no longer had to provide on-demand access to detention facilities for members of [[congress|Congress]], citing the government shutdown.[21] ICE officials, already contesting a lawsuit brought by Democratic lawmakers over previously denied visits, told lawmakers they lacked the staff or funding to support them, even though lawmakers had been legally permitted to demand such visits as part of oversight duties covering detention conditions and communication with detainees facing deportation.[21]

In court, ICE added that the shutdown had left no law on the books requiring the administration to accommodate lawmakers' visits, since that requirement had been contained in expired government funding laws; the agency told US District Judge [[jia-cobb|Jia Cobb]] it was funding operations through appropriations made in the [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill Act]], which imposes no congressional access requirement.[21] The episode is covered further at [[ice-congressional-access-suspension|ICE congressional access suspension]].[21]

## February 2024 budget shortfall and mass release proposal

In February 2024, ICE drafted plans to release thousands of immigrants and slash its detention capacity after the failure of a Senate border bill that would have erased a $700 million budget shortfall, according to four officials at ICE and the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]].[22] The [[bipartisan-border-bill-2024|bipartisan border bill]] that Republican lawmakers opposed would have provided $6 billion in supplemental funding for ICE enforcement operations.[22] Its demise led ICE officials to circulate an internal proposal to save money by releasing thousands of detainees and cutting detention levels from 38,000 beds to 22,000 — the opposite of the enforcement increases Republicans said they wanted.[22]

The budget crunch presented a difficult scenario for the Biden administration heading into the spring, when illegal crossings at the southern border were expected to spike again, and came days after House Republicans voted to impeach Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] over his border record, with immigration polling as President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s worst-rated issue.[22] Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the proposal as a counter-move in the "War of Stories" against the push for Ukraine proxy-war funding, describing the resulting dynamic as "Narrative Pincers."[22]

## Immediate impact of Trump's second-term orders (January 2025)

Within days of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s second inauguration, the posture of United States law enforcement toward illegal immigration changed sharply and ICE began enforcement operations, while local jurisdictions in some cases defied their states by refusing sanctuary status and cooperating with federal authorities.[23] The broader immigration system was inundated: thousands of migrants seeking asylum through the [[customs-and-border-protection|Customs and Border Protection]] mobile app had appointments cancelled, the humanitarian parole website for migrants from Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua and Venezuela went dark, refugee admissions were indefinitely halted, immigration authorities were newly permitted to conduct arrests in schools, churches and places of worship — reversing a policy that had long kept those sanctuaries off limits — and top immigration court officials were fired.[23] The [[pentagon|Pentagon]] announced military airlift support for [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]] removals of up to 5,000 detained aliens, following the deployment of at least 1,500 active-duty troops to the southern border.[23] Badlands contributor Ashe in America wrote that "criminal invaders are finally learning about consequences," citing Denver Mayor [[mike-johnston|Mike Johnston]]'s apparent backpedaling.[23]


## Denial of Mahmoud Khalil's Furlough Request (2025)

In April 2025 ICE denied a two-week furlough request from [[mahmoud-khalil|Mahmoud Khalil]], detained at its New Orleans field office, to attend the birth of his first child; field office director Melissa Harper rejected the request roughly 30 minutes after receiving it.[23] Khalil's wife, Dr. Noor Abdalla, gave birth alone and described the refusal as "a purposeful decision by ICE to make me, Mahmoud, and our son suffer."[23]

## June 2025 pause on select industry raids

In June 2025 the Trump administration directed ICE to pause raids against workers in the agriculture, hotel and restaurant industries, a shift in deportation policy the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] confirmed in a statement to NewsNation on Saturday, June 14 — days after President [[donald-trump|Trump]] signaled that "changes are coming" in a post on Truth Social.[24] Senator [[tom-cotton|Tom Cotton]] publicly objected the following day, telling CBS News's [[margaret-brennan|Margaret Brennan]] on *Face the Nation* that "I don't think we should pull back on any kind of enforcement at all" and that worksite enforcement in all industries needed to move forward with the support of political leadership.[24]

## MacArthur Park sweep (July 2025)

On Monday, July 7, 2025, dozens of federal immigration agents in tactical gear, accompanied by members of the California National Guard, were deployed to a mostly empty MacArthur Park in Los Angeles.[25] It was unclear whether anyone was taken into custody during the operation, which sparked outrage among local residents; an ICE spokesperson told CNN that the agency does not comment on ongoing operations.[25] Los Angeles Mayor [[karen-bass|Karen Bass]] called the sweep "outrageous" and "an attempt to spread fear," and said she went to the park to stop it, speaking by phone with a Customs and Border Protection official who told her the agents would leave within minutes.[25] The episode is treated in detail at [[macarthur-park-immigration-operation|the MacArthur Park immigration operation]].[25]

## Detention of a Los Angeles street vendor (2025)

In September 2025 a Los Angeles street vendor originally from Mexico, who came to the United States at age 5 and had held a green card for years, was reported to be in ICE detention after dropping his four children off at school, according to his wife and lawyer.[26] Newsweek said it had contacted ICE and the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] for comment.[26] The detention was described as part of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s continued illegal immigration crackdown, which critics called aggressive and in some cases arbitrary, particularly regarding immigrants living in the country for years with no record of violence or felonious activity.[26] While ICE and DHS faced increased scrutiny and backlash amid rising detentions and deportations, the White House remained consistent in saying that anyone living in the country unlawfully is considered a "criminal."[26] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] endorsed that framing, writing that no one is above the law and that objecting to a law is not a legal defense for breaking it and then evading detection for years.[26]

## Profiling allegations and Portland protests, October 2025

Illinois Governor [[jb-pritzker|JB Pritzker]] accused ICE of targeting individuals based on their "brown or black" skin color, a claim [[cnn|CNN]] anchor [[boris-sanchez|Boris Sanchez]] pressed on White House policy deputy [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] on October 6, 2025; Miller dismissed the question as "dumb" and rejected the allegation.[27] The same week, White House Press Secretary [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]] accused protesters outside a Portland, Oregon ICE facility of "inciting violence" while defending the administration's blocked [[national-guard|National Guard]] deployment.[27]
