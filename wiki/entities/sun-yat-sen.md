# Sun Yat-sen

Sun Yat-sen was a Chinese revolutionary leader and former president of China, cited in Badlands Media commentary as an early exponent of the moral framework underlying open-system political economy.[1] A physician by training, he led a revolutionary movement from 1893 and became the first president of the [[republic-of-china|Republic of China]] in 1912, remaining a beloved figure in both Taiwan and mainland China.[2]

## Early life and American connections

Sun's connections to the United States dated to his days as a student in Hawaii, and he studied in America from 1879 to 1883.[2] During those years he came to see in [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]]'s political economy the practical measures China needed to overcome a twofold crisis: outdated dynastic traditions from within and hostile manipulation by empires from without.[2] In the autumn of 1911 he was touring the United States and Canada lecturing and raising funds when news of the [[wuchang-uprising|Wuchang uprising]] broke; he cut the tour short in Colorado and returned home to oversee the dismantling of the [[qing-dynasty|Qing]] dynastic system.[2]

## Beyond left and right

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] stresses that Sun was neither a follower of [[karl-marx|Karl Marx]] nor a Bolshevik, though he was a revolutionary with no qualms about central planning and a healthy disdain for imperialism.[2] Equally, he was no proponent of the liberal theories of [[adam-smith|Adam Smith]], Malthus, Ricardo or [[john-stuart-mill|John Stuart Mill]], and rallied against what he considered the lies of British Free Trade used to justify the [[opium-wars|two opium wars]] of the previous century of humiliation.[2] Both a Christian and a Confucian scholar, he grounded himself instead in the moral philosophy of Lincoln's USA.[2]

He stated this explicitly in his 1904 pamphlet *A True Solution for the Chinese Question*, appealing to the American people for sympathy and support "because we intend to model our new government after yours; Above all, because you are the champion of liberty and democracy," and expressing hope of finding "many Lafayette's among you."[2]

## Three Principles of the People

In his [[three-principles-of-the-people|Three Principles of the People]], Sun articulated what [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] identifies as the principle of "Right makes Might" — an early formulation of [[win-win-cooperation|win-win cooperation]].[1] Writing in 1924, Sun is said to have understood, as Presidents [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] and [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] are argued to understand today, that a nation adhering to right-makes-might thinking will never lose the Mandate of Heaven, or Tianxia.[1]

The 1924 tract, transcribed from a lecture series, took its inspiration from the tricolon of Lincoln's [[gettysburg-address|Gettysburg Address]] and set out the principles of *minzu* ("national feelings of the people"), *minquan* ("rights of the people") and *minsheng* ("the people's livelihood").[2] In it Sun argued that "class war is not the cause of social progress, it is a disease developed in the course of social progress," and that Marx "can only be called a social pathologist; we cannot say that he is a social physiologist."[2]

## Political economy

Sun advocated the creation of a native manufacturing sector through protective tariffs and vast internal improvements in rail, roads, water projects and energy systems.[2] He compared a tariff to forts built at harbour entrances, protecting a nation's revenue and giving native industries a chance to develop.[2] He urged that "we must use the great power of the state and imitate the United States' methods," and argued that machine power could allow China to produce enough for eight hundred million people where human labor supported four hundred million.[2] He contrasted capitalism, which "makes profit its sole aim," with the Principle of Livelihood, which "makes the nurture of the people its aim."[2]

Writing after World War I amid rampant poverty and a shortage of national spirit, Sun despaired that "if China perishes, the guilt will be on our own heads and we shall be the world's great sinners."[2]

## Analysis of the British Empire

In his 1917 [[vital-problems-of-china|Vital Problems of China]], Sun set out a detailed critique of the [[british-empire|British Empire]]'s manipulation of China and the world, likening Britain's friends to silkworms destroyed once the silk has been drawn.[2] He argued that British policy for two centuries had been to attack the strongest enemy with the help of weaker countries — an outlook Ehret links to [[lord-palmerston|Lord Palmerston]]'s doctrine of permanent interests rather than permanent friends.[2]

## Infrastructure vision and later influence

In [[the-international-development-of-china|The International Development of China]] Sun forecast an interconnected Eurasian railway system and a US–Asia alliance, predicting that a developed China would become "another New World in the economic sense" and strengthen "the Brotherhood of Man."[2] He also warned that if a strong China chose to "crush other countries, copy the Powers' imperialism," it would merely follow in their tracks, and that only by resolving to "rescue the weak and lift up the fallen" would the nation prosper.[2]

Sun's early death in 1924 created a leadership vacuum within the [[kuomintang|Kuomintang]] that Ehret argues its corrupt and strategically incompetent leadership could not fill.[2] His vision of win-win cooperation was later revived by [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]] and by Vice President [[henry-wallace|Henry A. Wallace]], who in [[our-job-in-the-pacific|Our Job in the Pacific]] dated the awakening of the East to the 1911 revolution inspired by Sun's teachings.[2]

## Legacy in the multipolar framework

Ehret places Sun's principle alongside the [[peace-of-westphalia|Principle of Westphalia]] and the UN Charter as parallel Eastern and Western expressions of the same moral concept, contrasted with the closed-system, "might-makes-right" outlook he associates with [[thomas-malthus|Thomas Malthus]], [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] and [[al-gore|Al Gore]].[1] The lineage is invoked to argue that China's [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]] rests on a philosophical foundation predating the modern People's Republic.[1]

At an event commemorating the 150th anniversary of Sun's birth, [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] said the best way to commemorate him was "to learn from his noble character," praising his ardent love of country, his high degree of national self-esteem and confidence, and his tenacious and indomitable spirit through "forty years of effort to the national revolution."[2] Xi quoted Sun's insistence that "Chinese society is different from that of Western countries" and that "the power of China's development, when in our hands, shall ensure our survival; if entrusted to others, it shall be our downfall," noting that even on his deathbed "peace, struggle, and saving China" remained front and center in his mind.[2]

## The 1911 revolution and the International Development of China

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes Sun Yat-sen as a follower of [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] whose rearguard maneuver produced the surprise overthrow of the Manchu dynasty in 1911 and the institution of the Republic of China, with Sun as acting President — a development that partially checked the Anglo-American "open door" carve-up of China after the [[boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]].[3] Sun sided with [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]] and Lincoln against the followers of [[alfred-thayer-mahan|Alfred Thayer Mahan]] on the question of rail and industrial development, a position set out in his 1920 *International Development of China* program, which featured extensive engineering studies and financing assessments for rail, road, industrial corridors and ports.[3] The intrigues that sank the world into World War I made any early development of China impossible in Sun's lifetime.[3]
