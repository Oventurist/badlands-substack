# Sports Illustrated

Sports Illustrated is an American sports magazine whose near-total collapse in January 2024 is used by Badlands Media as the opening example of the mainstream media's demise — a case in which "the death certificates have already been signed."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm)

The magazine's publisher laid off all or most of its staff after the brand's owner, Authentic, was not paid its quarterly license fee, effectively shutting the operation down.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm) [[taylor-lorenz|Taylor Lorenz]] described it plainly in her February 2024 video: "Sports Illustrated basically shut down last week."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm)

Badlands places the collapse alongside cuts at the [[los-angeles-times|Los Angeles Times]], [[time-magazine|Time Magazine]], NBC News, National Geographic and Business Insider, arguing that "there are few industries more worthy of such a gruesome and undignified demise as modern mainstream journalism."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-swan-song-for-the-msm)

## AI-generated writers scandal (November 2023)

Two months before the shutdown, Futurism reported that Sports Illustrated had published articles credited to fake, AI-generated writers.[2] One byline, "Drew Ortiz," carried an author biography portraying an outdoorsman who spent his weekends camping, hiking, or visiting his parents' farm, yet outside the magazine Ortiz had no social media presence and no publishing history.[2] His profile photograph was found for sale on a site selling AI-generated headshots, where the image was catalogued as a "neutral white young-adult male with short brown hair and blue eyes."[2] A person involved in producing the content, speaking anonymously to avoid professional repercussions, said Ortiz was not the only AI-generated author published by the magazine.[2]

The magazine's parent, [[the-arena-group|The Arena Group]], initially blamed third-party vendors while denying AI content was involved — a defense that, as [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] noted, revealed that Sports Illustrated and The Street content had been licensed out rather than produced in house, and which collapsed further once the AI-generated headshots were traced to a commercial database.[2] The commentary framed the affair as one of the most "comically-dystopian" stories of the week and as an education for the American public in how much Establishment output is fake.[2]
