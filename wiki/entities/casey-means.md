# Casey Means

Casey Means is an American physician and wellness entrepreneur who was nominated by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in May 2025 to serve as Surgeon General of the United States.[1] Her nomination followed the withdrawal of Janette Nesheiwat, whose candidacy collapsed over allegations that she had inflated her credentials by claiming a degree from the University of Arkansas School of Medicine when she had in fact graduated from a medical school in the Caribbean and completed her residency in Arkansas.[1]

## Background and credentials

Means graduated from Stanford medical school but left her surgical residency in head and neck surgery during her fifth year in order to practice functional medicine instead.[1] That departure from conventional clinical training became the central point of contention in the debate over her fitness for the Surgeon General post, with critics describing her as a "wellness influencer" lacking experience in public health administration.[1]

## Nomination controversy

The nomination generated significant backlash, including from within the [[make-america-healthy-again|Make America Healthy Again]] movement itself, whose adherents objected that Means had not completed a residency and was not a practicing clinical physician.[1] Health and Human Services Secretary [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]] publicly defended the nomination on social media, arguing that the reaction "reveal[s] just how far off course our healthcare conversations have veered," and that Means was the ideal choice precisely because she had left the traditional medical system rather than in spite of that fact.[1]

Badlands commentary framed the episode as an irony of movement politics: figures who had built their worldview on rejecting the established system were appealing to that same system's credentialing standards to disqualify her.[1] Kennedy's former running mate [[nicole-shanahan|Nicole Shanahan]] went further, implying that Kennedy was either lying or being handled, a claim Kennedy was asked about in an interview.[1] Badlands writers argued that MAHA's stated problem — a "bundle of perverse incentives" — is a business transformation challenge rather than a medical one, and therefore requires a leader with a stomach for institutional change rather than a credentialed clinician occupying a bureaucratic seat.[1]
