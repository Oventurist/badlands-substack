# C. H. Douglas

Major C. H. Douglas was the British engineer who devised the Social Credit system after the First World War, and the founding theorist of the [[social-credit-party-of-canada|Social Credit Party]] that [[joshua-haldeman|Joshua Haldeman]] joined in 1943.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes him as a mysterious figure whose economics were obscure and whose politics were openly sympathetic to Nazism.[1]

## Social Credit theory

Douglas argued that too many workers were under-employed and that prices for goods were overvalued because low wages suppressed demand, and concluded that credits should be issued to all citizens as a form of universal basic income enabling them to purchase the products of the firms employing them.[1] Critics complained that the theory lacked applicability in any viable sovereign nation.[1] Ehret notes that while Douglas, like [[howard-scott|Howard Scott]], promoted the rhetoric of infinite abundance, he rejected all national involvement in the economy — the very instrument that might have realized his ambition — and instead promoted a variation of libertarianism fusing state welfare with the devolution of power to trade guilds.[1]

The name "Social Credit" was not Douglas's coinage but that of his mentor [[alfred-richard-orage|Alfred Richard Orage]], the Theosophist and Fabian associate who published one of Douglas's first works, "A Mechanical View of Economics," in his New Age magazine in 1919.[1]

Douglas was also a loud opponent of industrial civilization who demanded a return to small-scale farming and production, writing that "we are hypnotised by the propaganda of the international chemical combines into the belief that soil analysis, chemical fertilisers, and oil-driven farm machinery are far superior, and more 'scientific' than the intimate farming of the older order," and claiming there was "not a particle of genuine evidence" for that belief.[1] Ehret observes that this ignored the collapse in population levels such a reversion would entail.[1]

## Letter to Hitler

In May 1939 Douglas wrote directly to Hitler, celebrating the Nazi war on Jewish bankers.[1] Addressing the "Herr Fuehrer," he argued that there was in fact no conflict of ideologies between the democratic and totalitarian powers, since both proceeded from the assumption that full employment is the test of success and differed only in method; that the arms production forced by this assumption was "the ultimate example of waste and inefficiency"; and that the employment policy in question was "inseparable from the Jewish Financial System."[1] He urged Hitler to use the crisis to force an exposure of that policy, predicting that "not President Roosevelt, but yourself would be recognised as the representative of all those values which are cherished equally in the so-called democracies and their artificially created antagonists."[1]

Ehret notes that, much like the Fabian economist John Maynard Keynes — who cited Douglas in his General Theory — Douglas believed Hitler's regime was well suited to apply Social Credit as the basis of the post-war age.[1] Given that Hitler and the leading members of the SS were, in Ehret's account, occultists and radical ecologists committed to "back to nature" communalist feudal living, he argues it is unsurprising that Douglas expected his ideas to resonate with the Führer.[1]

## Legacy

Under Douglas's lead, Ehret writes, the Social Credit movement redirected popular conspiracy analysis away from royal bloodlines and esoteric mystery cults — networks with which he alleges both Douglas and Scott were closely affiliated — and toward a blanket blame on "Jew Bankers," culminating in the party's distribution of the [[protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion|Protocols of the Learned Elders of Zion]] in Canada.[1]
