# Matthew Alan Livelsberger

Matthew Alan Livelsberger, 37, of Colorado, was identified by law enforcement as the driver who detonated a Tesla Cybertruck outside a Las Vegas hotel on New Year's Day 2025, an event covered in the Badlands News Brief as the [[las-vegas-cybertruck-explosion|Las Vegas Cybertruck explosion]].[1] He was a five-time Bronze Star recipient and a new father, a biography that officials themselves described as difficult to reconcile with a suicidal New Year's Day attack.[1]

## Writings and stated motives

Law enforcement officials said on the Friday after the blast that Livelsberger had written of "political grievances," of armed conflicts abroad, and of domestic issues in the days before the explosion.[1] Police released two letters attributed to him. In the first — more than 400 words — he framed the incident not as a "terrorist attack" but as "a wake-up call," arguing that "Americans only pay attention to spectacles and violence" and that "fireworks and explosives" were the best available means of getting his point across.[1] A second, shorter letter of fewer than 125 words called for action to remove Democrats from both the federal government and the military, floated the occupation of roads and federal buildings, and did not rule out the use of force.[1]

Investigators recovered the writings from Livelsberger's cell phone alongside a ten-day "journal of activity," described also as a surveillance log, running from December 21 to December 31 and chronicling his purchases of firearms, camping equipment, and other items.[1]

## Background and mental health

An ex-girlfriend, Alicia Artist, who dated Livelsberger from 2018 to 2019 and again from 2020 to roughly 2022, told CNN he had been depressed during their relationship.[1] US officials familiar with his military records said he had been diagnosed with depression in the previous year but had not been assessed as a risk of violence or suicide.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] declined to treat the case as a forensic puzzle, advising readers to stop trying to establish "what happened" and instead to ask why the parties disseminating the narrative wanted the public to believe it happened, whether or not it actually did — casting the first approach as producing "a highly informed normie" and the second as actual thinking.[1]
