# Church Committee

The **Church Committee** was the 1975 congressional committee, led by Senator Frank Church, that investigated abuses by U.S. intelligence agencies.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## Findings on media control

According to *America Has a CIA-Run Economy*, the Church Committee discovered in 1975 that the [[cia|CIA]] had manipulated and controlled American media since 1960, and possibly earlier.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy) The committee grilled CIA Director William Colby under oath, and he revealed the existence of [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]] — the program under which the CIA maintained assets working inside various media companies.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] uses this history as the foundation for his argument that when the internet broke the CIA's grip on legacy media in the 1990s, the agency responded by creating and controlling search engines and social media platforms instead.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## Findings on assassination, coups and mind control

The August 2023 Badlands article "Clowns in America" describes the committee's year-long investigation of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] as the moment the agency's "dirty laundry" was exposed to the world, leaving it at the lowest point in its history with its very existence in question.[2] Senators uncovered assassination plots, secret wars, antidemocratic coups and mind-control experiments using LSD on unwitting human subjects — activities hidden from both Congress and the American people.[2]

## Bush and the turn to reform

President [[gerald-ford|Gerald Ford]] nominated [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] as Director of Central Intelligence in 1976 in an attempt to save the agency, and Bush spent much of his eleven-month tenure battling the senators concluding the investigation.[2] Bush decried the revelations rather than the facts revealed, arguing they had "devastated the morale of perhaps the finest group of public servants this country has."[2] Badlands writer [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argued that Bush, a former congressman, helped persuade Congress to answer the investigations with CIA reforms rather than criminal charges, and observed that the agency never ceased any of the practices it had been investigated for — an outcome he links to the growth of a [[shadow-government|shadow government]].[2]

## Formation and Colby's testimony

The committee was formed in 1975 and headed by Democratic Congressman [[frank-church|Frank Church]] of Idaho, assembled to look into a CIA that many believed had become out of control, wielding far too much power and using it in ways never intended when the agency was created in 1947.[3] [[william-colby|William Colby]], appointed director by [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]] in 1973, was interviewed under oath multiple times and divulged a great deal of hidden information about the agency's operations that it did not want exposed.[3] The CIA concluded that Colby had not fought hard enough to keep its secrets from Congress, viewed him as a traitor, and determined that the next director would have to be a loyalist — [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]], installed in 1976.[3]

What the sessions exposed included [[mk-ultra|MK ULTRA]], a CIA-run mind control operation that created unwitting assassins through drugs, torture and other psychological techniques; CIA surveillance of American politicians and civil rights leaders; assassinations of foreign leaders, the techniques used to kill them, and operations to overthrow foreign governments.[3] Perhaps most surprising was [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]], a program to control not only foreign media but the American press.[3] Badlands contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] notes that although the committee established CIA media manipulation as fact in 1975, outlets such as [[abc-news|ABC News]] still dismissed [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|RFK Jr.]]'s 2024 Mockingbird allegations as "bonkers."[3]

Badlands Media pairs the Church Committee with the [[pike-committee|Pike Committee]] as the two 1960s-70s investigations that exposed the intelligence community's "dirty laundry" to the public for the first time — assassination plots, human experimentation, domestic surveillance, mail interception, propaganda dissemination, media manipulation and inhumane covert operations.[4] The article argues that because the exposure made the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] radioactive in the eyes of foreign governments while its functions were still considered essential to Western hegemony, the response was the construction of the [[plausible-deniability-network|plausible deniability network]] of NGO cut-outs headed by the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]].[4]

## Formal name and origins

The committee's formal name was the Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities; "Church Committee" was the colloquial shorthand.[6] The year 1975 was dubbed the "Year of Intelligence" and gave the public its first real glimpse behind the curtain at what Badlands Media describes as a "silent war" — a relentless but furtive war on the privacy of American citizens waged by the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]], the [[fbi|FBI]] and the [[national-security-agency|NSA]].[6]

The committee arose directly out of the 1973 [[watergate|Senate Watergate Committee]] investigation, which revealed that the executive branch had directed national intelligence agencies to carry out constitutionally questionable domestic security operations.[6] In 1974 journalist [[seymour-hersh|Seymour Hersh]] published a New York Times article claiming the CIA had been spying on anti-war activists for more than a decade in violation of its charter, prompting former CIA officials and lawmakers including Senators William Proxmire and Stuart Symington to call for a congressional inquiry.[6] In January 1975 Senator [[john-pastore|John Pastore]] introduced a resolution to establish a select committee to determine "the extent, if any, to which illegal, improper, or unethical activities were engaged in by any agency of the Federal Government"; the [[united-states-senate|Senate]] approved it 82–4.[6]

Majority Leader [[mike-mansfield|Mike Mansfield]] cautioned the Senate "against letting the affair become a television extravaganza," and with Republican Leader [[hugh-scott|Hugh Scott]] carefully selected members balancing experienced lawmakers with junior members across a variety of political viewpoints.[6] After Philip Hart declined the chairmanship for health reasons, Mansfield selected [[frank-church|Frank Church]] of Idaho, then a 16-year member of the Committee on Foreign Relations who had previously co-chaired a special committee examining the executive branch's Cold War consolidation of power.[6] Republican [[john-tower|John Tower]] of Texas, a member of the Armed Services Committee, was selected as vice-chairman.[6]

## Hearings and public relations

Most hearings were held in closed executive session in order to "protect intelligence sources and methods," but the committee held a series of public hearings in September and October 1975 to educate the American public about the "unlawful or improper conduct" of the intelligence community, highlighting a few carefully selected examples.[6] Those deemed acceptable for public consumption included a CIA "biological agents program," a [[white-house|White House]] "domestic surveillance program," [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]] intelligence activities, and the FBI's program to disrupt the civil rights and anti-Vietnam War movements — the first time the American public was given an official opportunity to learn about decades of secret operations.[6] Chief Counsel [[frederick-schwarz|Frederick Schwarz]] recounted that at the outset of the investigations the NSA's general counsel told him, "The constitution does not apply to the National Security Agency."[6]

Criticism followed: some dismissed the inquiry as a vehicle for Church's 1976 presidential bid, which he declared in March 1976, while others argued the congressional inquiries had undermined the security and credibility of U.S. intelligence agencies.[6] The Christmas Day 1975 assassination of [[richard-welch|Richard Welch]], the CIA station chief in Greece, outside his Athens home diverted public attention from intelligence abuses and generated sympathy for the intelligence community.[6] Church nonetheless remained resolute in his belief in "the right of the public to know what the instrumentalities of their government have done."[6]

## The investigations

The committee's task — investigating the nation's most secret agencies and writing a detailed report with legislative recommendations — was originally expected to take one year but was extended to 16 months.[6] Church and Tower met with President [[gerald-ford|Gerald R. Ford]] and his top national security advisors to secure a pledge of White House cooperation; investigators did not always receive documents promptly but enjoyed seemingly unprecedented access to never-before-public materials, though whether those materials were pre-screened is unknown.[6] The best known internal report was the CIA's "[[family-jewels|Family Jewels]]," outlining misdeeds dating back to the Eisenhower administration, which along with reports from other agencies served as road maps for a peak staff of 150.[6]

Investigators identified previously unknown programs including the NSA's [[project-shamrock|Project SHAMROCK]] and [[project-minaret|Project MINARET]], which monitored wire communications to and from the United States and shared some of that data with other intelligence agencies.[6] The committee also investigated the FBI's [[cointelpro|COINTELPRO]], described as "covert action designed to disrupt and discredit the activities of groups and individuals deemed a threat to the social order," which targeted the National Lawyers Guild, the American Indian Movement, the [[black-panther-party|Black Panthers]], the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, the anti-Vietnam War movement, and individuals such as [[martin-luther-king-jr|Martin Luther King, Jr.]], along with local, state and federal elected officials.[6]

## Final report and legacy

After 126 full committee meetings, 40 subcommittee hearings, roughly 800 witness interviews in public and closed session, and 110,000 documents reviewed, the committee published its final report on April 29, 1976.[6] It found that from Franklin Roosevelt's administration through the early 1970s, "intelligence excesses, at home and abroad," were not the "product of any single party, administration, or man" but had developed as America rose to superpower status during the Cold War, and concluded that "Intelligence agencies have undermined the constitutional rights of citizens... primarily because checks and balances designed by the framers of the Constitution to assure accountability have not been applied."[6] In an appended separate view, Tower acknowledged the excesses and the need for expanded legislative, executive and judicial involvement, but cautioned Congress against "unnecessarily" restraining presidential discretion in national security.[6]

The report contained more than 90 legislative and regulatory recommendations designed to "place intelligence activities within the constitutional scheme for controlling government power," observing that "there is no inherent constitutional authority for the President or any intelligence agency to violate the law."[6] Badlands Media argues the resulting reforms were far from comprehensive, and that nearly 50 years later the intelligence community has taken its misconduct to new levels — the reason alternative media and conservative commentators called for a new "Church-style" body, realized in the [[select-subcommittee-on-the-weaponization-of-the-federal-government|Select Subcommittee on the Weaponization of the Federal Government]] chaired by [[jim-jordan|Jim Jordan]].[6]

## Legacy in the information-operations debate

Badlands Media's "The Information Imperium" cites the Church Committee twice: as the investigation whose exposure of [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] media manipulation destroyed the agency's credibility by the 1970s and created the need for a successor vehicle in the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]], and as the body that flagged [[usaid|USAID]] as a CIA front used for Vietnam-era intelligence work.[7] Both references anchor the article's claim that today's overt "democracy promotion" funding is the institutional descendant of operations the committee once exposed.[7]
