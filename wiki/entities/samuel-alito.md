# Samuel Alito

Samuel Alito is an Associate Justice of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] and the lead dissenter in the Court's June 2026 ruling on late-arriving absentee ballots.[1]

## Dissent in the Mississippi mail ballot case

Alito dissented from the 5-4 majority opinion authored by Justice [[amy-coney-barrett|Amy Coney Barrett]], warning that the ruling "leaves open opportunities for voter fraud" and could weaken public trust in elections.[1] He cited a 2005 bipartisan commission led by Jimmy Carter and James Baker that identified absentee voting as a major potential fraud risk, as well as a 2008 opinion by Justice John Paul Stevens acknowledging the real risk of absentee ballot fraud.[1]

Alito further wrote that the decision "opens Pandora's box," creating uncertainty over whether mailed ballots are ever final and over how states should regulate ballot handling under election-day rules.[1]

## Birthright citizenship oral argument

At the April 1, 2026 oral argument in [[trump-v-barbara|Trump v. Barbara]], the challenge to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order on [[birthright-citizenship|birthright citizenship]], Alito pressed a hypothetical about "a boy born here to an Iranian father who has entered the country illegally," reasoning that the child would automatically be an Iranian national with a duty of military service to Iran.[2] He asked whether such a child is "not subject to any foreign power," and extended the point to children born in the United States to Russian and Mexican parents, concluding: "It sure seems like that's a — that makes them subject to a foreign power."[2] The exchange went to the meaning of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]]'s "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" clause.[2]

## Louisiana v. Callais

Alito wrote the majority opinion in [[louisiana-v-callais|Louisiana v. Callais]], decided in April 2026, in which the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that [[louisiana|Louisiana]]'s congressional map creating a second majority-Black district was an unconstitutional [[racial-gerrymandering|racial gerrymander]].[3] He held that compliance with Section 2 of the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]] can justify consideration of race but does not require states to adopt maps that prioritize racial outcomes.[3] Alito wrote that Section 2, "correctly understood," does not impose liability that conflicts with the Constitution and did not mandate Louisiana's redraw.[3] The opinion left Section 2 in place while narrowing its application in future [[2026-redistricting-battle|redistricting]] disputes; Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] concurred separately and would have gone further.[3]

## Clerk job-reference controversy (2025)

In January 2025, left-wing media reported that Alito had provided a job reference on behalf of one of his former clerks to President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[4] Alito told ABC News: "William Levi, one of my former law clerks, asked me to take a call from President-elect Trump regarding his qualifications to serve in a government position. I agreed to discuss this matter with President-elect Trump."[4] CNN, Politico, CBS News, Rolling Stone, the Associated Press, The New York Times, NBC News and other outlets amplified the report.[4]

Critics of the coverage noted that federal judges and Supreme Court justices provide references for former clerks regularly, and that ethics guidelines for federal judges acknowledge this reality in the Published Ethics Advisory Opinions, which state that "there would be no impropriety in a judge answering an inquiry from a screening committee or appointing authority with respect to the judge's knowledge concerning the qualifications and other relevant factors of a nominee for appointment to any public office."[4] The Federalist argued that a reader would not learn that judicial ethics codes approve of job references from Adam Liptak's conspiratorial essay in The New York Times.[4]

## Dissent in the Chicago National Guard case

In December 2025 Alito dissented from a Supreme Court order temporarily blocking President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from deploying the [[national-guard|National Guard]] in Chicago, calling the ruling "unwise," "imprudent" and "puzzling" in a scathing three-page dissent.[5] He accused the six-justice majority of failing to give sufficient deference to Trump, who said the deployment was needed to protect federal immigration officers sweeping the city for illegal migrants as part of [[operation-midway-blitz|Operation Midway Blitz]].[5] The majority had held that the government failed to identify a source of authority permitting the military to execute the laws in Illinois and that the President had not invoked any statutory exception to the [[posse-comitatus-act|Posse Comitatus Act]].[5]

Alito was joined in dissent by Justices [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] and [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]].[5] "Whatever one may think about the current administration's enforcement of the immigration laws or the way ICE has conducted its operations, the protection of federal officers from potentially lethal attacks should not be thwarted," Alito wrote.[5]

## Easter 2025 dissent in the Alien Enemies Act case

Alito, joined by Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]], dissented from the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court's]] April 19, 2025 order barring the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration from removing Venezuelan detainees under the [[alien-enemies-act|Alien Enemies Act]].[6] Writing on Easter, he objected that "literally in the middle of the night, the Court issued unprecedented and legally questionable relief without giving the lower courts a chance to rule, without hearing from the opposing party, within eight hours of receiving the application, with dubious factual support for its order, and without providing any explanation for its order."[6] The dissent was widely cited by critics who argued the [[american-civil-liberties-union|ACLU]] had been rewarded for bypassing ordinary appellate procedure.[6]

## Immutability questioning in the Tennessee gender-care case (December 2024)

During the December 4, 2024 oral arguments in the challenge to Tennessee's ban on gender-affirming care for minors, Alito was among several conservative justices who focused on "detransitioners" — people who regret gender-affirming treatments received earlier in life — while expressing skepticism that transgender Americans should receive heightened legal protection.[7] He was particularly interested in whether transgender status is "immutable," a quality the Court has historically treated as a key characteristic of groups deserving greater protection.[7]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the line of questioning a "legal chess move" that could reshape the transgender debate and reinforce guardrails around protected status, reasoning that if self-identification is fluid and changeable it cannot be immutable, and therefore should not generate special protection.[7] See [[united-states-v-skrmetti]].[7]
