# Henry C. Carey

Henry C. Carey (1793–1879) was an American economist who served as economic advisor to [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] and is described as a champion of American industrial growth and a leading anti-Malthusian thinker.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presents Carey as the clearest American enunciator of the principle of self-perfectibility operating simultaneously in science, technology and statecraft.[1]

## Refutation of the "law of diminishing returns"

Carey refuted what Ehret calls the "dismal science" of the [[british-east-india-company|British East India Company]] economists [[john-stuart-mill|J. S. Mill]] and [[david-ricardo|David Ricardo]], who had advanced the pseudo-scientific [[law-of-diminishing-returns|law of diminishing returns]].[1] That supposed law presumed a deterministic devaluation of land over time as rents rose under a "law of exploitation" of the unfit by the "more fit".[1]

In his 1872 work *Unity of Law*, Carey wrote that [[thomas-malthus|Malthus]] had assumed that cultivation always began on the richest soils and that population growth forced recourse to inferior soils with steadily diminishing returns to labor; Ricardo then assumed that diminishing production must be attended by the landlord's power to take an ever larger share of a shrinking product, leaving the laborer a steadily diminishing share of a constantly declining quantity — so that the growing inequality of the people of England was "thus proved to be the result of a great law established by the Creator for government of the human race."[1] Carey noted that Mill, having assumed a tendency toward the subjection of the masses as a necessary result of divine institutions, called the law of constantly decreasing productiveness of agricultural labor "the most important proposition in political economy".[1]

## Positive doctrine

Against these closed-system premises, Carey demonstrated that technological progress caused unproductive lands to become more productive over time, and argued that the power to support life increased rather than diminished, with increased returns to all parties in a non-zero-sum system of mutual cooperation.[1] He focused on the ratio of human mentation to the forces of nature as a reciprocal interaction over time, establishing a coherence between humanity and the discovered laws of creation in the interplay of the "subjective" forces of mind and the "objective" forces of natural law.[1]

Carey wrote that the more perfect the power of self-direction, "the greater is the tendency towards increased control of mind over matter; the wretched slave to nature gradually yielding place to the master of nature, in whom the feeling of responsibility to his family, his country, his Creator and himself, grows with the growth of power to guide and direct the vast and various forces placed at his command."[1]

## Legacy in the argument against transhumanism

Ehret situates Carey alongside [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] as representatives of an American anti-Malthusian heritage whose claims are vindicated by the material record: humanity rose from roughly one billion people in 1800 to nearly eight billion today, and US life expectancy rose from about 40 years in 1800 to 78 years.[1] The closed-system theories Carey opposed are presented as the basis both of Marx and Engels' theory of class struggle — which ignored the anti-imperial economic school then active in the United States — and of the [[club-of-rome|Club of Rome]]'s 1968 neo-Malthusian revival, later absorbed into the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] and its [[transhumanism|transhumanist]] creed.[1]
