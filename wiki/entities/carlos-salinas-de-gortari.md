# Carlos Salinas de Gortari

Carlos Salinas de Gortari was the 60th president of Mexico, serving from 1988 to December 1994, and the leader of the country's long-governing [[institutional-revolutionary-party|Institutional Revolutionary Party]] (PRI).[1] He was one of the three heads of government who signed the North American Free Trade Agreement, alongside U.S. President [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] and Canadian Prime Minister [[brian-mulroney|Brian Mulroney]].[1] In Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]]'s account, Salinas is not merely a partner in a trade negotiation but a president "installed" by a Bush-directed [[shadow-government|shadow government]] for the purpose of opening the southern border to narcotics.[1]

## The 1988 election

Salinas was elected in 1988, the same year Bush was elected president of the United States — a simultaneity Lange treats as more than coincidence.[1] The vote itself is described as fraudulent: according to a 2004 New York Times report on the memoirs of outgoing president [[miguel-de-la-madrid|Miguel de la Madrid]], initial results from areas around the capital showed Salinas losing badly to opposition leader Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas, whereupon the government announced that the vote-tabulating computer system had crashed and subsequently declared Salinas the winner.[1] Three years later, in an alliance between the PRI and the conservative National Action Party, the Mexican Congress ordered the 1988 ballots burned, destroying the only hard evidence of the fraud.[1] Lange attributes the durability of Mexico's one-party system to long-standing [[cia|CIA]] control of the Mexican government, noting that presidents served single six-year terms and that the outgoing president hand-picked his successor until Vicente Fox broke roughly seventy years of PRI rule.[1] See [[1988-mexican-presidential-election]].

## Privatization

Salinas oversaw the largest [[privatization-in-mexico|privatization]] program in Mexican history, which the article ties directly to NAFTA.[1] By 1993 the government had sold or dissolved more than 80 percent of the 1,155 businesses it once ran, in what the New York Times called one of the most sweeping sales of state companies in the world.[1] Savings on debt payments allowed Mexico to spend more on social programs and to press ahead with bolder reforms including the NAFTA negotiation, but the program also cost more than 400,000 jobs since 1983 and did not deliver greater efficiency, competition or a better life for ordinary Mexicans; new private owners proved as unresponsive as the old bureaucrats.[1] The columnist Alvaro Cepeda Neri wrote in La Jornada that "the booty of privatization has made multimillionaires of 13 families, while the rest of the population — some 80 million Mexicans — has been subjected to the same gradual impoverishment as though they had suffered through a war."[1]

In a November 1994 valedictory speech Salinas listed his accomplishments: a budget deficit exceeding 12 percent of GDP in 1988 turned to slight surplus, inflation cut from an average of 108 percent in 1988 to around 7 percent for 1994, and banks nationalized in 1982 privatized again with nearly 50 new foreign-currency financial institutions licensed.[1] He said far less about Mexico's growing divisions: where Mexico had just two billionaires in 1991, 24 individuals or families appeared on the Forbes billionaires list during his regime and at least four more were identified afterwards, a total of 28.[1] Lange calls this "the rise of the Mexican robber barons."[1]

## Carlos Slim and Telmex

The most prominent beneficiary was [[carlos-slim|Carlos Slim Helú]], described by Wired as a friend of Salinas who "never seemed to make a wrong move" during the six years of his presidency.[1] Slim took control of the national telephone monopoly [[telmex|Telmex]] with a $1.76 billion bid for 20.4 percent of its stock in 1990; just after the bid was accepted the government authorized a 40 percent rate increase for local service, and Salinas's policies guaranteed Slim a private monopoly on long-distance service until 1997.[1] Slim had been a member of the Salinas election committee during the 1988 campaign and was, in Lange's phrasing, "handsomely rewarded" afterward.[1]

## Flight and legacy

Salinas left office in December 1994 and went into self-imposed exile in Ireland, according to Frontline fearing he might be charged with the murder of his chosen successor Luis Donaldo Colosio.[1] Lange notes that Ireland placed him beyond extradition.[1] Frontline describes him as extremely unpopular in Mexico, blamed for a decreased standard of living, economic difficulties, the rise of the drug trade and the massive corruption of his administration, with many Mexicans blaming NAFTA for the country's economic problems.[1] A second reason for his flight, the article argues, was the prosecution of his elder brother [[raul-salinas-de-gortari|Raúl Salinas de Gortari]], who was convicted of murder and imprisoned.[1]

Former DEA official Michael Levine described the Mexican drug cartel as a "family affair" in which both Carlos and Raúl were prominent members — knowledge that reached U.S. Attorney General Edwin Meese in 1987, a year before Salinas's inauguration.[1] Swiss investigators concluded that Raúl channelled drug money into his brother's presidential campaign.[1] The Bush administration, on this account, was fully aware of the Salinas presidency's links to organized crime but kept the public uninformed so as not to jeopardize NAFTA; Phil Jordan, who headed the [[drug-enforcement-administration|DEA]]'s Dallas office from 1984 to 1994, said "we were under instructions not to say anything negative about Mexico. It was a no-no since NAFTA was a hot political football."[1]
