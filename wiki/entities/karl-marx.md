# Karl Marx

Karl Marx was the German philosopher whose name is attached to the *Communist Manifesto* and to the doctrine of [[communism|Communism]]. He is quoted for the line "Religion is the opiate of the people," from his *Introduction to Critique of the Hegelian Philosophy of Right* (1844).[1]

## Claimed relationship to the Illuminati lineage

In the Badlands Media account by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], Marx was not the true progenitor of the ideas put forth in the *Communist Manifesto*. Instead he was paid and directed by a group called the [[league-of-the-just|League of the Just]], known in Germany as the Bund der Gerechten, or simply the Bund — itself a secret society that later became known as the International Communist Party and that DeLarme describes as either the spiritual successor of, or a direct collaborator with, the [[bavarian-illuminati|Bavarian Illuminati]].[1]

Marx is also cited as an example of a broader pattern: rebellion against the Abrahamic religions is described as a common theme among prominent ex-[[jesuits|Jesuits]], with [[adam-weishaupt|Adam Weishaupt]] and Marx given as parallel cases.[1]

## Sun Yat-sen's critique

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] emphasizes that [[sun-yat-sen|Sun Yat-sen]] — revered by the [[chinese-communist-party|Chinese Communist Party]] as the forebearer of its revolutionary legacy — took direct aim at Marx, whose theories were rapidly permeating the young Chinese intelligentsia in the early 20th century.[2] In [[three-principles-of-the-people|Three Principles of the People]] Sun argued that "society progresses through the adjustment of major economic interests rather than through the clash of interests," rejecting class struggle as the engine of history.[2]

Sun concluded that "class war is not the cause of social progress, it is a disease developed in the course of social progress," and that "Marx can only be called a social pathologist; we cannot say that he is a social physiologist."[2] He added that Marx "found only one of the diseases of society; he did not discover the law of social progress and the central force in history," holding instead that the struggle for subsistence is that central force.[2] Ehret argues that Sun's emphasis on technological leaps from lower to higher productivity offsets the tensions Marx attributed to class struggle, overcoming the "law of diminishing returns" advanced by [[john-stuart-mill|John Stuart Mill]].[2]

In Carlson's framing, Communism served a specific British purpose. The Industrial Revolution created a middle class, which meant the [[british-cabal|British Cabal]] could no longer run the world through a two-tiered system of elites and working poor; Communism was introduced as a "middle-class killer."[3] Asked who really pulled the strings of Communism, Carlson answers: England.[3]
