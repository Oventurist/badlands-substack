# Alison Miller

Alison Miller is a clinical psychologist and author whose clinical work with survivors of [[ritual-abuse|ritual abuse]] and organized [[mind-control-programming|mind control]] forms the backbone of the Badlands Media article "Gazing Into the Abyss pt.2: a Therapist's Tale."[1] She is described as the author of two works considered highly useful to clinicians in the field, *Healing the Unimaginable* (2011) and *Becoming Yourself*.[1]

## Clinical framing and warnings

In the introduction to *Healing the Unimaginable*, Miller offers two reasons why genuine survivors should not read detailed accounts of what ritual abusers and mind controllers do: first, that survivors' own memories may be contaminated by exposure to other people's information, since survivors already struggle to believe their own recollections; and second, that graphic detail can trigger flashbacks or trained behaviors, which is unpleasant and destabilizing.[1] The Badlands article reproduces these two warnings verbatim as its own disclaimer, restricting its recommended audience to therapists, psychoanalysts, skeptics of ritual abuse, and readers who believe they can handle extreme material.[1]

Miller describes the beginning of this type of clinical work as a "hit the ground running" period, in which the clinician must absorb disclosures that force a first confrontation with horrific realities and that immediately demand new skills and knowledge.[1]

## The "first four" clients

Miller's entry into the subject came through four early clients diagnosed with what was then called multiple personality disorder, later reclassified as [[dissociative-identity-disorder|dissociative identity disorder]].[1]

The first was **Lorraine**, a woman crippled by severe osteoarthritis and confined to a wheelchair. As Lorraine she was sweet-natured, gentle and fond of reading and sewing; as an alter called Big Susie she was a playful five-year-old with a collection of giant dolls and a stuffed gorilla, for whom Lorraine sewed clothes; as Little Susie she was a mute, terrified three-year-old.[1] A few weeks before her death, Lorraine asked Miller whether suicide was a mortal sin, and Miller answered no, knowing she was in a sense granting permission.[1] Miller recorded that she had told the parts of Lorraine that wanted to die that killing the parts which did not want to die would be murder, and that the parts kept taking an internal vote in which more of them wished to go each time.[1]

The second was **Teresa**, Lorraine's best friend and also a DID patient, a single parent who had disclosed sexual abuse by her father and alcoholism in both parents.[1] Miller later acquired two further clients: **Tony**, a young man supposedly suffering from schizophrenia who had a child alter and alters representing each member of the band Guns N' Roses, and **Jennifer**, a teenager removed from her home after disclosing that she had sexually abused her younger brother.[1]

## The anomalies

A series of unexplained events surrounded the four clients.[1] Jennifer, placed in foster care and barred from contact with her mother, reported that her mother nonetheless appeared out of nowhere at random locations whenever she left the foster home.[1] Tony described "waking up" at midnight standing on a corner by a gas station near his home, apparently waiting for a ride, with no memory of getting there.[1]

Miller also recounted driving Teresa home from treatment and confronting Teresa's elderly grandmother with the statement that Teresa had told her everything; when Miller specified "alcoholism and sexual abuse," the grandmother appeared to sigh with relief and said she was just an old woman who would not know about such things — a response Miller found very strange.[1] Shortly afterward Teresa abruptly called both Miller and her child protection worker to say she could no longer handle her young daughter and had to give her up for adoption, despite Miller's assessment that Teresa's parenting had markedly improved and that she loved the child.[1]

The explanation, when it emerged, was that all four clients belonged to the same cult group, largely through their own families: Teresa had known Tony in junior high and still attended the group with him; Jennifer and her family were also involved, as was Lorraine.[1] Tony's mother was described as the cult's high priestess and his stepfather as a kind of regional manager.[1] All four had been abused by the same people, mostly relatives, and were still being abused and compelled to take part in rituals; Teresa's daughter was likewise being used until Teresa surrendered her, and only after the adoption did Teresa begin disclosing her own ritual abuse.[1]

## Interference by the perpetrator group

Miller's notes record that cult members lurked outside her office building to abduct her clients and take them elsewhere to be punished for talking to her, indicating that the group knew what had been said in session.[1] Handlers would set off "programs" — conditioned responses trained into the clients' child parts — causing clients to fall asleep in session, telephone the group to report the contents of therapy, suddenly lose the ability to understand English, cut themselves, or attempt to harm Miller.[1] The group also operated a regular "pick-up system" outside the mental hospital, in which admitted members were overcome by an urge to smoke at a precise time each night, a post-hypnotic trigger of the sort documented in G. H. Estabrooks' *Hypnotism*, whereupon a cult member would approach to threaten them or activate further programs enabling future abductions.[1]

## Lorraine's death

After Miller took a month's leave to travel in Europe with her youngest child, she returned to find that Lorraine had spent nearly the whole period in the psychiatric emergency ward, and that the abuse had escalated in her absence.[1] Lorraine reported being picked up on the street by cult people who told her that if she did not kill herself properly this time they would kill Miller, her psychiatrist, her sister in a neighboring city, and her sister's children; because most parts of Lorraine were children, they believed the threat.[1]

Miller attempted to have Lorraine held in hospital, but authorities judged her too much trouble and planned to transfer her to housing in another city.[1] Miller wrote in the ward book: "Do not discharge her until I've had a chance to see her again. If you do, she will kill herself."[1] The psychiatrist telephoned that night, said he believed the note but could not keep Lorraine until Miller's next available appointment, and asked whether discharging her Thursday rather than Sunday would matter; Miller answered that she was not safe either way.[1] Lorraine was discharged on Thursday and, early Saturday, fell or jumped from her apartment window to her death.[1]

There was no investigation, as her long history of suicidality led to the assumption of suicide rather than murder.[1] Miller regarded the death as suspicious: the bathroom window was tiny, high off the floor and almost impossible to open or squeeze through, Lorraine was heavy and had no muscle tone, and the apartment door was locked.[1] Miller noted that Lorraine had grown up in a group home where her original cult abuse occurred, the group-home parents being cult-involved and using the children in rituals.[1] At a brief memorial service attended by several suspicious-looking people, Miller spoke about Lorraine and Susie, saying she was sad but less so than when Lorraine was alive and constantly being re-abused by people who were not acknowledged to exist, and that on this subject at least she agreed with the cult.[1]

## Threats to the therapist

Miller has written that the first generation of therapists doing this work were told by clients that a single massive cult was everywhere, knew everything, possessed state-of-the-art technology, and was willing to kill both clients and therapists to prevent disclosure.[1] She reported hearing constant threats to her life, with Teresa relating in detail how the cult high priest — whose identity Miller knew — intended to lie in wait in her office hallway and kill her.[1] The worst incident she describes was learning that cult leaders had driven the four clients in a van to her home's driveway, had them abused while drugged by a woman impersonating her, then given them her address and instructed them to set fire to the house while Miller and her children slept; the account was corroborated about a year later by another of the four.[1]

## Outcomes and outlook

Despite Lorraine's death, Miller's record is not uniformly bleak: two of the "first four" eventually reintegrated their alters, recovered from their trauma, and now live productive lives beyond the cult's reach.[1] The article argues that this demonstrates that humans can heal from virtually any psychological wound given determination and compassion, that therapists must know success is possible, and that recovered survivors often become powerful allies in raising awareness and helping others heal.[1] Miller presented on the subject at the Survivorship Conference in 2017.[1]

## Biography

Miller is a now-retired clinical psychologist who was previously in private practice in Victoria, British Columbia, Canada, and who worked for many years in child and youth mental health services treating children and families.[2] She is the original developer of the Living in Families Effectively (LIFE) Seminars and has co-authored two books on parenting with Dr. Allison Rees.[2] Since 1991 she has been treating and learning from people with dissociative disorders — in particular survivors of ritual abuse and mind control — and has developed a protocol for effective treatment and reintegration of the fractured personality.[2]

Badlands Media names Miller and Dr. [[james-randall-noblitt|James Randall Noblitt]] as the two modern authorities on the subject and as the psychotherapists it relied on most heavily in researching the "Gazing Into the Abyss" series, noting that both have had tremendous success treating dissociative identity disorder arising from ritual abuse — evidence, the series argues, that the condition can in fact be treated.[2]

## On traumatic dissociation

In *Becoming Yourself* (2014), Miller describes the human brain as a complex system of component circuits communicating through electrical and chemical signals, and traumatic dissociation as a state in which those parts are held apart by strong barriers so that one part is unaware of the content or feelings held by the others.[2] The child who attends school does not remember the abuse that happens at home or via the family, and may not even remember abuse from the preceding night; as an adult the person may have no conscious memory of it while still suffering flashbacks and related symptoms.[2] This passage is used in the series to explain why the fracturing produced by [[ritual-abuse|ritual abuse]] provides the substrate for [[mind-control-programming|mind control]] and the emergence of [[alter-personalities|alters]].[2]
