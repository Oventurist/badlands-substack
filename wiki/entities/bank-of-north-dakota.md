# Bank of North Dakota

The Bank of North Dakota (BND) is a state-owned bank headquartered in Bismarck, North Dakota, which opened its doors on July 28, 1919. In [[a-seismic-shift|A Seismic Shift]], [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presents it as the only publicly owned bank in the continental United States and as the surviving American proof that a public banking option works.

## Origins

Drawing on a 2019 Vox report, the article describes early-twentieth-century North Dakota as a **"banking desert."** The state was settled sparsely by farmers, and the low population density meant local financial institutions had trouble taking root. Combined with the deregulation and inequality of the gilded age, this left the state's economy at the mercy of corporate banks in financial capitals such as Chicago and Minneapolis, which could inflate farmers' loan rates or undercut grain prices "without fear of reprise." David Flynn, chair of the economics and finance department at the University of North Dakota, is quoted saying that much of a resident's economic livelihood was effectively controlled by out-of-state interests who might not have local producers' best interests in mind. Lange characterises the founding movement — driven by farmers, socialists and populists — as being fundamentally about removing out-of-state banker control over the people.

## Performance and durability

The bank is owned by all residents of the state and has operated for more than a century, surviving the Great Depression, the Savings and Loan scandal of the 1980s and the 2008 credit crisis. Between 1919 and 1933 it lent almost $41 million to more than 16,000 farmers. During the Depression it enabled the state to sustain itself and recover quickly; while school teachers elsewhere in the country were paid essentially in IOUs typically redeemed at a 15 percent loss, the BND paid them in full. In the 1940s farmland foreclosed during the Depression was sold back, in many cases to the same families that had lost it. The article notes similar success in helping the state withstand the 2008–09 financial crisis.

## Benefits cited

Lange highlights two ongoing benefits. The bank returns a portion of its profits to the state general fund — more than a billion dollars over its history — which both lowers taxes and helps fund infrastructure projects. It also offers lower-cost student loans to state residents, which the article says promotes education, creates more local control and gives power back to the people.

## Influence

The bank's record has inspired a public banking movement elsewhere. California has pursued a public bank since 2018 with mixed success, and efforts have been made in nearly two dozen other states; in one recent year New York, New Mexico, New Hampshire and Massachusetts introduced bills to create or explore public banks, none of which passed. Advocates cited in the article include Ellen Brown of the Public Banking Institute, who argues that public banking keeps funds in local economies rather than fuelling Wall Street speculation.

The article treats the BND as a state-level analogue to the national [[postal-savings-system|Postal Savings System]] and to the proposed FedAccounts, and as an American echo of the public banking safeguards established in Costa Rica by José "Pepe" Figueres.
