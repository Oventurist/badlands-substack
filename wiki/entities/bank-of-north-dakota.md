# Bank of North Dakota

The Bank of North Dakota (BND) is a state-owned bank headquartered in Bismarck, North Dakota, that opened its doors on July 28, 1919, and is described as the only publicly owned bank in the continental United States.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) It is owned collectively by the residents of the state, and has operated continuously for more than a century — a history that includes surviving the Great Depression, the Savings and Loan scandal of the 1980s, and the credit crisis of 2008.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Origins

In the early 1900s North Dakota was, in the framing used by Badlands Media, a "banking desert": a sparsely settled farming state whose low population density made it difficult for local financial institutions to take root.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) That decentralization, combined with the predatory character of the Gilded Age — an era of economic deregulation and extreme income inequality — left the state's fledgling economy vulnerable to corporate banks headquartered in financial capitals such as Chicago and Minneapolis, which could inflate farmers' loan rates or undercut grain prices without fear of reprisal.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) David Flynn, economics and finance department chair at the University of North Dakota, is quoted describing how much of local economic livelihood was "controlled by out-of-state interests who may not have the best interests of the local producers in mind."1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

Private corporate banks saw little profit in servicing the state's small, mostly agricultural population, leaving residents at the mercy of predatory lenders and out-of-state banks.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) The movement that produced the BND was accordingly framed as an effort to remove out-of-state banker control over the people, driven by a coalition of farmers, socialists and populists struggling against corporate domination, debt burdens, gaping inequality, ineffective reformists and bought politicians.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Performance and public benefits

Between 1919 and 1933 the BND lent almost $41 million to more than 16,000 farmers in the state, and during the Great Depression the bank helped North Dakota uniquely sustain itself and recover quickly — foreshadowing similar success in helping the state withstand the 2008–09 financial crisis.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) While school teachers elsewhere in the country were paid essentially in IOUs typically redeemed at a 15 percent loss, the BND was able to pay them in full; in the 1940s, farmland foreclosed during the Depression was in many cases sold back to the same families that had lost it.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

The bank returns a portion of its profits to the state general fund, which both lowers taxes and helps finance infrastructure projects; over its history it has returned more than a billion dollars.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) It also offers lower-cost student loans to state residents, which the article presents as promoting education, increasing local control, and giving power back to the people.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Model for other states

The BND's record has drawn national attention. A movement to create a public bank in California has gained prominence, with both successes and failures, since 2018, and observers note that features of the modern economic landscape — rising income inequality and predatory practices by financial institutions — closely resemble the conditions of a century ago when the BND was created.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Efforts to establish public banks have been made in nearly two dozen other states; in one year alone, four legislatures besides California — New York, New Mexico, New Hampshire and Massachusetts — introduced bills to create or explore public banks, though none passed.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

Ellen Brown, chair of the Public Banking Institute, is quoted in the source arguing that a public banking system can fund the goods, services and infrastructure required to satisfy the needs of the people and the economy without unsustainable debt, taxation or environmental degradation.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Badlands Media presents the BND as proof that a [[public-banking-option|public banking option]] is the safest arrangement in times of financial crisis, and as a model that parallels the reforms of [[jose-figueres|José "Pepe" Figueres]] in Costa Rica.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Comparison with Costa Rica

Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] cites the Bank of North Dakota as the sole American counterpart to the four state-owned banks of [[costa-rica|Costa Rica]].[2] Quoting the [[world-business-academy|World Business Academy]], he notes that publicly-owned banks have been available in Costa Rica for so long and work so well that citizens there take for granted that any country which knows how to run an economy has a [[public-banking-option|public banking option]] — and that "Costa Ricans are amazed to hear there is only one public depository bank in the United States (the Bank of North Dakota), and few people have private access to it."[2] Lange stresses that limited access as the key shortcoming, asking whether that is about to change and promising a following article arguing that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] intends to create a nationwide American public banking option as a safeguard against bankers controlling the government again.[2]
