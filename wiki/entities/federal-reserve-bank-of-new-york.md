# Federal Reserve Bank of New York

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York was the origin point of what the article, citing CNBC, calls the largest airborne transfer of currency in the history of the world.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest)

Cash was packed onto pallets inside a heavily guarded New York Federal Reserve compound in East Rutherford, New Jersey, trucked to Andrews Air Force Base outside Washington, and flown by military aircraft to Baghdad International Airport.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) By one account the New York Fed shipped about $40 billion in cash between 2003 and 2008; in the first two years alone the shipments included more than 281 million individual bills weighing 363 tons.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) A typical pallet held 640 bundles — "bricks" — of a thousand bills each and weighed 1,500 pounds, separated by colour-coded seals: gold for $100 bills, brown for $50, purple for $20.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest)

The chain of custody was rigorously documented as the money left the Fed's custody and was signed over to Air Force officers, who oversaw loading of the C-17s and flew with the cash to Baghdad.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) The article's central question is why such diligence was applied on the American end only for the paper trail to vanish entirely once the money reached Baghdad, where every flight was met by a single [[coalition-provisional-authority|CPA]] official.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) Its answer is that the absence of a paper trail was purposeful, and that the omission is what made the [[taxpayer-theft|theft]] possible.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) The article ties this to a Q question asking how much cash the Fed sent to Iraq during the [[iraq-war|Iraq War]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest)

## Survey of Consumer Expectations

The New York Fed also publishes a monthly consumer survey tracking household expectations for inflation, income and debt repayment, known as the [[survey-of-consumer-expectations|Survey of Consumer Expectations]].[2] The September 2023 edition, released in October 2023, showed one-year inflation expectations rising for a second consecutive month to 3.67 percent from 3.63 percent, three-year expectations rebounding to 3.00 percent, and five-year expectations falling to 2.84 percent — the lowest since May 2023 — while median inflation uncertainty rose slightly across all three horizons.[2] The most widely reported finding was an unexpected spike in household delinquency expectations, the worst reading since the April 2020 COVID-19 crash, which commentators treated as evidence that American households had exhausted their capacity to pay down debt.[2]
