# Pierre Teilhard de Chardin

Pierre Teilhard de Chardin is described in Badlands Media reporting as a "Jesuit transhumanist" whose theories, along with those of Marshall McLuhan, [[julian-huxley|Sir Julian Huxley]] and cybernetics founder [[norbert-wiener|Norbert Wiener]], were followed by the priests of the [[fourth-industrial-revolution|Fourth Industrial Revolution]] in preaching a new gospel to humanity.[1]

His intellectual heir is identified as [[ray-kurzweil|Ray Kurzweil]], and the lineage is treated as the doctrinal foundation of the [[transhumanism|transhumanist religion]] promoted by figures such as [[yuval-noah-harari|Yuval Noah Harari]] within the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] orbit.[1] [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] has treated Chardin's thought elsewhere under the heading of the Darwinian perversion of Christianity, presenting him as one of the most influential and destructive Jesuit forces in modern history.[1][2]

## Early life and formation

Chardin was born in 1881 in Auvergne, France, and was enrolled in a Jesuit school at the age of 14.[2] When the order was barred from France in 1901 he finished his studies in England, where he fell under the influence of the leading modernist theologian [[george-tyrrell|George Tyrrell]].[2] The modernists were obsessed with reconciling Christianity with the new ethics and science of the modern age, and the hardest problem facing Jesuit modernists was the reconciliation of two seemingly irreconcilable systems — Christianity, which held mankind to be sacred, and Darwinism, whose mechanistic universe denied the divine in humanity or the broader universe.[2] Harmonizing these worlds became Chardin's mission in life.[2]

His talents were recognized early, and he acquired a following of devotees among his classmates and even among superiors of the order.[2]

## Piltdown Man and Peking Man

After teaching paleontology in Cairo from 1905 to 1908, Chardin was called back for a holiday in Piltdown, England, where in 1912 he discovered a skull and bone fragments in a field during a short walk.[2] The remains were hailed as the long-awaited "missing link" between ape and man, and the international press trumpeted them as proof that Darwin was right; a team of British archaeologists was deployed by the Royal Academy to complete the excavation.[2] Chardin became a celebrity, although doubts persisted among scientists — especially dentists — who asked why no teeth had been found and why the jawbone so resembled that of an orangutan.[2] Despite the site being remarkably unprotected, Chardin returned on holiday and conveniently stumbled on a tooth no one else had seen.[2] Only in 1953 did scientists conclusively prove that [[piltdown-man|Piltdown Man]] was a dyed and shaved monkey jaw joined to a human skull, and that the tooth Chardin found belonged to a dog painted and shaved to fit.[2]

In the wake of the First World War Chardin was stationed in China, where he lived for the next twenty years.[2] While in Peking he joined a [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]]-funded expedition that uncovered a new claimed missing link in 1926, publicized as [[peking-man|Peking Man]].[2] Though suspicion about Piltdown was already spreading, Chardin published several scientific papers on the new find, creating a worldwide sensation.[2] The hundreds of bone fragments were crated for shipment to the United States and mysteriously lost; Chardin expressed no remorse and made no attempt to recover them, telling friends there was no point crying over spilled milk and that all should proceed with renewed faith that Darwinism must be accepted as the essence of Christianity.[2]

## Conflict with Rome

Chardin's neo-Darwinian Christianity became extremely popular among wide groupings of his fellow Jesuits but alarmed influential bishops and cardinals in Rome, since it challenged fundamental dogma of the Church itself, including the divinity of Christ and the nature of sin, forgiveness and the mass.[2] He was deprived of the right to teach or publish his theories and told to focus on missionary work instead — a command he received with disgust.[2]

Ehret documents a deep antipathy in Chardin toward the Chinese and the poor in general, noting that he never bothered to learn even the rudiments of the Chinese language despite living in China for over 20 years.[2] In 1929 Chardin wrote: "Do the yellows [the Chinese] have the same human value as the whites?… the cause seems to be the natural racial foundation… Christian love overcomes all inequalities, but it does not deny them."[2] In a 1936 letter he called for official recognition of both "the primacy/priority of the earth over nations" and "the inequality of peoples and races," arguing that because not all ethnic groups have the same value, "they must be dominated."[2]

## A new religion

Chardin described his project candidly in 1936: "What increasingly dominates my interest is the effort to establish within myself, and to diffuse around me, a new religion (let's call it an improved Christianity if you like) whose personal God is no longer the great Neolithic landowner of times gone by, but the Soul of the world."[2] In a letter of March 21, 1941 he wrote: "I cannot fight against Christianity; I can only work inside it by trying to transform and convert it. A revolutionary attitude would be much easier, and much more pleasant, but it would be suicidal. So I must go step by step, tenaciously."[2]

His system replaced Darwinian randomness with a directionality untethered from morality or purpose, oriented toward a future [[omega-point|Omega Point]] at which humanity would bifurcate into a new evolutionary organism — a notion Ehret compares to Kurzweil's [[technological-singularity|Singularity]].[2] The universe was divided into four phases: cosmogenesis, biogenesis, homogenesis (the age of the [[noosphere|Noosphere]]) and Christogenesis (the Omega Point).[2] Ehret stresses that Chardin's Noosphere differed sharply from that of the Russian academician [[vladimir-vernadsky|Vladimir Vernadsky]].[2]

## Beyond good and evil

In place of moral change measured against a universal standard of right and wrong, Chardin substituted "quantitative complexity," rendering acts of evil pure acts of nature devoid of moral judgement.[2] In *Comment je vois les Choses* he wrote that "in our modern perspective of a Universe in a process of cosmogenesis, the problem of evil no longer exists," events being "essentially subject to the play of probabilities of chance."[2] The former Jesuit historian [[malachi-martin|Malachi Martin]] recorded that Teilhard "was not overly shocked by bloodshed, regarded violence as a necessary concomitant of Evolution, and seemed to have enjoyed war," treating death as a "mutation."[2]

Because evil had no real existence in his system, Ehret argues, nothing prevented Chardin from extolling racially targeted [[eugenics|eugenics]] in a closed system of limited resources.[2] In *Human Energy* he asked what attitude "the advancing wing of humanity" should take toward "fixed or definitely unprogressive ethnical groups," how far the earth should "tolerate, racially or nationally, areas of lesser activity," and "to what extent should not the development of the strong… take precedence over the preservation of the weak?"[2] Ehret characterizes this as pure Nietzsche, Galton and [[thomas-malthus|Malthus]].[2]

Chardin nevertheless favored expanding eugenics for all races, complaining that "our generation still regards with distrust all efforts proposed by science for controlling the machinery of heredity, of sex-determination and the development of the nervous systems."[2] In 1951 he called for "a nobly human form of eugenics," concluding that "eugenics applied to individuals leads to eugenics applied to society."[2]

## Julian Huxley and transhumanism

Chardin's friendship with Julian Huxley, founder of transhumanism and a leading eugenicist, was mutually reinforcing.[2] In 1941 Chardin praised Huxley's *The Uniqueness of Man* as running "so parallel to my own ideas (even though without integrating God as the term of the series) that I feel greatly cheered."[2] Huxley had been repackaging Darwin alongside [[h-g-wells|H.G. Wells]] and J.B.S. Haldane under the [[new-evolutionary-synthesis|New Evolutionary Synthesis]], essentially identical to Chardin's thesis but without any pretense of harmony with scripture.[2] Chardin wrote his 1949 *Man's Place in Nature* as homage to Thomas Huxley's 1904 work of the same name, and Julian wrote the introduction to Chardin's *The Phenomenon of Man*.[2]

Chardin also embraced [[cybernetics|cybernetics]] as it emerged after 1945, asking in *The Future of Man*: "How can we fail to see the machine as playing a constructive part in the creation of a truly collective consciousness?" — pointing to radio, television and "astonishing electronic computers" as manifestations of "a kind of super-Brain."[2]

## Death and legacy

Asked late in life how he felt about the Church's continuing ban on his works, Chardin replied: "I have so many friends now, in good strategic positions, that I have no fear of the future. I have won the game."[2] When he died in 1955 his writings were still largely banned as heresy by the [[vatican|Vatican]], yet they continued to circulate as a kind of Soviet-era samizdat, recruiting converts to his "new and improved Christianity."[2]

Over the following decades his followers played a major role in shaping the [[second-vatican-council|Second Vatican Council]] launched by Pope John XXIII in 1962, and networks concentrated in Ibero-America innovated [[liberation-theology|Liberation Theology]].[2] Chardin himself had remarked that "a good dip into Marxism might start things moving again."[2] Ehret writes that while Popes John Paul I and II tried to resist this deconstruction of Christianity, "a touch of poison and a couple of assassin's bullets" brought the Holy See back into line, preparing the ground for a full [[jesuits|Jesuit]] takeover of the Church and the integration of Christianity into a eugenics-driven religion.[2]
