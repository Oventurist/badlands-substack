# Rain Country Dance Association

The Rain Country Dance Association (RCDA) is a Seattle-based organization that describes itself as an "LGBTQ+ nonprofit social dance organization driven by our members." It came to national attention in April 2024 when it ejected a line-dancing troupe from one of its contests over the performers' American flag costumes.[1]

## The 2024 flag costume controversy

The [[borderline-dance-team|Borderline Dance Team]], a troupe of line-dancing women, was scheduled to take part in an RCDA contest in Seattle but was told before the event that the star-spangled suits shown in its promotional material "were offensive to some of the convention goers." In a social media post, the team said it was informed that those who complained were "associated with the situation in Palestine and the Trans community" and that they felt "triggered and unsafe" because of the outfits.[1]

Following widespread public backlash, the RCDA announced on its website that since the Monday afternoon after the incident it had been "receiving an extreme amount of online hostility and harassment" and had received "a threat of violence deemed credible by Seattle police," adding that it was in communication with the teams directly involved, would make no further public comments to media, and was prioritizing the safety and well-being of its community.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the episode as an illustration that "consistency of principle is not a feature of the activist left," but that "emotionally-driven outrage that boomerangs on their stated values certainly is." She noted the irony that a body she dubbed the "rainbow commie dance association" had prevented a group of dancers from "making the flag gay," and contrasted the outrage with the reception of the third Pitch Perfect film, in which the Barden Bellas perform a USO tour in sequined US-flag costumes and military fatigues without comparable objection.[1]

She further argued that the organizers, being avowedly activist, predictably claimed victimhood after backlash that was a direct consequence of their own public and controversial choice, and concluded that she was not troubled that the American flag had been disallowed by people who, in her characterization, advocate the mutilation and sterilization of children and the societal erasure of women — suggesting the outcome "could even be framed as a win."[1]
