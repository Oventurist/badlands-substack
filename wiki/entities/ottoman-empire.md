# Ottoman Empire

The Ottoman Empire was the imperial power that controlled the [[hashemites|Hashemite]] kingdom of Hejaz from the 16th century onward and against which the Hashemites sought sovereignty during the First World War.[1]

## Destruction of the First Saudi State

The [[emirate-of-diriyah|First State of Saud]] came to an end in 1814 when Abdullah — son and successor of [[mohammed-bin-saud|Mohammed bin Saud]] — attempted to take Hejaz from Ottoman control.[1] Abdullah and his kinsmen were captured, taken to Istanbul, and publicly beheaded, and Abdullah's head was thrown into the waters of the Bosphorus.[1]

## WWI context

During the First World War the leaders of the 1916 Arab Revolt, King Hussein and his son Prince Faisal, sought independence for Arabia from both the Ottoman Empire and the British Crown — a struggle the article promises to treat in its sequel, alongside what it calls the British Cabal's betrayal of the Hashemites.[1]

## Battle of Vienna and decline

In 1683 the Ottoman Empire besieged Vienna with a host of 150,000 invaders and was defeated at the [[battle-of-vienna|Battle of Vienna]] by a relief force whose decisive element was an 18,000-strong cavalry charge — described as the largest in recorded history — led by Polish King John III and his 3,000 [[winged-hussars|Winged Hussars]].[2] The defeat is considered the beginning of the empire's decline, which ended with its dissolution at the close of World War I, the core Ottoman homeland becoming modern-day Turkey.[2]

## Badlands historical framing (2024)

The September 2, 2024 Badlands News Brief revisited the empire's history in the context of Middle Eastern realignment, noting that the Ottoman Empire lasted over 600 years and stretched from Central Europe to Russia and Iran and across the north coast of Africa.[3] Ankara, capital of modern Turkey, was the seat of power, while Damascus was considered one of the empire's most important cities.[3]

The commentary argued that the Ottomans are viewed as malevolent in Western historiography because they usurped and ended the Byzantine Empire, a Greco-Christian civilization that lasted a thousand years — longer than the Roman Empire — a subject the writer said is nearly absent from mainstream school curricula.[3] Drawing on discussions with Matthew Ehret on the program Breaking History, the piece contended that it was not the Ottomans but Christian Crusaders from Italy who effectively destroyed Byzantium: during the Fourth Crusade the Knights Templar sacked Constantinople en route to the Holy Land and returned home with their loot, abandoning the Crusades and, in the writer's reading, demonstrating that the enterprise was about gold, conquest and glory rather than Christianity.[3]

The brief further asserted that the British convinced the Saudis, the Hashemites and others to revolt against the Ottomans during World War I and then betrayed those allies by refusing the freedom and autonomy they had been promised, leaving a century of turmoil in the region.[3] It presented the subsequent rapprochements — Syria's return to the Arab League, Turkish-Saudi-Emirati normalization, and [[recep-tayyip-erdogan|Erdogan]]'s mediation in Africa — as the healing of that century-old wound.[3]
