# Haqqani Network

The Haqqani network is the Afghan insurgent militia founded by Jalaluddin Haqqani that American officials, a decade into the occupation of [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]], described as the country's deadliest insurgent group.[1] Badlands Media contributor Joe Lange presents the network as the clearest proof that the enemy in the [[war-on-terror|War on Terror]] was manufactured by the very agency fighting it.[1]

## Origins in the anti-Soviet campaign

The network's rise traces directly back to the secret, multi-billion-dollar campaign run by the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]] to build a radicalized and well-equipped army of Islamic jihadists — the [[mujahideen|mujahideen]] — to fight the Soviet Union in Afghanistan during the 1980s.[1] Steve Coll, author of *Ghost Wars*, is quoted saying "We facilitated his rise — we and the Saudis and Pakistani intelligence," identifying the CIA, Saudi intelligence and [[inter-services-intelligence|Pakistan's ISI]] as the three services behind [[jalaluddin-haqqani|Jalaluddin Haqqani]].[1] Former ambassador to Afghanistan Peter Tomsen told PBS *Frontline* in 2006 that the CIA "gave Haqqani the infrastructure" to build his network, and former senior CIA analyst Paul R. Pillar allowed that the group is plausibly stronger today than it would have been without the 1980s assistance.[1]

## Operations and tactics

By 2011 the network was blamed for a series of especially bold attacks, including the day-long assault on the U.S. embassy in Kabul.[1] Haqqani is credited with introducing suicide bombing as a tactic in Afghanistan — a form of radicalism that, during the Cold War, American planners treated not as a liability but as an asset.[1] Lange asks how many suicide bombings the world has since endured because of what he calls the true "axis of evil" of intelligence agencies, and how many innocent people were killed to keep the money spigot of war flowing.[1]

## Safe haven in Pakistan and the Trump aid cutoff

The network was based in [[pakistan|Pakistan]], which served as its safe haven, and it turned on the American military after the U.S. invaded Afghanistan.[1] A *New York Times* report in December 2017 that Washington might scrap $255 million in aid cited a case in which the United States demanded access to a captured militant from the Taliban-linked Haqqani network and the Pakistani government refused.[1] That refusal, together with the safe-haven issue, was the proximate reason [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] moved to cut off aid to Islamabad — what Lange calls "money flow disruption."[1]
