# USEIP (United States Election Integrity Plan)

USEIP is a Colorado-based grassroots election-integrity group that became the lead named defendant in *NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.*, a federal civil-rights suit filed in the United States District Court for the District of Colorado on March 9, 2022.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The plaintiffs alleged that USEIP and individually named defendants were "planning to, threatening to, and actually deploying armed agents to knock on doors throughout the state of Colorado," with the purpose and effect of intimidating Coloradans from voting, attempting to vote, assisting others to vote, or advocating for particular political beliefs.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Litigation

The suit brought two claims under Section 11(b) of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and one claim under the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) According to a defendant who represented herself pro se, no evidence of intimidation was produced during discovery, across two and a half years of motions, replies and responses, or at trial.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The case nevertheless remained active for 208 days after the affidavit that sustained it was filed.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

The trial, scheduled for five days, commenced July 15, 2024 and was terminated by the court after three days on a Rule 52(c) judgement based on partial findings — meaning the defendants prevailed without having to present their case in chief — with costs awarded to the defendants.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Presiding judge Charlotte Sweeney found that "plaintiffs have failed to introduce any evidence that can remotely be perceived as intimidating or threatening on behalf of the three defendants."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

The sole witness who claimed to have been approached and intimidated by canvassers admitted under oath that she had named "USEIP volunteers" only at the suggestion of plaintiffs' counsel; the court observed that she then "adopted that position and included it in her affidavit."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) That affidavit, signed under penalty of perjury, had been filed by the plaintiffs in December 2022 to defeat the defense's motion for summary judgement.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Significance

Defenders of USEIP characterize the litigation as an attempt to treat two constitutionally protected activities — questioning election outcomes and door-to-door political canvassing — as inherently intimidating conduct, and therefore as an assault on the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Related pages: [[naacp-v-useip]], [[election-denial]], [[canvassing]], [[lawfare]].

## Canvassing playbooks and the Colorado report

A March 23, 2023 Badlands Media article recommended USEIP as a resource for citizens seeking to verify local election records, noting that USEIP.org publishes free, publicly available playbooks and training materials for conducting [[canvassing]] in a manner that is legal and can stand up in court.[2] The article also pointed to a USEIP canvassing report that had been used in Colorado for legal action, public education, and public pressure on state and local election officials, along with a video overview of the canvassing project hosted on the organization's site.[2]

The article argued that canvassing is "basically the only way to determine whether election records are accurate and valid in any state or locality," and that a finding of inaccurate records opens the door to legal action — placing USEIP's methodology at the centre of one of the five local remedies it proposed for restoring election integrity.[2]
