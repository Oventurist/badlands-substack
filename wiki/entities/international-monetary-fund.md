# International Monetary Fund

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) is the international financial institution that extends credit to member states and, in doing so, exercises substantial leverage over the fiscal policy of indebted governments.[1]

## Syria's debt and the Saudi payoff

In Badlands commentary on the January 2026 Moscow summit between Syrian President [[ahmed-al-sharaa|Ahmed al-Sharaa]] and Russian President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]], the writer GhostofBasedPatrickHenry recounted that after Sharaa seized control of [[syria|Syria]] in December 2024, Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] stepped in and paid off Syria's debt to the IMF.[1] The stated effect was to avert what the author described as a "firesale/repo situation" of the kind he argued was then playing out in [[argentina|Argentina]] under Javier Milei.[1]

The episode is presented in that commentary as an example of Gulf capital displacing multilateral lender leverage in the region, clearing the way for Gulf states to invest billions in rebuilding Syria's natural gas infrastructure and to make the country attractive to institutional investors, banks, and insurers.[1]

## April 2024 Fiscal Monitor warning on U.S. deficits

In its April 2024 benchmark Fiscal Monitor and World Economic Outlook, the IMF warned that the United States' recent economic performance was partially the product of unsustainable borrowing, and that massive U.S. fiscal deficits had stoked inflation and posed "significant risks" for the global economy.[2] The fund wrote that "the exceptional recent performance of the United States is certainly impressive and a major driver of global growth, but it reflects strong demand factors as well, including a fiscal stance that is out of line with long-term fiscal sustainability," adding that "fiscal policy developments in major economies, notably in the United States, have implications for global financing conditions."[2]

The IMF said the United States had exhibited "remarkably large fiscal slippages," with the fiscal deficit reaching 8.8 percent of GDP in 2023 — more than double the 4.1 percent recorded for 2022 — and calculated that "Bidenomics" and its [[inflation-reduction-act|Inflation Reduction Act]] had contributed 0.5 percentage points to core inflation.[2] It projected a U.S. deficit of 7.1 percent the following year, more than three times the average for other advanced economies, and also raised concerns about Chinese government debt as Beijing coped with weak demand and a housing crisis.[2]

Badlands commentary read the report as an establishment warning flare rather than a message aimed at the public, arguing that a major central-banking institution was signaling that the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration's spending and money printing threatened not only the dollar but the Eurodollar system underpinning global fiat currencies — and doing so in an election year.[2] The commentary drew two conclusions: that the world's banks were not aligned on 2024 fiscal policy, and that theories of economic accelerationism embedded in financial readings of Operation Warp Speed "hold water."[2]

## September 2024 analysis of the inflation cycle

In a September 2024 Finance & Development article, IMF authors argued that recent price swings largely reflected energy- and supply-related shocks rather than macroeconomic overheating.[3] The piece noted that as inflation began to rise in 2021, most policymakers and analysts predicted the increase would be neither particularly large nor persistent, yet by 2022 inflation had become an acute problem for central bankers, before ebbing almost as suddenly as it had risen following some of the sharpest and most synchronized monetary tightening on record.[3]

The authors offered two broad explanations. The first stressed domestic drivers: countries were subjected to a similar sequence of shocks — the pandemic, mobility restrictions and associated fiscal and monetary support — with more support, tighter labor markets or less-well-anchored expectations translating into higher inflation.[3] The second stressed global causes, in which the surge in energy and food prices, intensified by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, triggered an energy crisis akin to the 1970s oil shocks, with geopolitics as the common cause; global energy prices and headline inflation rose together even as long-term inflation expectations held steady.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] accepted that inflation may have happened "everywhere, all at once," but observed that the recovery was plainly not consistent across the globe, citing a breakfast of two coffees and two handmade pastries in France for €6.14 (about $6.82) against far higher Colorado prices.[3] She noted the IMF article's point that French monetary policy uses government subsidies to keep sticker shock away from consumers, said she opposes such subsidies on principle while conceding she loves affordable breakfast, and paired the observation with the broader picture of US consumer spending appearing solid while lower-income shoppers at chains such as Dollar Tree continued to struggle.[3]
