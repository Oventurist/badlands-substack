# Costa Rica

The Republic of Costa Rica is a Central American nation that Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] holds up as the world's clearest working model of constitutional **safeguards** against capture of a government by banks, militaries and foreign intelligence services.[1] In Lange's framing, Costa Rica is "a republic similar to the United States" that, alone among its neighbours, was never ruled by a dictator or brutalized by its own government, and it ranks near the top of the world happiness index.[1]

## Contrast with the region

Lange draws the comparison directly from his earlier work on the [[school-of-assassins-and-dictators|School of Assassins and Dictators]], in which he described [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] and the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] installing dictators across Central and South America and training their militaries at the [[school-of-the-americas|School of the Americas]] to terrorize and murder their own populations in order to maintain control of those governments.[1] Costa Rica, he writes, stood out from the rest: its government does not resemble the neighbouring states that suffered dictatorship, oppression and rampant poverty.[1] His explanation for the difference is a single word — safeguards — and a single man, [[jose-figueres-ferrer|José "Pepe" Figueres Ferrer]].[1]

## Origins of the modern republic

After the 1948 election was annulled by a legislature controlled by the losing incumbent's party, Figueres led an irregular force of about 700 in the 44-day [[costa-rican-civil-war|Costa Rican Civil War]], defeated the army, and headed a provisional junta for 18 months before handing power back to the president the people had elected.[1] That junta enacted welfare legislation, promoted education, nationalized the banks and disbanded the armed forces, after which — in the words of a [[new-york-times|New York Times]] editorial quoted in the article — "ever since, Costa Ricans have settled their arguments constitutionally."[1]

## Constitutional safeguards

The constitutional assembly elected under the junta wrote a series of protections into the new constitution.[1] Per [[freedom-house|Freedom House]], the president is directly elected for a four-year term and may seek only a non-consecutive second term, with a 40 percent threshold to avoid a runoff; the 57-seat unicameral Legislative Assembly is elected every four years by proportional representation, and deputies may not run for two consecutive terms though they may return after skipping one.[1] The abolition of the armed forces was likewise constitutionalized, removing the centralized force a cabal could use to seize control.[1]

## Elections and the cédula

Costa Rica's electoral integrity rests on the [[cedula|cédula de identidad]], a credit-card-sized photo ID with a personal identification number issued to every Costa Rican over the age of twelve and to citizens only.[1] Citing QCostaRica.com, the article reports there has not been a single documented case of voter fraud in post-1948 Costa Rican elections — 1948 being the year the elections were deemed fraudulent and annulled by Congress, precipitating the civil war.[1] Foreigners with legal residency receive a "Dimex" residency card rather than a cédula, and all others use a passport; only the cédula is valid for voting, so the credential itself proves citizenship.[1]

## The public banking system

Figueres nationalized the banking system, which Lange describes as a massive move to take power over the government away from the cabal's puppet masters.[1] Quoting the [[world-business-academy|World Business Academy]] and resident Scott Bidstrup, the article reports that of 29 licensed banks, mutual associations and credit unions in Costa Rica, **four** were established as national, publicly-owned banks in 1949 and have remained in public hands ever since, despite enormous pressure from the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]] and the United States to privatize them.[1] While countless private banks and mutual associations came and went over three decades — with depositors losing most of the value of their accounts — the four state banks compete fiercely with one another, none have failed in 31 years, and they now hold fully **80% of all retail deposits** in the country.[1]

Bidstrup adds that the stability of the local economy is a principal reason Costa Rica has never had much difficulty attracting direct foreign investment and remains the regional leader in that regard; because most money sits in banks carrying the full faith and credit of the Republic, funds stay in the banking system and are available for lending, whereas neighbours with fully privatized systems have effectively encouraged citizens to keep savings at "Mattress First National" and lurch from crisis to crisis.[1] This arrangement is the archetype of what Lange calls the [[public-banking-option|public banking option]], of which he says the [[bank-of-north-dakota|Bank of North Dakota]] is the only American analogue.[1]

## Pressure from the global financial system

Lange concedes the cabal was never able to eliminate the public option banks, but says it damaged Costa Rica's economy when the world was forced onto the [[petrodollar|petrodollar]] and the country went into debt to purchase oil; the IMF and the [[world-bank|World Bank]] then imposed austerity as they had on many small countries in order to keep them trapped in the debt-slavery system.[1] Even through those hard times, he writes, the people's money remained protected in the publicly owned banks — the enduring proof of "Don Pepe's" greatest safeguard, that giving the people power over the banking system also ensures they retain power over their government.[1]
