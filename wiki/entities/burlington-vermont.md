# Burlington, Vermont

Burlington is the largest city in Vermont, situated on Lake Champlain, and the municipality where [[bernie-sanders|Bernie Sanders]] first won elective office.[1]

## The 1980 mayoral upset

After losing two Senate campaigns on the [[liberty-union-party|Liberty Union]] ticket, Sanders lowered his sights in 1980 and entered a crowded race for mayor of Burlington as a socialist, leveling a series of attacks against local developer [[antonio-pomerleau|Tony Pomerleau]].[1] The combination of socialist rhetoric and criticism of a wealthy land developer launched what would become the Bernie brand, and it proved effective by the narrowest of margins: Sanders upset Burlington's local political establishment by winning the race by ten votes out of 8,650 cast.[1]

## Waterfront development controversy

Sanders had campaigned on the slogan "Burlington is not for sale!", directed specifically at Pomerleau, whose plan was to build a condominium and shopping center on the city's lakefront — a project Sanders denounced as an "enclave for the rich."[1] Progressive journalist Greg Guma recalled that opposition to real estate development was largely why Sanders won, with a coalition of local progressives protesting that the city was being transformed by gentrification: "Low-income people were being driven out of town... by condominium and office conversions and high rents. They were being replaced by upwardly mobile, young professionals with disposable income."[1]

Once in office, Sanders reversed course. He struck an alliance with the Pomerleau family that lasted until Pomerleau's death in 2018, eased his rhetoric, and began collaborating with Burlington's upper class on a variety of projects.[1] He killed the Pomerleau plan as promised but replaced it with a far larger waterfront development called "Aldean" that would "dwarf the $30 million Pomerleau project," bringing in outside investors — including the heiress to the Dow Jones fortune — rather than local ones who might have returned revenue to the community.[1] Supporters subsequently learned that Mayor Sanders had been holding private meetings with these investors, the very sort of conduct he had condemned in the political establishment.[1] When the Citizens Waterfront Group pressed for transparency and an end to the closed-door meetings, Sanders "blasted the group," and figures in the green movement accused him of "collusion with business interests."[1] Sanders ultimately sought a city referendum approving municipal bonds to finance the project, and the voters rejected it.[1]

## Defense manufacturing and the local economy

Despite the referendum defeat, Sanders remained popular because Burlington's economy was booming — propped up in part by a [[general-electric|General Electric]] plant, GE then being one of the nation's largest defense contractors, and by the military spending Sanders denounced in his campaigns.[1] The plant employed three thousand workers and had nearly doubled its defense contracts by 1986, producing Vulcan Gatling guns for attack helicopters and fighter jets.[1] When progressive protesters picketed the facility, Sanders sided with GE executives, denounced the demonstrations, and had some protesters arrested.[1] He later persuaded [[lockheed-martin|Lockheed Martin]] to site a research center in Burlington and secured the basing of 18 [[f-35|F-35]] fighter jets at the city's airport for the Vermont National Guard.[1]
