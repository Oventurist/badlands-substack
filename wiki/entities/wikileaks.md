# WikiLeaks

WikiLeaks is the publishing organization through which, according to "A Bright Red Line," the February 2008 U.S. embassy cable from Moscow became publicly available.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-bright-red-line) That cable, written by [[william-burns|William Burns]], stated that Ukrainian entry into [[nato|NATO]] was "the brightest of all redlines for the Russian elite (not just Putin)" and predicted Russian meddling in Crimea and eastern Ukraine if a Membership Action Plan were offered prematurely.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-bright-red-line)

The article emphasizes the evidentiary value of such disclosures: because these are internal documents never meant for public scrutiny, they are void of the typical propaganda and largely deal in geopolitical fact.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-bright-red-line) WikiLeaks' work is attributed jointly to [[julian-assange|Julian Assange]], listed among the independent figures who broke through the Western media consensus on the war.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-bright-red-line)

## The Podesta emails

"America for Sale — Part 3" cites WikiLeaks as the source of the disclosure that reframed the 2009 Pyongyang hostage mission.[2] The organization published emails from the account of [[john-podesta|John Podesta]], which researchers believed had been hacked, and among them was a memo recording [[kim-jong-il|Kim Jong Il]]'s warm invitation for [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] to return to [[north-korea|North Korea]] "officially and unofficially."[2] The article treats this leaked correspondence as evidence that the ostensibly adversarial hostage negotiation was in fact a friendly meeting between longtime counterparties, and that the hostages served as a cover story.[2]

## Assange extradition proceedings (2024)

US prosecutors sought Julian Assange's extradition to face criminal charges arising from WikiLeaks' publication of extensive caches of confidential US military records and diplomatic cables, contending that the leaks endangered the lives of their operatives.[3] In March 2024, London's High Court prepared to rule on what could be [[julian-assange|Assange]]'s last appeal within the British court system.[3]

## Assange plea-deal reporting, 2023

Reporting in August 2023 indicated Washington was considering a plea deal allowing WikiLeaks founder [[julian-assange|Julian Assange]] to return to Australia after four years of detention in Britain.[4] The charges against him arose from WikiLeaks' 2010 publication of classified U.S. material, including video of a Baghdad airstrike that killed 18 civilians among them journalists, and hundreds of thousands of Iraq War field reports; 17 of the 18 counts allege [[espionage-act|Espionage Act]] violations.[4] Badlands commentary held that WikiLeaks "exposed the crimes of the US government and embarrassed the establishment," which is why officials had long sought to punish Assange.[4]

## 2010 Israeli embassy cables on Likud

A July 2024 Badlands News Brief highlighted WikiLeaks' 2010 publication of U.S. diplomatic communications in which the American Embassy in Israel expressed "grave concern" over the election of Inbal Gavrieli to the [[knesset|Knesset]] as a member of [[likud|Likud]], noting she was the niece of the head of the Gavrieli crime family.[5] According to that account, the communications revealed serious concern that organized crime syndicates had infiltrated Likud's central committee and effectively controlled it.[5] Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] speculated that this disclosure may have contributed to the persecution of [[julian-assange|Julian Assange]].[5]

## "The Ultimate USAID Couple?" (February 2025)

In February 2025 WikiLeaks published a post alleging that [[jeanne-bourgault|Jeanne Bourgault]], head of the media-development nonprofit [[internews|Internews]], is secretly married to [[ray-jennings|Ray S. Jennings]], a high-profile regime-change specialist for [[usaid|USAID]].[6] The post stated that Bourgault paid herself $451,000 while telling her alma mater that she goes to the office "once or twice a month," and that Jennings had authored numerous papers recommending funding for his wife's methods.[6]

WikiLeaks added that the relationship appeared to have only one obscure reference on the entire internet — a University of Washington alumni magazine feature titled "Alumna brings an information revolution to the developing world" — and that it had not been disclosed by USAID, Internews, or the 4,291 media outlets connected to the organization.[6] The [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] republished the item under the heading "The Ultimate USAID Couple?"[6]
