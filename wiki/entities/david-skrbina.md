# David Skrbina

**David Skrbina** (rendered "David Skribina" in the source article) is an American philosophy professor characterized by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as an advocate for eco-terrorist thinking and a modern co-thinker alongside [[andreas-malm|Andreas Malm]] and [[david-suzuki|David Suzuki]].[1]

## Thesis on autonomous technology

Throughout his works, Skrbina advances the argument that mindless evolutionary forces shape technology's growth as a force of nature, using humans as instruments toward an end point in which technology enslaves and then replaces humanity.[1] In an interview quoted by Ehret, Skrbina stated: "Technology advances with a tremendous, autonomous power. Humans are the implementers of this power, but we can't really guide it and we certainly can't stop it. In effect, it functions as a law of nature… Technology is rapidly heading toward true autonomy. Our opportunity to slow or redirect it is rapidly vanishing." He added that if technology reaches true autonomy — taking Ray Kurzweil's singularity date of 2045 as a rough guide — humanity will "either become more or less enslaved, or else wiped out."[1]

Ehret observes that this dystopian thesis echoes the science fiction of *2001: A Space Odyssey*, *Terminator* and *The Matrix*, and that it is structurally shared by transhumanists such as [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]], [[peter-thiel|Peter Thiel]], [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]] and [[yuval-noah-harari|Yuval Noah Harari]] — the difference being that where Skrbina's camp wishes to go to war with technological civilization, the transhumanists argue that humans must merge with technology to remain relevant.[1]

## Relationship with Ted Kaczynski

Skrbina corresponded extensively with [[ted-kaczynski|Ted Kaczynski]] between 2003 and 2023, brought Kaczynski's theories of technology into his classroom for over a decade, and wrote the foreword to Kaczynski's 2010 book *Technological Slavery*.[1] In 2016 he described Kaczynski as a "prophet," and potentially "a kind of savior of humanity and the planet."[1]

Skrbina also framed Kaczynski's bombing campaign in instrumental terms, saying: "Kaczynski killed in order to gain the notoriety necessary to get the manifesto into the public eye. And it worked. When it was published, the Washington Post sold something like 1.2 million copies that day—still a record. He devised a plan, executed it, and thereby caused millions of people to contemplate the problem of technology in a way they never had before."[1]
