# James Stillman

James Stillman was a cotton broker who became president of the National City Bank of New York, the institution later known as [[citibank|Citibank]], taking the reins in 1891.[1] He was the son of [[charles-stillman|Charles Stillman]], a "long-time National City Bank client" who made his initial fortune profiteering during the Mexican-American War and expanded it by smuggling contraband cotton out of Brownsville, Texas across the Rio Grande during the Civil War.[1] The Stillman family took control of Citibank after [[moses-taylor|Moses Taylor]] died.[1]

## Career and Citibank's rise

James Stillman began as a cotton broker after the Civil War, shifted into railroad investment, and then partnered with the Rockefeller family and [[standard-oil|Standard Oil]] as a Robber Baron promoting monopoly power in the United States.[1] Under his leadership Citi won Standard Oil and the U.S. government as clients and became the first American bank to reach $1 billion in assets.[1]

## Mexico and political influence

Maintaining his father's interests in Mexico, Stillman during the 1870s brokered a civil war that overthrew the Mexican government.[1] He also recruited former U.S. [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] officials to work for Citibank, ensuring the bank's increased wealth and political influence.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues this recruitment became a template across the bank's history, shielding it from prosecution and helping to secure future bailouts.[1]

## The revolving door and the Federal Reserve

Anticipating the revolving door between Citi and Washington, Stillman recruited his eventual successor, [[frank-vanderlip|Frank Vanderlip]], from the Treasury Department.[1] After secretly accepting Stillman's offer, Vanderlip spent another year at Treasury — what the cited account calls a patent conflict of interest — and later became one of the architects of America's third central bank, the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve System]].[1] Lange uses this lineage to argue that Citibank was directly behind the formation of the Federal Reserve and that the United States has had a centralized banking system controlled by foreign bankers ever since.[1]
