# Ketanji Brown Jackson

Ketanji Brown Jackson is an Associate Justice of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]].[1]

## Urias-Orellana opinion (2026)

In March 2026, Jackson wrote for a unanimous Court in a decision holding that federal appeals courts must defer to immigration agencies' factual findings when determining whether an asylum seeker's accepted facts qualify as persecution.[1] She wrote that the [[immigration-and-nationality-act|Immigration and Nationality Act]] requires this deferential standard of review, meaning appeals courts can overturn an agency decision only where it is clearly unsupported by the evidence.[1] The Court affirmed the lower court's ruling and no justices dissented.[1]

The case involved Douglas Humberto Urias-Orellana, a Salvadoran national who entered the United States without authorization in 2021 with his wife and child and testified that a hitman had targeted him in El Salvador.[1] An immigration judge found his testimony credible but concluded the events did not meet the legal threshold for asylum — a conclusion the Supreme Court left in place.[1] The ruling was widely characterized as a legal win for the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]].[1]

## Remarks at the birthright citizenship argument

During the April 1, 2026 oral argument in [[trump-v-barbara|Trump v. Barbara]], Jackson suggested that language used in [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] opinions had been written not strictly as legal reasoning but partly to perform public relations for the Court — "to help the public accept the outcome of this case."[2] Commentator [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] called the exchange "a rather shocking admission on the part of the Court," noting that most of the day's argument concerned the semantics of precedent, which together with statutory law based on the Reconstruction amendments forms the bedrock of the American legal system.[2] Paul asked why the limits of government power should be determined on a basis that could amount to "nothing more than public relations."[2] Badlands commentary extended the point to the Civil War and Reconstruction era, arguing that the precedent-setting language of that period served to calm and distract the public.[2]

## Dissent in Chiles v. Colorado

Jackson filed the lone dissent in [[chiles-v-colorado|Chiles v. Colorado]], the 8-1 decision issued at the end of March 2026 striking down Colorado's ban on "conversion therapy" for minors.[3] She warned that the ruling "opens a dangerous can of worms" and could impair states' ability to regulate medical and mental health care, potentially allowing licensed professionals to provide what she called "substandard medical care."[3] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], noting the lopsided vote, referred to her as "Autopen Jackson."[3]

## Broadway debut (December 2024)

In December 2024 Jackson made her Broadway debut, becoming the first member of the nation's highest court to appear on its stage, according to the production that invited her.[4] She took a one-night-only walk-on role on Saturday night in the Tony-nominated romantic comedy musical *& Juliet*, a modern retelling that imagines what would have happened if Shakespeare's female protagonist had survived and taken control of her own life.[4] The show announced her appearance days in advance on Instagram, noting she would also join a post-show talkback with the audience, and Jackson discussed the plan on that Saturday's episode of NPR's *Wait Wait … Don't Tell Me*, recorded at Carnegie Hall hours before she took the stage: "They have invited me to do a special walk-on role that I'm told they wrote for me. So I'm very excited."[4]

The production later posted behind-the-scenes footage showing Jackson rehearsing songs and choreography, having her hair and makeup done, and trying on a monochromatic teal costume of baggy jeans, tunic and corset.[4] The video captures a cast member bringing her onstage at the Stephen Sondheim Theatre and introducing her as the night's "very special guest"; Jackson ran on — and later took her bows — to roars of applause, exclaiming at the end, "I did it! I made it to Broadway."[4]

Badlands' commentary framed the appearance through its "we're watching a movie, but all the world is a stage" motif, calling Jackson the first black female Supreme Court justice to act on Broadway and attributing the racial qualifier to Ruth Bader Ginsburg, who gave a historic one-night-only performance in *The Daughter of the Regiment* in 2015.[4] The writer, while objecting to the premise of reimagining *Romeo and Juliet*, praised Jackson's stage presence and framed the spectacle as a form of political [[kayfabe]] parallel to "Trump-a-Mania."[4]

## Fourteenth Amendment disqualification argument (February 2024)

During the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]]'s February 2024 consideration of the effort to bar [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the ballot under Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment, Jackson — one of the Court's liberal justices — called the disqualification effort anti-democratic.[5] Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] cited her remark as evidence that the justices had given the theory, promoted as "unassailable" by Harvard's [[laurence-tribe|Laurence Tribe]], a reception that "bordered on the glacial," raising the possibility of a unanimous rejection.[5]

## Trump v. CASA dissent (2025)

Jackson's dissent in [[trump-v-casa|Trump v. CASA]], decided June 27, 2025, drew unusual attention for its tone, which commentators said shocked both court watchers and her colleagues and echoed the rhetoric of the [[no-kings-protests|"No Kings" protests]].[6] Critics wrote that the most junior justice had effectively accused her colleagues of being toadies for tyranny, prompting the majority to push back on the rhetoric.[6] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described Justice [[amy-coney-barrett|Amy Coney Barrett]]'s response as an "absolute dismantling" of a dissent that reimagined the Constitution.[6]
