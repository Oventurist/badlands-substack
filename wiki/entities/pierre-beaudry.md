# Pierre Beaudry

Pierre Beaudry was a Canadian-born historian whose pioneering studies of [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] are credited by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] with making the recovery of West's story possible.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

Ehret writes that West's role as a coordinator of an American renaissance movement in England has been obscured from the history books, and that were it not for Beaudry's work the story would still be hidden.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) It was through Beaudry's studies that Ehret says he was able to identify the human figure embedded on the rag in West's 1776 self-portrait as the priest and red-coated soldier from *The Death of General Wolfe*.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

Beaudry also advanced a hypothesis about the five cherubs in West's 1816 painting *Benjamin Franklin Drawing Electricity from the Sky*, noting the irony that while some carry a natural baby-like innocence, one displays strange adult attributes — read as a statement that true Promethean scientific discovery occurs only when adults tap into child-like innocence and wonder.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Studies of Samuel Morse

Beaudry is also the author of *Samuel F.B. Morse, The Leonardo DaVinci of America*, in which he analysed the public debates between [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]] and [[john-trumbull|John Trumbull]], concluding that Trumbull "was forced to resign by popular consent" and that Morse's flanking manoeuvre had been "right on target."[2] Beaudry framed the dispute in terms drawn from [[friedrich-schiller|Friedrich Schiller]]'s lectures on universal history as the difference between "studies for bread" and "studies for truth," observing that what gave the right to vote in the [[american-academy-of-fine-arts|Academy of Fine Arts]] was a share in stocks, whereas in the [[national-academy-of-design|National Academy of Design]] it was a share in principles.[2]

In his 2011 study *Samuel F.B. Morse's Gallery of the Louvre*, Beaudry wrote that Morse's design in [[gallery-of-the-louvre|that painting]] was "the passion of communicating to mankind the universal actions of discovery that the Italian Renaissance had made," particularly the power of immortality he had found in Italy around the themes of the Crucifixion and Resurrection, which became the central reference point of the work and Morse's contribution to the immortality of his species.[2]
