# Paul Fitzgerald

Paul Fitzgerald is an American journalist, documentary filmmaker and author who, together with his wife and writing partner [[elizabeth-gould|Elizabeth Gould]], produced first-hand reporting from Soviet-occupied Afghanistan and later wrote the book [[valediction-three-nights-of-desmond|Valediction: Three Nights of Desmond]], published by Trine Day Press.[1] Fitzgerald and Gould are described as the only two American journalists permitted to enter the war-torn country in 1981, and again in 1983.[1]

## Reporting from Afghanistan

The two documentaries the couple produced during that period are credited with going far to shatter the carefully constructed narrative of a "Russian Vietnam" that had been built up for years by what the account calls a western deep state.[1] Their reporting placed Afghanistan's story inside the wider frame of world history rather than treating it as an isolated Cold War episode.[1]

Preparation for the 1981 camera trip involved Fitzgerald and Gould interfacing with a network of highly placed agents in dominant positions within the [[state-department|State Department]] and the media industrial complex, whose overlap with the murder and cover-up of President [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] and with the management of the earlier [[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]] the authors found shocking.[1]

## The Lowenstein encounter

Fitzgerald's story, as told in Valediction, begins with a chance encounter with [[allard-lowenstein|Allard (Al) Lowenstein]], chief of staff to presidential candidate [[edward-kennedy|Edward "Ted" Kennedy]], in the run-up to the 1980 elections.[1] In their brief exchange Lowenstein described his and Kennedy's intention to shed light on the [[cia|CIA]]'s involvement in the murder of the two Kennedy brothers.[1] When Lowenstein was shot dead in his office by a former colleague two weeks later, Fitzgerald and Gould began to realise they were pressing on something much larger than themselves.[1]

## Two Americas thesis

Taking the reader through their journey of discovery, the couple relay how they grappled with the startling conclusion that there was not one United States but two opposing factions of U.S. intelligence at war with each other.[1] That clash turned on whether American foreign policy would presuppose an honest Soviet intention to adhere to détente and the 1972 [[salt-i|SALT]] treaty, or whether U.S. security doctrine would assume the Soviets were liars intent on imposing global world government.[1] The pair documented the 1976 rise of [[team-b|Team B]] and its revival of the [[committee-on-the-present-danger|Committee on the Present Danger]] as the mechanism by which the second view triumphed.[1]

The authors also traced the assassination of U.S. Ambassador [[adolph-dubs|Adolph Dubs]] in Kabul, arguing that evidence points to western intelligence rather than Soviet or Afghan forces, and that Dubs had been pursuing an agenda opposed to [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]] designs for the region.[1]
