# Adam Grant

Adam Grant is an organizational psychologist and writer who authored an August 2023 [[new-york-times|New York Times]] opinion piece arguing that the United States would be better governed if public officials were chosen by lottery rather than elected.[1]

## "The Worst People Run for Office"

Published on the eve of the first debate of the 2024 presidential race, the essay observed that trust in government was rivaling historic lows and argued that, while officials had worked to safeguard elections and assure citizens of their integrity, genuine integrity in public office might be better served by eliminating elections altogether.[1] Grant invoked ancient Athens, where many officials were selected by [[sortition|sortition]] — a random lottery from a pool of candidates — and the American jury system as precedents, asking what would happen if mayors, governors, legislators, justices and even presidents were chosen the same way.[1]

He cited experiments led by the psychologist Alexander Haslam finding that groups made smarter decisions when leaders were chosen at random than when they were elected or selected on leadership skill, because randomly chosen leaders led more democratically; systematically selected leaders, the researchers suggested, tend to "assert their personal superiority."[1] A leader picked at random, Grant wrote, does not experience enough power to be corrupted by it and instead feels a heightened sense of responsibility, and group members were more likely to stand by decisions such leaders made.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that the article was first published under the headline "Elections are Bad for Democracy" before the Times changed it, and characterized the argument as open advocacy for "selections" in place of elections.[1] The critique focused on the absence of any mechanism: Grant proposed that candidates be vetted and tested to "improve our odds of avoiding the worst candidates in the first place," but never identified who would do the vetting — which, in this reading, amounts to the death of representative government and the abandonment of consent as a requirement for governance.[1] The commentary also objected that Grant cited national division and congressional incivility as grounds for abolishing elections while writing in an outlet the Badlands team holds responsible for manufacturing that division, describing the pattern as an establishment outlet creating a problem and then selling the regime's preferred solution.[1] Whether the idea would "go [[operation-mockingbird|mockingbird]]" across other outlets was offered as the test of how seriously it was meant.[1]
