# Federal Communications Commission

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) is the U.S. regulatory agency responsible for licensing broadcast television and radio stations. In July 2026 the agency became central to a dispute after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said television networks that declined to air his primetime White House address on election security should have their broadcast licenses revoked.[1]

FCC Commissioner [[anna-gomez|Anna Gomez]] said the following day that Trump's proposal was unconstitutional, stating that broadcasters are not subject to FCC penalties for choosing not to air a presidential speech.[1] Reuters reported that legal experts said broadcasters have First Amendment discretion over programming decisions and that revoking a broadcast license would require a formal administrative process.[1]

## Equal-time enforcement and the Colbert dispute (2026)

In February 2026 the FCC's enforcement of the [[fcc-equal-time-rule|equal-time rule]] became a public controversy when [[stephen-colbert|Stephen Colbert]] criticized the commission as "bullies" on his [[cbs|CBS]] program, saying network lawyers had told him Rep. [[james-talarico|James Talarico]] could not appear on air.[2] CBS said the guidance concerned the risk of triggering equal-time obligations for two other candidates, including Rep. [[jasmine-crockett|Jasmine Crockett]].[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that equal-time regulations "have always been a thing" but simply "weren't enforced by the FCC during the age of Trump," and that broadcasters accepting government licenses and protections must abide by the accompanying rules.[2]

FCC authority also framed the February 2026 media consolidation story: because [[paramount-global|Paramount]] holds broadcast assets and licenses, any [[warner-bros-discovery|Warner Bros. Discovery]] transaction involving them would require regulatory approval.[2]

## Senate scrutiny of the Paramount–Warner merger (2026)

On March 23, 2026, a group of Democratic senators led by [[cory-booker|Cory Booker]] and including [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] and Elizabeth Warren wrote to the FCC demanding a full and independent investigation into the proposed $111 billion [[paramount-warner-bros-discovery-merger|Paramount Skydance–Warner Bros. Discovery merger]].[3] The senators pressed the commission to reject Paramount Skydance's claim that the roughly $25 billion in foreign investment behind the deal — including Gulf sovereign wealth funds and [[tencent|Tencent]] — requires only minimal scrutiny, insisting instead on rigorous review under the Communications Act and on limits governing foreign ownership of broadcast licenses.[3] They also urged the FCC to coordinate with the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]], the Committee on Foreign Investments in the United States, and intelligence agencies in determining whether the arrangement serves the public interest.[3]

## Digital equity rulemaking dispute (2023)

In November 2023, FCC Commissioner [[brendan-carr|Brendan Carr]] warned that the federal government was preparing to "micromanage nearly every aspect of how the internet functions," citing pressure from President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] on the commission to approve rules drawn from a one-page section of the 2021 [[infrastructure-investment-and-jobs-act|Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act]] that Carr said contained "new rules of breathtaking scope, all in the name of 'digital equity'."[4] Carr blamed the administration's economic policies for the skyrocketing costs of building internet infrastructure and for failing broadband policies, arguing that regulatory red tape had led the commission to needlessly block and delay new broadband builds even while seeking to expand 5G services.[4]

The commission was ostensibly created to safeguard citizens from the telecommunications sector, but critics within the [[truth-community|truth community]] have long considered it captured by the deep administrative state.[4] Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] compared the FCC to the ATF as an agency with a long history of corruption — from what he characterized as fatally flawed standards on radiation from wireless technologies to shielding industry from scrutiny — while crediting Carr with publicizing a plan that would otherwise have gone unnoticed.[4]

## Emergency alerting

The FCC jointly plans and conducts nationwide tests of the emergency alert system with the [[fema|Federal Emergency Management Agency]], and it maintains the consumer guidance describing how wireless emergency alerts are used to warn about dangerous weather, missing children, and other critical situations.[5] The two agencies conducted the [[2023-nationwide-emergency-alert-test|2023 nationwide emergency alert test]] on October 4, 2023, which reached cell phones nationwide along with radios and televisions and was described as the third nationwide test of the wireless system but only the second aimed at all cellphones.[5] The wireless alerts system had been used more than 84,000 times since its 2012 launch.[5]

## Approval of the Skydance–Paramount merger (July 2025)

On July 24, 2025, the FCC said it would allow [[paramount-global|Paramount]] to merge with the Hollywood studio [[skydance-media|Skydance]], clearing the way for one of the most highly scrutinized media deals in a decade.[6] Chairman [[brendan-carr|Brendan Carr]] said in a statement that the agency approved the deal after receiving assurances from Skydance that the new company was committed to unbiased journalism and would not establish programs related to diversity, equity and inclusion.[6]

"Americans no longer trust the legacy national news media to report fully, accurately and fairly," Carr said. "It is time for a change. That is why I welcome Skydance's commitment to make significant changes at the once storied CBS broadcast network."[6] Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] responded that critics of the conditions were "angry that they're being forced to end their discriminatory practices."[6]

## 2024 CBS news-distortion complaint

In October 2024 the FCC received a formal complaint from the [[center-for-american-rights|Center for American Rights]] charging [[cbs-news|CBS News]] with "significant and intentional news distortion" over its editing of the [[60-minutes|60 Minutes]] interview with [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]], an episode covered at length in the [[2024-cbs-60-minutes-harris-interview-edit|60 Minutes interview edit]] controversy.[7] Badlands' analysis stressed the agency's structure as the decisive factor: the FCC rules by committee, with five commissioners appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, and at the time the balance was 3–2 Democrat — chair [[jessica-rosenworcel|Jessica Rosenworcel]], Geoffrey Starks and Anna Gomez against Republicans [[brendan-carr|Brendan Carr]] and Nathan Simington.[7]

Because the public comments about CBS's potential liability came from one of the outnumbered Republican commissioners, the brief expressed skepticism that the complaint would advance beyond rhetoric, at least until after January 20, 2025.[7] The commentary added that while the FCC "goes hard on regulating the internet — and independent creators — they throw up their hands when it comes to accountability for MSM deception."[7]

## Denial of Starlink's rural broadband subsidy, December 2023

On December 12, 2023, the Federal Communications Commission reaffirmed its 2022 decision denying SpaceX's Starlink unit $885.5 million in rural broadband subsidies, rejecting the company's challenge.[8] The agency held that Starlink had failed to meet basic program requirements and could not show it would deliver the promised service, with Chair Jessica Rosenworcel saying the commission "followed a careful legal, technical and policy review to determine that this applicant had failed to meet its burden."[8] The FCC cited SpaceX's failure to successfully launch Starship, warning that "the uncertain nature of Starship's future launches could impact Starlink's ability to meet" its obligations; the original August 2022 rescission had rested on speed-test data after Starlink agreed to serve 642,000 rural homes and businesses in 35 states.[8]

The two Republican members of the five-member commission dissented, arguing the agency was improperly enforcing 2025 targets three years early and attributing the outcome to the Biden administration's animus toward Elon Musk.[8] Commissioner Brendan Carr accused the FCC of joining "the growing list of administrative agencies that are taking action against Elon Musk's businesses" in a decision that "certainly fits the Biden Administration's pattern of regulatory harassment."[8]

## Carr Chairmanship and Big Tech Inquiry (2024)

In November 2024 President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] named senior Republican commissioner [[brendan-carr|Brendan Carr]] as permanent chairman of the commission, describing him as a "warrior for free speech."[9] Ahead of that announcement Carr used the agency's letterhead to press Google, Microsoft, Meta and Apple for information about their role in what he termed a "censorship cartel," and about their commercial relationships with the ratings firm [[newsguard|NewsGuard]], setting a December 10 deadline for responses.[9] The inquiry signaled that the commission under Carr would treat platform content moderation and third-party "fact-checking" partnerships as a central regulatory concern.[9]

Shortly before the November 2024 election, Carr — then one of five commissioners, having been nominated to the body by both Trump and [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] — said that Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]]'s surprise cameo on [[saturday-night-live|Saturday Night Live]] alongside [[maya-rudolph|Maya Rudolph]] may have violated the commission's "equal time" rule, which governs equitable broadcast access for political candidates.[10] The FCC is the federal agency responsible for regulating radio and television in the United States.[10]

## Rejection of the SpaceX subsidy and the Musk "green light" claim (2023)

In December 2023 the FCC rejected a $900 million rural broadband subsidy for [[spacex|SpaceX]] amid concerns about government weaponization, and Commissioner [[brendan-carr|Brendan Carr]] stated in a post on X that "President Biden gave federal agencies the green light to go after him [Musk]."[11] Coverage in [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] treated the remark as further proof that the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration was directing agencies against [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]], noting Musk's own September comments to podcast host David Sacks about "a significant increase in the weaponization of government and really sort of misuse of prosecutorial discretion," and his view that "aspects of interests aligned with President Biden ... probably do not wish good things for me."[11]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] linked the episode to the wave of lawsuits filed by Musk through [[x-corp|X]], [[rumble|Rumble]], [[truth-social|Truth Social]] and the State of Texas against the [[censorship-industrial-complex|censorship industrial complex]], arguing that the FCC commissioner's confirmation supplied additional context for coordination between federal agencies and private enforcement arms posing as independent market actors.[11]
