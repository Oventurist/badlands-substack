# Pecora Commission

The Pecora Commission was the United States Senate investigation into the causes of the [[stock-market-crash-of-1929|1929 stock market crash]], named for its counsel Ferdinand Pecora.[1] In 1933 it investigated [[charles-e-mitchell|Charles E. Mitchell]] as its first witness for his part in tens of millions of dollars in losses, excessive pay, and tax avoidance.[1]

Pecora and his investigative team revealed that banks could lend money to a company and then issue stock in that same company without disclosing the bank's underlying conflict of interest; if the company then failed, the bank suffered no losses while its investors were left holding the bag.[1] Testimony also uncovered that [[national-city-bank|National City Bank]] had taken on bundles of bad loans, packaged them as securities and unloaded them on unsuspecting customers, and that Mitchell had made more than $1 million in bonuses in 1929 while paying zero taxes.[1] A top executive of Chase National Bank, a precursor of [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]], was shown to have grown rich by short-selling his company's shares during the crash, and financier J.P. Morgan was revealed to have issued stocks at discounted rates to a small circle of privileged clients, including former President Calvin Coolidge.[1]

The hearings captivated an increasingly disgusted American public, which began to refer to these men as "banksters" — a term coined for financial leaders who put the nation's economy at risk while pocketing profits.[1] The findings underpinned passage of the [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall Act]].[1]

## Role in FDR's war on Wall Street

[[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]] supported the Pecora Commission as the opening move in his war on Wall Street; according to [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] it sent thousands of bankers to prison and exposed the criminal activities of the top tier of Wall Street's power structure, which had manipulated the depression by buying political offices and pushing fascism.[2] Ferdinand Pecora, who ran the commission, said that "this small group of highly placed financiers, controlling the very springs of economic activity, holds more real power than any similar group in the United States."[2] The commission's highly publicized success empowered Roosevelt to impose sweeping regulation in the form of [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]] bank separation, bankruptcy reorganization, and the creation of the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]].[2]

## Origins and conduct of the investigation

The commission grew out of the U.S. [[senate-committee-on-banking-and-currency|Committee on Banking and Currency]], established in 1932 by Senators [[peter-norbeck|Peter Norbeck]] and [[george-norris|George Norris]] at a moment when unemployment had reached 25 percent, over 40 percent of banks had failed and more than half of American industrial capacity had shut down.[3] The committee was a relatively impotent body until Norbeck called in [[ferdinand-pecora|Ferdinand Pecora]] to lead it in April 1932, after which Pecora — paid $250 a month — was granted broad subpoena powers to audit banks and compel the most powerful men in America to testify.[3]

Within his first two weeks Pecora forced [[charles-e-mitchell|Charles Mitchell]], then preparing to advise [[benito-mussolini|Benito Mussolini]], to admit short-selling his own bank's stock, scamming depositors with Cuban junk debt and years of tax avoidance; Mitchell resigned and New York Stock Exchange chairman [[richard-whitney|Dick Whitney]] left the court in handcuffs days later.[3] Similar displays of corruption were extracted from the heads of Kuhn Loeb, Chase Bank and [[brown-brothers-harriman|Brown Brothers Harriman]], and Roosevelt personally encouraged Pecora to take on [[jp-morgan-jr|J.P. Morgan Jr.]] of the [[house-of-morgan|House of Morgan]].[3] Unlike the earlier [[pujo-committee|Pujo Commission]] of 1911, the Pecora inquiry was backed by a president who amplified its powers; when warned that the exposures would hurt the economy, Roosevelt answered, "they should have thought of that when they did the things that are being exposed now."[3]

The commission's revelations also helped defeat the [[business-plot|fascist coup]] plotted against Roosevelt using General [[smedley-butler|Smedley Butler]], and paved the way for the [[new-deal|New Deal]] reforms of the first hundred days.[3]
