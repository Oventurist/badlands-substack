# United States Navy

The United States Navy is the maritime service branch of the U.S. armed forces. In December 2023 it became the central subject of a debate over whether American naval deterrence had collapsed in the [[red-sea-shipping-crisis|Red Sea]] amid sustained attacks by the [[houthis|Houthi]] movement on commercial shipping.[1]

## Red Sea deterrence debate

By late December 2023, commercial container ships transiting the Red Sea were coming under drone and missile attack on a daily basis, triggering delays and rising goods prices as major shipping lines avoided the route altogether.[1] The [[wall-street-journal|Wall Street Journal]] ran the headline "U.S. Naval Deterrence Is Going, Going, Maybe Even Gone," pointing to the reality and immediate consequences of the U.S. Navy coming under attack while failing to respond or decisively hit back.[1]

Commentary aggregated by [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] noted that the commander-in-chief, [[joe-biden|President Joe Biden]], had not ordered a military response even when American warships in waters off Yemen came under direct attack themselves.[1] Defense officials cited by [[politico|Politico]] were frustrated that the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] was being held back from responding forcefully against Houthi positions in Yemen by the Biden White House, with some officials arguing the Iranian proxies would only grow more emboldened.[1] Analysts quoted in the coverage worried it was already too late to reestablish deterrence.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode in terms of the [[devolution|Devolution]] thesis, arguing that whatever was happening in the Red Sea was so far failing to trigger a U.S. military response under the framing of National Essential Functions, and that Biden either had no desire to intervene or — more likely in that view — no ability to do so.[1] He said he had assigned [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] to the story ahead of the following week's Power Hour.[1]

## Trump-class battleship program

The Navy's announcement that it would begin implementing the [[trump-class-battleship|Trump-class battleship]] project drew shock and criticism in Western specialized defense publications, which argued that the real challenges facing the service dictated essentially the opposite decision.[2] Estimates placed the cost of a single ship at $10–15 billion, comparable to the $12.8 billion nuclear aircraft carrier Gerald R. Ford, while the design's strike capabilities were judged narrow relative to the alternative of building roughly five times as many destroyers.[2] Analysts contended the program benefits [[china|China]], which already fields more ships than the U.S. Navy and was projected to hold a 1.5-fold advantage in pennants by 2030, and [[russia|Russia]], whose counter-Navy strategy has long rested on a submarine fleet aimed at large formations such as carrier strike groups.[2]

## Recruiting crisis and lowered education standards (2024)

In late January 2024 the Navy announced it would begin enlisting applicants who had not graduated from high school, the latest attempt to fill its ranks amid a recruiting crisis affecting every branch of the U.S. military.[3] Under the new policy, recruits without a high school diploma may join provided they score 50 or higher on the Armed Forces Qualification Test (AFQT), the exam used to determine basic eligibility, on which the maximum possible score is 99.[3] Applicants lacking a General Educational Development (GED) credential — a four-subject battery certifying skills equivalent to a high school graduate — may likewise enlist if they meet the same AFQT threshold.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] disputed the Navy's framing that COVID-19 school dropout rates were the cause, arguing instead that qualified military-aged American men are deliberately opting out of service, a shift she attributed to the change in commander in chief from [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[3] She described her own son abandoning a lifelong plan to enlist in the Army after the 2020 election and the military vaccine mandates, and predicted that if relaxed standards fail to produce enough recruits the administration would reinstate selective service and the draft.[3] Conscription worked in World War II because no one doubted the commander in chief's allegiance to America, she argued, worked less well in Vietnam, and would fail in a future major war because the speed of information access means young people are questioning not merely a given war but "the war machine" and the legitimacy of those selling it.[3]
