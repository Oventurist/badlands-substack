# German Christians

The **German Christians** (Deutsche Christen) were a pro-Nazi church movement that emerged alongside [[adolf-hitler|Adolf Hitler]]'s 1933 acquisition of totalitarian power as Germany's Führer. In Matthew Ehret's account, the movement functioned as an exoteric pseudo-Christian front promoted for the uninitiated masses, while the inner elites of the Nazi [[schutzstaffel|SS]] pursued a Germanic Luciferian occultism derived from the Theosophy of [[helena-blavatsky|Helena Blavatsky]].[1]

## Doctrine and leadership

The Berlin leader of the German Christians was Dr. Reinhold Kraus, who demanded that Christianity be redefined in accord with the ambitions of the thousand-year Reich envisioned by the Nazi high command.[1] Kraus held that the church must be purged of everything "Un-German in worship and confession" and demanded "liberation from the Old Testament with its Jewish morality of profit and its stories of cattle traders and pimps."[1]

## Relationship to the Reichs Bible

The movement's program was realized in the 1939 [[reichs-bible|Reichs Bible]], which excised the Old Testament, rewrote New Testament passages referencing Jewish heritage, deleted the commandments against killing and stealing, and added new commandments including "honor your Führer and master."[1] Under the German Christian framework, the German churches were made extensions of the state, loyal to the surrogate god of the state itself.[1] This view was articulated by [[hans-kerrl|Hans Kerrl]], the Nazi Minister of Church Affairs, who declared that "true Christianity is represented by the party" and that "the Fuehrer is the herald of a new revelation."[1]

## Assessment

Ehret argues that the German Christians were not a genuine Christian revival but a control mechanism, noting Hitler's private statements that "National Socialism and Christianity are irreconcilable" — a formulation also used by his secretary Martin Bormann — and the parallel campaign to replace Christian rituals with Nordic pagan substitutes such as the Jul Fest, SS marriage consecrations, and Nazi name-giving ceremonies.[1]
