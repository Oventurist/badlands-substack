# Richard Secord

Richard Secord was a US military officer and covert operations figure who, with Oliver North, used Swiss bank accounts to launder large sums during the [[iran-contra-affair]].[1] Joe Lange notes that the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] has made extensive use of Swiss banks, and that Switzerland has been a banking hub for criminals for many years.[1]

Secord was also named in connection with the [[nugan-hand-bank]]. In 1983 the Australian Joint Task Force on Drug Trafficking reported to Parliament that [[ted-shackley]], Secord, [[thomas-clines]], [[rafael-quintero]] and [[edwin-wilson]] were people whose background "is relevant to a proper understanding of the activities of the Nugan Hand group and the people associated with that group."[1] None of these names appeared in media reporting on the bank's collapse, which Lange attributes to their status as protected "untouchables."[1]

## Military and Iranian background

Secord's work with the CIA and his friendships with agency personnel date from the 1960s, when he served in Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos.[2] A decorated Air Force veteran who flew 285 combat missions, he is reported to have been involved in covert US bombing and other actions in Southeast Asia during the Vietnam War — bombings Joe Lange suspects were concentrated in the [[secret-war-in-laos|secret war in Laos]], where [[ted-shackley|Shackley]], [[donald-gregg|Gregg]] and [[felix-rodriguez|Rodriguez]] were also operating.[2] From 1975 to 1978 he headed the US Air Force military assistance group in Iran while the Shah was building up his air force with American firepower, and he was said to be on a first-name basis with most of the Iranian armed forces leadership.[2] Company flight logs subpoenaed by federal investigators show that in the early to mid-1970s [[southern-air-transport|Southern Air Transport]] shuttled cargo for the Shah's air force, the CIA-owned airline's highest-paying client other than the U.S. government.[2]

## The 1972 thesis

Secord's 1972 Master's thesis, written under a thesis director who was the CIA adviser to the head of the U.S. Naval War College, was titled "Unconventional Warfare/Covert Operations as an Instrument of U.S. Foreign Policy."[2] In the 55-page document he argued the CIA should be given "the authority" to run all such operations, that the Pentagon should be "removed from the chain of command," and that "parochial outcries" from competing government groups should be ignored.[2] "Bureaucratic obstacles should be dismissed out of hand," he wrote, as should opposition from "the press, [[congress|Congress]], academia and others," with a high-level group — "probably the [[national-security-council|National Security Council]]" — developing and implementing the required programs.[2] Lange observes that the thesis reads as a blueprint for what actually happened during [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]], and calls Secord the fitting image of an elite class that believes it is above the law.[2]

## Iran-Contra role and sentence

A "knowledgeable Reagan administration official" quoted in the Chicago Tribune said Southern Air was the carrier of choice of Secord and [[oliver-north|Oliver North]], each of whom played a direct role in establishing a contra supply network, and that the men pressed contra leader Adolfo Calero to use the airline.[2] The Los Angeles Times described Secord the businessman as a privateer and international arms dealer specializing in secret shipping, shell companies and Swiss bank accounts, and as North's top lieutenant — a "black-ops" jack-of-all-trades who coordinated arms shipments to Iran, controlled the Swiss accounts for those transactions and allegedly used the profits to arm the contras while US military aid was illegal.[2] The Tower board reported that at least $32 million from the Iran arms deals and up to $40 million in alleged contributions for the contras were funnelled through Secord's accounts, and that what happened to most of the money remains a mystery.[2]

The [[justice-department|Justice Department]] asked Swiss authorities to assist in investigating both North and Secord over two Swiss bank accounts that may have been used to channel funds to the contras, and telephone records showed repeated calls to Secord's home and business from a "safe house" in El Salvador used by crew of the secret air resupply operation at a time when Congress had banned US military aid.[2] Secord denied wrongdoing, describing his role as that of an adviser, and invoked the Fifth Amendment before the Senate Intelligence Committee rather than answer questions.[2]

He also handled the sale of AWACS radar planes to the Saudis in 1981 before retiring, and congressional investigators concluded that he and his partner [[albert-hakim|Albert Hakim]] were involved in the covert contra supply program; Saudi officials sought to route $15 million to the rebels through the pair.[2] In January 1990 Secord, who ran the "shadowy enterprise" set up to channel money from the Iran arms sales to the contras, was sentenced to two years' probation: he faced up to five years in jail, but U.S. District Judge Aubrey E. Robinson Jr. said prison would be too harsh, and Secord pleaded guilty to one felony count of misleading Congress in a plea bargain dismissing eleven other charges, paying the minimum $50 fee.[2]

## The Costa Rica airstrip companies

Reporting cited in "School of Assassins and Dictators" places Secord at the head of the corporate layer that built the covert supply infrastructure in Central America.[3] The secret 7,000-foot airstrip in Costa Rica used by the Nicaraguan rebels was built by [[udall-research|Udall Research]], a Panama-based company since dissolved that was closely tied to other firms administering secret Swiss bank accounts funnelling profits from arms sales to Iran and setting up the covert supply network.[3] Those companies "appear to have been overseen in part by retired Maj. Gen. Richard V. Secord, a key figure in the Iran-contra arms affair."[3] The network was exposed when a rebel plane was shot down over Nicaragua in October 1986.[3]
