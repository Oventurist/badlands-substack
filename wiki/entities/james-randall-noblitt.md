# James Randall Noblitt

Dr. James Randall Noblitt is an American clinical psychologist described by Badlands Media as one of the two modern clinical authorities — alongside [[alison-miller|Alison Miller]] — on [[ritual-abuse|ritual abuse]] and trauma-based mind control.[1] "Gazing Into the Abyss pt.1," the July 21, 2023 introduction to a Badlands series on cult ritual abuse and mind control, opened with a dedication to Noblitt, crediting his "bravery and compassion in taking on these patients" with making both the series and a forthcoming documentary possible.[1]

## Career

Noblitt is a Professor of Clinical Psychology in the Clinical Psy.D. Program and Coordinator for the Multi-Interest Option of the Clinical Ph.D. and PsyD Programs at the Los Angeles campus of [[alliant-international-university|Alliant International University]].[1] He earned a Ph.D. in Clinical Psychology from the University of North Texas as an Air Force Institute of Technology Scholar, and completed his clinical internship at the USAF Medical Center at Wright-Patterson AFB, Ohio.[1] He subsequently served as a [[united-states-air-force|U.S. Air Force]] clinical psychologist at Lakenheath AB, England, and at Sheppard AFB Regional Hospital in Texas.[1] In 1984 he opened a clinical psychology practice in Dallas, Texas, which he maintained until moving to Los Angeles in 2006 to join the Alliant faculty.[1]

He is the author of *Cult and Ritual Abuse: Its History, Anthropology, and Recent Discovery in Contemporary America*, from which the case narratives recounted in the Badlands series are drawn.[1]

## Clinical encounters with ritual abuse

Noblitt has written that the initial professional response to allegations of ritualized abuse was incredulity: while it is standard practice that all psychiatric patients be respectfully heard, there was at the time no compelling reason to treat the claims as literal.[1] Over time, however, a remarkable number of similar reports emerged from patients presenting very similar psychiatric symptoms — multiple inner identities, powerful trance reactions, and psychogenic amnesia among them.[1]

### "The Church in Thetford Forest" (1979)

Noblitt's first brush with what he called the occult underground came in 1979 while he was stationed as a psychologist at an Air Force mental health installation at RAF Lakenheath, England.[1] A sergeant identified as "Bill" described a party in an abandoned church in Thetford Forest attended by British nationals and U.S. military personnel, which degenerated into an orgy in which some participants donned dark hooded robes and chanted to Satan; Bill alleged he was raped there.[1] What drove Bill to counseling was not the occult trappings but a fear that his failure to resist meant he was a latent homosexual, a worry he eventually resolved.[1] Noblitt concluded at the time that the group simply used demonic imagery to heighten a "forbidden fruit" fantasy, and — finding little published literature on occult-inspired sexual practices — assumed he would never see such a patient again.[1]

### "Little Susie," Billy and the "Evil One"

After leaving the Air Force, Noblitt entered private practice in a suburb of Dallas, where he worked extensively with patients diagnosed with borderline personality disorder.[1] Consulting on a married woman in her thirties called "Susie," hospitalized for intractable headaches, he observed *la belle indifférence* — an incongruous lack of concern about her presenting complaint — and administered the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, which returned results consistent with BPD.[1] As trust developed, Susie disclosed that her father was an alcoholic who had abused her physically and sexually.[1]

Noblitt, then skeptical that [[dissociative-identity-disorder|multiple personality disorder]] was a legitimate diagnosis, asked Susie to visualize comforting her childhood self; she opened her eyes and greeted him in a soft, high-pitched, childlike voice as "Doctor Randy," identifying herself as "Little Susie," aged four.[1] The adult Susie afterward had no recollection of the exchange, leaving Noblitt "puzzled and confused" by what appeared to be a textbook case of a diagnosis he did not accept.[1] Further [[alter-personalities|alters]] followed — children, teenagers and adults, male and female — narrating compartmentalized memories of sadistic abuse.[1] A male alter named "Billy" insisted the other fragments' accounts were fabricated before vanishing abruptly; Badlands notes that such "enforcer" alters are common markers of sophisticated ritual abuse and mind control.[1] Another hostile male alter announced, "I am the 'Evil One,'" which Noblitt only much later understood to be an internal representation of the devil; roughly two years into therapy, accounts of ritual abuse in a satanic cult began to emerge.[1]

### "Sharon" and the star

A later patient, "Sharon," also presented initially as a straightforward borderline case.[1] During a Sunday emergency session she produced a razor blade from her purse and sliced open her left forearm from elbow to wrist, responding to the injury with an expression of ecstasy rather than pain, then ordering Noblitt to "Back off!" in a low masculine voice before shifting into an apologetic, childlike persona.[1] Strapped to a gurney in the adjacent hospital's emergency room, she repeated in a deep hoarse voice, "Kill the bitch."[1] Hospital staff concluded she had suffered a psychotic episode, and she rarely presented alters in front of nurses, explaining to Noblitt that "people inside aren't s'posed to be out" because "Sharon's father said so … when the body was little."[1]

Noblitt asked his dissociative patients to keep journals and found that their handwriting changed dramatically when an alter was present, and that many produced geometric and occult designs — sometimes inducing trance in other patients who viewed them.[1] Free-associating to simple drawings, Sharon responded to a star with the word "Sex," eventually disclosing that her father drew a star in blood on her abdomen before raping her.[1] A child alter then emerged calling herself "Sacrifice," named for her role as the "sexual sacrifice" at rituals held in her family home, usually on Friday evenings and typically with both parents present.[1] Sharon was later overprescribed antipsychotics and subjected to electroconvulsive therapy; Noblitt's objection to that treatment led her psychiatrist to end the consultations.[1]

### The group trance

In a hospital group for MPD patients, a patient named "Karen" reacted angrily when Noblitt questioned another patient about a biofeedback therapist's phrase "deeper and deeper."[1] Moments later Karen herself was in a deep trance — as was every other member of the group, all staring glassy-eyed at the floor, something Noblitt had never witnessed in eleven years of group therapy.[1] Clinicians later recognized this as a programmed response of the kind produced by [[psychogenic-triggers|psychogenic triggers]].[1]
