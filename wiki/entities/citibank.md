# Citibank

Citibank, formerly National City Bank and First National City Bank, is the New York commercial bank that Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] describes as "the most corrupt bank in American history."[1] In Lange's telling the institution is a "cabal bank" from its inception, one whose recurring rescues by "friends in high places" gave the article its title.[1]

## Role in the creation of the Federal Reserve

Lange writes that Citibank worked behind the scenes to create America's third central bank, the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], and was rewarded for doing so.[1] A provision of the Federal Reserve Act permitted American banks to open branches in foreign countries, which he credits with allowing Citibank to grow into the global behemoth it became — and with allowing its corruption to spread worldwide.[1] Controlling New York City or Wall Street, he argues, does not compare to controlling a government and an entire economy through a centralized banking system, which gives the elite the power to make the rules and reap the benefits.[1]

## Haiti

The article's central case study is [[haiti|Haiti]].[1] Citing a 2022 New York Times investigation, Lange recounts that in December 1914 eight American Marines walked out of the headquarters of Haiti's national bank with $500,000 in gold packed in wooden boxes, carried it by wagon to the shore past American soldiers in civilian clothes, and loaded it onto a gunboat; the gold was in the vault of a Wall Street bank within days.[1] The operation was a precursor to the full-scale invasion of Haiti the following summer, beginning a 19-year military occupation — one of the longest in American history — after which Haiti remained under the control of American financial officers for a further 13 years.[1]

According to the Times reporting quoted in the article, National City Bank, Citigroup's predecessor, pressed the United States to step in and seize control of Haiti for the wealth it promised; the Americans elbowed the French aside, dissolved Haiti's parliament at gunpoint, killed thousands of people, controlled the country's finances for more than 30 years, and shipped a large portion of its earnings to bankers in New York.[1] United Nations officials determined in 1949 that Haitian farmers who generated those profits often lived on a diet "close to starvation level."[1] Lange states the parliament was dissolved because Haitian legislators refused to change their constitution to allow foreigners to own land.[1]

The bank's Haitian national bank successor charged the government for every deposit and expense while generating profits for foreign shareholders, and issued a loan on which Haiti received roughly $9 million but owed nearly $12.3 million in face value.[1] In a 1932 filing to the Senate Finance Committee, National City Bank said it had secured one of its largest margins during the 1920s from a debt it controlled in Haiti; Lange notes Haiti is barely mentioned in Citigroup's official timeline today and that the company declined to provide access to its archives.[1]

## Customs houses and political patronage

Lange argues that Citibank's control of Haiti extended to the customs houses that handled import and export taxes, giving it command of the country's entire commerce.[1] He connects this to the bank's earlier experience with the [[new-york-custom-house|United States Custom House]] in New York, where as the biggest bank in the city it controlled the politicians who appointed the customs house's leadership.[1] He cites the assassination of President [[james-garfield|James Garfield]], who had tried to break Senator [[roscoe-conkling|Roscoe Conkling]]'s patronage grip on the customs house, as an event he does not believe was a coincidence.[1]

## The 1929 crash and Glass-Steagall

Lange holds Citibank responsible for the [[stock-market-crash-of-1929|market crash of 1929]] that triggered the Great Depression, singling out National City Bank chairman [[charles-e-mitchell|"Sunshine" Charley Mitchell]].[1] The bank's activities under Mitchell's leadership were a major contributing factor in the passage of the [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall Act]], and the [[pecora-commission|Pecora Commission]] made Mitchell its first witness in 1933.[1] Testimony revealed that National City Bank had taken bundles of bad loans, packaged them as securities, and unloaded them on unsuspecting customers — a "template," Lange writes, that the bank would reuse in the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 financial crisis]].[1]

## Deregulation, Citigroup and the bailouts

The 1999 repeal of Glass-Steagall allowed the merger of Citibank and Travelers — owner of the investment banks Salomon Brothers and Smith Barney — creating [[citigroup|Citigroup]], which Lange says led directly to the 2008 global financial crisis and to the largest bailout in world history.[1] Reviewing the book *Borrowed Time* by James Freeman and Vern McKinley, the New York Times put the total Citigroup rescue at more than $517 billion, some $40 billion above the roughly $476 billion in cash and guarantees described in a 2011 congressional report, and argued that across the bank's 206-year history it had repeatedly teetered on ruin only to be rescued by friends in high places.[1]
