# Kalshi

Kalshi is a regulated prediction market platform that allows users to trade contracts on politics, elections, sports, and current events. It features in the [[badlands-brief-0fb|April 24, 2026 Badlands Brief]] as the subject of the first insider trading enforcement action in its history.

## 2026 insider trading bans

According to the source, Kalshi suspended three political candidates and issued its first-ever insider trading bans after they placed wagers on their own 2026 primary races. The candidates were Mark Moran, an Independent running in the Virginia U.S. Senate Democratic primary; Matt Klein, a Democrat in a Minnesota primary race; and Zeke Enriquez, a Republican in a Texas primary race. All three were flagged under newly implemented safeguards designed to block political candidates from trading on their own contests.

Kalshi's head of enforcement said that any trade by a candidate who can influence a market outcome violates platform rules regardless of the size of the position, characterized the cases as political insider trading, and confirmed that the matters were not referred to the [[commodity-futures-trading-commission|Commodity Futures Trading Commission]] or the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]].

Moran stated on X that he had bet on himself deliberately in order to be caught and thereby draw attention to Kalshi, which he described as destructive to young men. He vowed that as a senator he would impose a 25% "vice tax" on the platform to help reduce the national debt.

## Scrutiny of prediction markets

The source places the enforcement actions amid growing scrutiny of prediction markets such as Kalshi and [[polymarket|Polymarket]], with lawmakers warning that large stakes on elections could incentivize interference or otherwise undermine the electoral process.

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argues in the Brief that even with insider trading occurring inside the predictive gambling markets, the results may be more reliable and legitimate — and are certainly more auditable — than the outcomes of United States elections. She adds that weighting the predictions has been a persistent problem, since political markets can swing thirty percentage points in a day as political action committees flood the site, making prices "not an indicator of anything except where the money is." Because reported market outcomes can nonetheless sway voters, she suggests that manipulation of the betting markets may help awaken Americans to what she describes as fake elections.
