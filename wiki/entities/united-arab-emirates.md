# United Arab Emirates

The United Arab Emirates (UAE) is a Gulf federation led by President [[mohamed-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan]].[1]

## February 2026 succession rumors

The UAE became the focus of an international rumor wave in February 2026 when social media accounts claimed its president had died. The claims intensified after Turkish President [[recep-tayyip-erdogan|Recep Tayyip Erdogan]] publicly postponed a visit to Abu Dhabi citing the Emirati leader's "health problem," in a post that was quickly deleted. The Emirati and Turkish governments both issued statements describing a phone call between the two leaders, but no official confirmation of a death was ever produced.[1]

## Regional realignment

Badlands analysis situated the episode within an apparent effort to drive a wedge between the UAE and [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]], noting that both states had capitalized on the evolving dynamic to negotiate new bilateral agreements with regional powers including India and Pakistan.[1] Writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] concluded that the death rumors were most likely a ruse to sow confusion while those geopolitical arrangements were reshaped.[1]

## Exit from OPEC (2026)

The UAE was reported to be formally exiting [[opec|OPEC]] on May 1, 2026, after nearly six decades of membership — a move described as a major strategic fracture in the global oil order that weakened the cartel's ability to control supply and defend price floors.[2] Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the departure as a deliberate sovereign policy decision rather than an impulsive wartime defection, noting Abu Dhabi had invested heavily to expand capacity from roughly 3.4 million barrels per day toward 5 million by 2027.[2] By stepping outside the quota system, the Emirates were said to be signaling the end of the enforced scarcity model they once helped perpetuate, intending to supply rising world energy demand on their own terms.[2]

Russia downplayed the risk of an immediate price war, citing Iran-war disruptions and [[strait-of-hormuz|Hormuz]] constraints, but analysts expected that once Gulf exports normalized the UAE could ramp output beyond quota limits and force [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] into a market-share fight.[2] The move was framed as an objective of [[operation-epic-fury|Epic Fury]]: breaking Iran's leverage, weakening cartel coordination, and driving postwar oil prices lower.[2]

## Drone strike near the Barakah nuclear plant (May 2026)

Emirati authorities said a drone strike caused a fire near the [[barakah-nuclear-power-plant|Barakah nuclear power plant]] on 17 May 2026, with no injuries and no radioactive leaks.[3] The UAE defense ministry reported that three drones entered the country from the western border direction, two of which were intercepted while one struck an electrical generator outside the plant's inner perimeter in the Al Dhafra region.[3] The UAE blamed [[iran|Iran]] or allied proxy groups, and presidential adviser [[anwar-gargash|Anwar Gargash]] called the strike a "dangerous escalation" violating international law and norms, with investigations into the drones' source continuing.[3]

[[international-atomic-energy-agency|IAEA]] Director General [[rafael-grossi|Rafael Grossi]] said military activity threatening nuclear safety was "unacceptable" after discussions with Emirati officials, while the UAE's nuclear regulator reported no impact on reactor operations or public safety.[3] [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] condemned the attack and separately intercepted three drones approaching from Iraqi airspace.[3]

Badlands commentary disputed the attribution, noting that the UAE had joined the collective condemnation of Israeli aggression against Iran during the [[twelve-day-war|Twelve Day War]] and that the drones came from the west — the direction of [[israel|Israel]] and [[iraq|Iraq]], not Iran — arguing that the strike on a Muslim country's nuclear facility was intended to provoke war among Muslim nations to Israel's benefit.[3]

## Gulf AI partnerships and technology-transfer concerns (July 2026)

Some current and former US officials raised concerns that the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s expanding artificial intelligence and semiconductor partnerships with Gulf nations — particularly the UAE and [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] — could create pathways for advanced American technology or sensitive know-how to reach [[china|China]].[4] Critics argued that the Gulf states maintain significant commercial relationships with Chinese firms, making diversion or indirect technology transfer a long-term national security risk.[4] The concerns stemmed from the administration's broader strategy of deepening economic and technology ties through investments in AI infrastructure, data centers, and access to advanced US chips; supporters countered that bringing Gulf AI development into the US technology ecosystem strengthens American influence, attracts foreign investment, and reduces reliance on Chinese providers, with export controls, licensing requirements and security agreements intended to mitigate transfer risks.[4]

## Internal emirate factionalism

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] noted that the UAE is a federal semi-constitutional monarchy comprised of seven emirates, each maintaining its own sovereignty in parallel with the federal government, producing a natural rivalry between the kingdoms and a year of rumor and speculation about an internal power struggle.[4] A Financial Times report on the competing development plans of Dubai-based [[dp-world|DP World]] and Abu Dhabi-based AD Ports was cited as reinforcing that narrative, with Dubai apparently in a struggle against Abu Dhabi and many other emirates siding with Abu Dhabi.[4]

[[sultan-ahmed-bin-sulayem|Sultan Ahmed bin Sulayem]], DP World's longtime chairman and CEO, was involved in [[donald-trump|President Trump]]'s original Dubai real estate venture and was executive chairman of Nakheel when it announced its Trump Tower Dubai partnership with the Trump Organization.[4] An executive order Trump signed during his May 2025 UAE visit explicitly cited a $1.4 trillion UAE investment framework into US infrastructure, energy and technology, and the commentary argued that Gulf AI development honoring that order flies in the face of the [[state-department|State Department]] and [[deep-state|Deep State]], with potential Chinese involvement read as a signal of the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] theory.[4] The concern voiced by a former [[ted-cruz|Ted Cruz]] aide was taken as further evidence of factions within UAE leadership pursuing differing agendas, since the UAE is ostensibly a strong ally of [[israel|Israel]].[4]

## Announcement of the OPEC exit (April 2026)

On April 28, 2026 the UAE formally announced its withdrawal from [[opec|OPEC]] and OPEC+, presenting it as a unilateral policy decision following a review of its energy strategies and production policies.[5] Energy Minister [[suhail-al-mazrouei|Suhail Mohamed al-Mazrouei]] cited frustrations over insufficient [[gulf-cooperation-council|Gulf Cooperation Council]] support against Iranian attacks and threats during the conflict, and acknowledged the exit as a blow to OPEC unity amid the energy shock from [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] disruptions.[5] The announcement came the same day as U.S. statements on a possible reopening of the Strait, and Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the withdrawal as the first domino falling against a cartel built on engineered scarcity.[5]

## Response to the Hormuz disruption (July 2026)

As shipping through the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] collapsed to six vessels on one day in July 2026, the UAE reserved the right to respond to the disruption, while [[india|India]] summoned [[iran|Iran]]'s ambassador.[6] The episode unfolded as President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] abandoned his [[hormuz-transit-fee-proposal|20% transit fee proposal]] in favor of trade and investment deals with Gulf states.[6]

## 2024 suspension of coordination with Israel

In April 2024 the UAE announced a suspension of diplomatic coordination with
[[israel|Israel]] in the wake of the deaths of seven
[[world-central-kitchen|World Central Kitchen]] humanitarian workers in Gaza,
and halted all coordination on humanitarian aid.[7] International and Israeli
press reports described ties between the two states as near breaking point,
only a few short years after diplomatic normalization was hailed through the
[[abraham-accords|Abraham Accords]].[7] The announcement coincided with Israel
placing its embassies worldwide on high security alert over a heightened
Iranian response threat, and helped send Brent crude above $90 for the first
time since October.[7]

Badlands commentary framed the UAE as a valuable US ally and the closest ally
of [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]], noting the emirate's long-standing influence
in Washington through figures such as Ambassador
[[youssef-al-otaiba|Yousef Al Otaiba]], and describing the UAE as ruled by
[[mohamed-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Mohamed bin Zayed]] (MBZ), emir of Abu Dhabi and
president of the UAE.[7] MBZ was said to have been the leading advocate to the
Saudi royal family for making Mohammed bin Salman Crown Prince, and to have
stood with him and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] as they took on
[[qatar|Qatar]] and the [[muslim-brotherhood|Muslim Brotherhood]].[7]

## Trump's May 2025 visit

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] visited the UAE on May 15, 2025 as the latest stage of a Gulf tour, pledging to strengthen US ties with the Emirates in a visit expected to deepen cooperation on artificial intelligence.[8] Meeting UAE President [[mohamed-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan]], Trump said, "I have absolutely no doubt that the relationship will only get bigger and better."[8] He referenced a UAE pledge to invest $1.4 trillion in the United States over ten years, conveyed weeks earlier in Washington by [[tahnoon-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan]], Sheikh Mohamed's brother, the UAE's national security adviser and chairman of two Abu Dhabi sovereign wealth funds: "And all I can say is thank you very much. We will work very hard to deserve it."[8] The stop followed a trip to [[qatar|Qatar]], where Doha unveiled plans to invest $10 billion in a US military facility.[8] Badlands commentator [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] described the Gulf States as having demonstrated a mutually beneficial partnership with Trump and called the trip "a complete and total success."[8]

## Rising influence in Africa (2024)

A May 2024 Financial Times investigation described the UAE as a new cash-rich player reshaping the African continent.[9] It recounted President [[mohamed-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Sheikh Mohammed bin Zayed al-Nahyan]] flying several hundred friends into a private airport in South Africa's Eastern Cape province to celebrate Eid, ahead of a stay at his private resort in one of the country's poorest regions; the Emirati ruler was reported to have donated R20mn ($1mn) to upgrade the runway at the backwater airport, which authorities made an international port of entry for the occasion.[9] Despite the display of goodwill, [[south-africa|South Africa]] had failed to persuade the UAE to hand over the [[gupta-brothers|Gupta brothers]], and roughly two weeks before the visit a Dubai court refused to extradite two of them citing incorrect paperwork — a ruling South Africa's justice minister called "shocking."[9]

The article also detailed Emirati acquisition of mining rights across Africa. Primera, an Abu Dhabi-based gold trader, was granted a 25-year monopoly by the government of the [[democratic-republic-of-the-congo|Democratic Republic of Congo]] for all small-scale "artisanal" gold supplies in the country.[9] [[international-resources-holding|International Resources Holding]], chaired by UAE National Security Adviser Tahnoun bin Zayed Al Nahyan, paid $1.1 billion for a majority stake in the [[mopani-mining|Mopani]] copper mine in [[zambia|Zambia]], previously owned by [[glencore|Glencore]].[9]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the piece as a hit against a purported member of the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] from the Financial Times of London, an outlet [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] regularly brands "the official paper of record" for the WEF oligarchy.[9] He argued the framing on the Guptas was deceptive, since two brothers had been arrested in Dubai in June 2022 and the third in India in May 2024, and concluded that [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s 2017 [[executive-order-13818|Executive Order 13818]] led directly to the 2024 acquisition of nefarious African mining companies by Sovereign Alliance members, in this case the UAE.[9]

## Withdrawal from Yemen (December 2025)

On Tuesday, December 30, 2025, the Emirati Ministry of Defense announced that the UAE would withdraw its remaining forces from [[yemen|Yemen]], voluntarily terminating its counterterrorism units there and citing concerns for the safety of personnel.[10] These were the UAE's only forces remaining in the country since it completed a wider military withdrawal in 2019; Abu Dhabi had joined the Saudi-led coalition formed in 2015 to fight the [[houthis|Houthi]] rebels at the request of Yemen's internationally recognized government.[10]

The announcement followed a coalition airstrike earlier the same day on the southern Yemeni port of Mukalla, which Riyadh said targeted weapons and combat vehicles unloaded from ships arriving from the UAE and allegedly bound for the [[southern-transitional-council|Southern Transitional Council]], a southern separatist group.[10] The UAE denied that the shipment contained weapons.[10]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that mainstream framing of the episode — and of the conflict in Sudan — as evidence of a growing rift between Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] and Emirati President [[mohammed-bin-zayed|Mohammed bin Zayed]] was unlikely, since MBZ had lobbied the Saudi royal family extensively to name MBS Crown Prince.[10] He read the split instead as tribal: Saudi- and Emirati-backed Yemeni tribes that had fought the Houthis together, with the UAE-backed faction now seeking its own claim to govern.[10] He characterized the episode as a sign of multipolarity and "regions of responsibility" — the Arabs solving their own problems — and highlighted the Saudi military's emergence as a regional authority on the back of a $12 billion equipment deal signed with [[donald-trump|Trump]] in his first term, before the $142 billion deal signed the previous month.[10]
