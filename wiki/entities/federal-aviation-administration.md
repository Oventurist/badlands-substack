# Federal Aviation Administration

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is the United States agency responsible for regulating civil aviation and controlling national airspace. It sits within the Department of Transportation, headed in February 2026 by Secretary [[sean-duffy|Sean Duffy]].[1]

## El Paso airspace shutdown (February 2026)

In February 2026 the FAA issued a surprise notice closing the airspace above El Paso, Texas — a major city on the US-Mexico border — halting all flights up to 18,000 feet for a stated period of ten days due to "special security reasons."[1] The restricted area covered a ten-mile radius.[1] The abrupt and unexplained nature of the closure fueled speculation before the underlying cause was disclosed.[1]

The Trump Administration subsequently confirmed that "cartel drones" had entered US airspace near El Paso and had been disabled through joint action by the FAA and the [[department-of-war|Department of War]].[1] Secretary Duffy posted on X that "The FAA and DOW acted swiftly to address a cartel drone incursion. The threat has been neutralized, and there is no danger to commercial travel in the region. The restrictions have been lifted and normal flights are resuming."[1] The restrictions were lifted Wednesday night, well short of the ten days initially noticed.[1]

Anonymous officials cited by *Business Insider* said the administration disabled the drones using counter-drone measures; the department did not publicly specify the exact technology used.[1] Mexican President [[claudia-sheinbaum|Claudia Sheinbaum]] rejected reports of the incursion entirely, stating that "There is no information about the use of drones on the border."[1]

## Flying car and supersonic mandates (2025)

On 6 June 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed executive orders directing the FAA to begin testing electric vertical takeoff and landing aircraft — "flying cars" — through a pilot program run with public and private stakeholders.[2] A companion order repealed regulations that had hindered supersonic flight and instructed the FAA to create a standard for supersonic aircraft noise certification, while advancing research coordination with the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy and promoting international engagement to align global supersonic regulations and bilateral agreements.[2] OSTP director [[michael-kratsios|Michael Kratsios]] said the orders would "accelerate American innovation in drones, flying cars, and supersonic aircraft."[2] See [[2025-executive-orders-on-flying-cars-and-supersonic-flight|the 2025 executive orders on flying cars and supersonic flight]].[2]

## Montana airspace closure (February 2023)

During the [[chinese-spy-balloon-incident-2023|Chinese spy balloon]] transit on February 1, 2023, the FAA's Salt Lake City tower directed the shutdown of [[billings-logan-international-airport|Billings Logan International Airport]] and closed roughly 50 square miles of airspace over the Livingston, Montana area, affecting Bozeman, Helena and Billings for about two hours.[3] Two inbound flights were diverted and one departure delayed before the airspace reopened.[3]

## Diversity hiring lawsuit and the 2025 midair collision

The FAA is fighting a class-action lawsuit alleging that it denied roughly 1,000 would-be air traffic controllers jobs because of diversity hiring targets.[4] The suit was filed in 2015 and its lead plaintiff is [[andrew-brigida|Andrew Brigida]], who has suggested that the agency's obsession with diversity hiring and inclusion made an accident likely to happen.[4]

Complaints about the FAA's hiring policies resurfaced after an [[american-airlines|American Airlines]] passenger plane and a Black Hawk helicopter collided over Washington, DC, in late January 2025, killing 67 people in the country's deadliest aviation disaster in almost a quarter century.[4] It was revealed that controller staffing levels were "not normal" at the time of the collision.[4] Commentary in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] argued that the FAA's [[diversity-equity-and-inclusion|DEI]] revelations, the lawsuit, and the recent air disasters were building public support for a return to valuing competence before compassion in staffing safety-critical capabilities.[4]
