# Slobodan Milosevic

Slobodan Milosevic was the leader of the Yugoslav Republic of Serbia whose removal from power Badlands Media presents as the signature achievement of Western-sponsored [[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]].[1]

## The 1991 monetary defiance

The former Soviet satellite [[yugoslavia|Yugoslavia]] had accepted development loans from the [[world-bank|World Bank]] and other Western financial institutions, and by the late 1980s Western creditors led by the [[imf|IMF]] were using that leverage to push the country toward neoliberal economic restructuring.[1] In January 1991 Milosevic signed a law behind the backs of international creditors requiring Serbian-controlled national banks to issue $1.8 billion in new money to pay pensioners and farmers and to avert industrial bankruptcies, in defiance of those policies.[1]

The Western ruling class, DeLarme writes, was "none too thrilled about a populist nationalist upstart daring such a brazen move."[1] The international press turned against Milosevic, the United States terminated aid to Yugoslavia, and Washington threatened to use its veto power at the World Bank and IMF to suspend credit.[1]

## War, sanctions and bombing

The following decade was characterized by atrocity, with Yugoslavia essentially compelled into the violence of the Yugoslav Wars.[1] The [[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]] "helped to train" the separatist Albanian [[kosovo-liberation-army|Kosovo Liberation Army]] — previously designated by the U.S. as a terrorist group — and, in the Guardian's account, "encouraged them to launch a rebellion in southern Serbia in an effort to undermine the then Yugoslav President Slobodan Milosevic."[1]

[[nato|NATO]] imposed numerous rounds of economic sanctions and refused relief unless Milosevic accepted permanent NATO occupation and complete privatization of the economy, a demand the article says was "deliberately" framed "to provoke rejection by Belgrade."[1] When he refused, as designed, NATO launched a 78-day aerial bombing campaign.[1]

## Removal and trial

Western Europe viewed Milosevic's regime as an obstacle to integrating the Balkans into the European Union, and his survival in office at the end of the century prompted a new strategy: the [[otpor|Otpor!]] movement.[1] The article also frames the effort through NATO's desire to continue the Cold War against Russia and eventually dominate the Eurasian landmass, noting Milosevic's close ties with Moscow, which supplied arms and diplomatic support.[1]

Milosevic was removed from office in September 2000 and apprehended in 2001 for war crimes, then transported to The Hague for trial despite assurances to the contrary.[1] The [[national-endowment-for-democracy|NED]] spent a reported $41 million on the year-long operation to remove him, known as the [[bulldozer-revolution|Bulldozer Revolution]].[1] DeLarme is explicit that he is not commenting on whether the overthrow was justified, only on the mechanism used.[1]

## Otpor and the NED institutes

"Two Wings, One Ugly Bird" describes Milosevic's removal in the 1990s as the work of the youth movement [[otpor|Otpor]], funded by the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]] with help from [[george-soros|George Soros]]' [[open-society-foundations|Open Society]] network.[2] Training camps, cash and strategy sessions are said to have converted a ragtag resistance into a polished battering ram branded as "people power," while the [[national-democratic-institute|National Democratic Institute]] shaped the opposition coalition that took over, producing a government friendlier to NATO and United States interests.[2]
