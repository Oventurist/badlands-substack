# Iran

Iran appears in the article as the oil source at the heart of the [[sanctions|sanctions]] scam — a country embargoed by the United States, the article argues, less to punish it than to render its oil cheap and its financial system closed.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

## The Clinton sanctions

Citing Wikipedia, the article records that President [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] imposed some of the toughest sanctions against Iran in March 1995, during the presidency of Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, in response to the Iranian nuclear program and Iranian support for Hezbollah, Hamas and Palestine Islamic Jihad, which the United States considers terrorist organizations.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Executive Order 12957 prohibited the United States from trading in Iran's oil industry, and in May 1995 Executive Order 12959 prohibited trade with Iran altogether, abruptly ending commerce that had been growing since the end of the Iran–Iraq War.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

The article stresses the timing: these orders came just five months after Clinton agreed under the [[agreed-framework|Agreed Framework]] to lift sanctions on [[north-korea|North Korea]] and provide it with oil.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Both states were pursuing nuclear weapons and sponsoring terrorism, and North Korea's program was arguably further along, so the disparate treatment is presented as a contradiction resolved only by oil and money — the inference being that Clinton and the CIA/shadow government intended to obtain Iranian oil to deliver to North Korea.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

## Marc Rich and the hostage crisis

Iran's 1979 revolution and the ensuing American hostage crisis produced the oil embargo that [[marc-rich|Marc Rich]] exploited: he was convicted of tax fraud in the wake of oil purchases from Iran during the crisis.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) Rich bought Iranian oil below market price while Iran was desperate to sell, then resold it above market price to apartheid-era [[south-africa|South Africa]], itself sanctioned.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) He also pioneered uranium-for-oil commodity swaps in the 1980s between apartheid South Africa, Islamic Iran and Soviet Russia, which the article offers as an answer to how Iran and North Korea obtained uranium for their weapons programs.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3) The Washington Post is quoted noting that Rich clinched deals with everyone from Communist bureaucrats to Third World dictators to Iranian ayatollahs.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-for-sale-part-3)

## The "cookie jar" and the JCPOA

A later Badlands piece by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] treats Iran as a forbidden "cookie jar" — whoever opened it would be dipping into a goldmine.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away) For fifty years Iran had been kept under heavy sanctions and, despite sitting on the world's second-largest natural gas reserve and third-largest oil reserve, remained economically suppressed because it was blocked from trading with other countries and private companies.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away) The prevailing consensus held that a thriving Iran on the level of [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] would dramatically alter the Middle East, potentially producing a greater regional war in which Iranian proxies, suddenly enjoying hundreds of times more funding, could plausibly threaten [[israel|Israel]]'s existence.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away) The [[joint-comprehensive-plan-of-action|JCPOA]] is accordingly described as one of the most consequential geopolitical maneuvers in recent history, and the issue that split the Western ruling class factionally more than any other.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away)

## Factional flashpoint

DeLarme argues that the real motivation for U.S. and Israeli tensions with Iran was never "Schrödinger's nuke" but geopolitical favors and the question of which segment of the Western elite would shape the Middle East's future.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away) Western-partnered Iranian gas was intended to supplant Russian gas in Europe during the [[barack-obama|Obama]] administration's economic warfare against Russia, which is why Russia opposed sanctions relief for Iran.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away) Energy majors funding the [[atlantic-council|Atlantic Council]] treated Iran as a much larger version of the Ukrainian opportunity where [[chevron|Chevron]] had signed $10 billion deals with Naftogaz.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away)

## Nuclear diplomacy and escalation (February 2026)

By February 2026, U.S.–Iran tensions had escalated sharply following U.S. airstrikes on Iranian nuclear facilities in June, anti-government protests inside Iran during December and January that Tehran blamed on U.S. and Israeli instigation, and U.S. military deployments led by the USS Abraham Lincoln carrier group.[10] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] demanded that Iran restrict uranium enrichment and its ballistic missile program, prompting Tehran to announce missile upgrades and a shift toward an offensive military doctrine.[10]

Iran maintains that its nuclear program is peaceful, has enriched uranium to 60% purity since the United States withdrew from the 2015 deal and reimposed sanctions, and recently concluded indirect talks in Oman, with Foreign Minister [[abbas-araghchi|Abbas Araghchi]] stressing the need for calm dialogue without threats; the U.S. responded by announcing new sanctions.[10] Trump described the Oman-mediated track as "very different" from past negotiations and expressed optimism that Iran "wants to make a deal very badly," while warning of tough military action, including additional carrier deployments, if diplomacy fails.[10]

Former [[islamic-revolutionary-guard-corps|IRGC]] commander [[hussein-kanani|Hussein Kanani]] told RT that Iran could respond to U.S. aggression by striking Israel with missiles or closing the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], disrupting global oil flows including to China and Russia, and claimed that [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] already possesses a nuclear bomb.[10] Badlands commentary treated the Saudi claim as unsupported narrative bait aimed at provoking Israel rather than a genuine disclosure.[10]

## 2026 war with the United States and Israel

Reporting collected in March 2026 stated that U.S. and Israeli forces launched coordinated strikes on Iran in late February 2026, prompting retaliatory attacks by Tehran across the region.[11] U.S. forces struck Iranian military targets on [[kharg-island|Kharg Island]], the terminal handling roughly 90 percent of Iran's oil exports, with [[donald-trump|President Trump]] saying oil infrastructure itself was spared and warning of broader strikes if Iran interfered with shipping in the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]].[11] Iran threatened retaliation against U.S.-linked energy infrastructure in Gulf countries, and traffic through the strait — about one-fifth of world oil supply — was disrupted.[11]

White House economic adviser [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]] put the war's cost at roughly $12 billion and argued that American domestic oil production insulated the U.S. economy from Iranian pressure, while Iran's targets were more import-dependent.[11] The [[israel-defense-forces|Israel Defense Forces]] said they had struck dozens of targets in Tehran to deepen the blow against the Iranian regime, and Iranian official [[ali-larijani|Ali Larijani]] said the war would not end before Iran's enemies were made "sorry for their grave miscalculation."[11] Israeli Foreign Minister [[gideon-saar|Gideon Saar]] said only Iranians could bring down their government but might need outside help, while [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] warned Israel could target Iran's Supreme Leader.[11]

The war also produced a domestic American speech dispute: Trump threatened media organizations with treason charges over coverage he said relied on AI-generated Iranian fabrications, and commentator [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]] claimed the [[cia|CIA]] was preparing a [[foreign-agents-registration-act|FARA]] referral over his pre-war contacts with people in Iran.[11] Badlands analysis situated all of this within a wider [[global-energy-war|global energy war]].[11]

## Peace talks with the United States (March 2026)

Efforts to negotiate an end to the conflict between the United States and Iran remained active but unresolved in late March 2026, with both sides signaling that diplomacy was difficult yet not fully abandoned.[12] Indirect channels — primarily through intermediaries such as Pakistan, along with other regional go-betweens — continued to carry proposals between Washington and Tehran, including a US-backed ceasefire framework that mediators said had been relayed to Iran and publicly detailed on March 25.[12]

Iran rejected at least one recent US-backed proposal and put forward a counter-proposal and broad conditions for ending the conflict, underscoring the wide gap between the two sides.[12] Public statements remained inconsistent: [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and other US officials pointed to "productive conversations," while Iranian officials repeatedly denied direct negotiations or questioned their value, and earlier in March Tehran's envoy to the United Nations said Iran had not initiated talks and voiced skepticism about diplomacy while strikes continued.[12] The standoff followed nuclear and de-escalation talks that ended without a deal in late February and were then overtaken by US and Israeli military action, which disrupted formal diplomacy without eliminating back-channel engagement.[12]

Badlands contributor GhostofBasedPatrickHenry wrote that Trump and Iran continued "to play games regarding the true nature of this war," noting Trump's claim that the Iranians had given him a gift signaling a deal was possible, his statement that his administration had identified Iranian officials it trusted and expected Iran to deny the talks, and the reported removal of Iranian leaders' names from the US kill list as evidence that engagement was genuine.[12]

## The 2026 war

In early March 2026 the United States, under [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] and in coordination with [[israel|Israel]], launched military strikes on Iran that escalated into an ongoing [[iran-war|war]] in its fifth day.[15] Supreme Leader [[ali-khamenei|Ayatollah Ali Khamenei]] was killed in a weekend attack, confirmed by Iranian state media although no official photograph of the body was released.[15] Trump reported that Iran's navy, air force, air defenses, radar and most military capabilities had been "knocked out" and said most potential future Iranian leaders were already dead.[15]

## June 2026 de-escalation and draft memorandum

In June 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] appeared to pull back from another round of threatened military escalation against Iran after signaling that negotiations with Tehran might be nearing a breakthrough; days after warning that the US would "bomb the shit out of them" absent an agreement, he said a major peace deal could be signed soon and indicated planned strikes had been suspended while talks continued.[16] The diplomatic shift came despite continued clashes in and around the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], where US forces struck Iranian targets and enforced a naval blockade on Iranian shipping while Iran launched retaliatory missile and drone attacks and threatened broader disruption of regional energy flows, each side accusing the other of violating ceasefire arrangements.[16]

Iran publicly stated that no final agreement had been approved and that key issues remained unresolved, including sanctions relief and other negotiating demands, while warning that any renewed US attack would trigger a harsher response than previous exchanges.[16] Axios subsequently reported a proposed memorandum of understanding that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz without tolls, provide sanctions relief tied to Iranian compliance, and extend a ceasefire for 60 days including in Lebanon while negotiations on Iran's nuclear program proceeded.[16]

Under the reported framework, Iran would commit not to acquire a nuclear weapon and would work toward resolving disputes over its enriched uranium stockpile, with one option under discussion involving down-blending highly enriched uranium inside Iran under United Nations supervision, though any nuclear measures would require a further and more detailed agreement.[16] Negotiators reportedly reached a tentative deal following talks involving Qatari mediator [[ali-al-thawadi|Ali Al-Thawadi]] and Iranian Foreign Minister [[abbas-araghchi|Abbas Araghchi]]; if signed, the accord would be known as the [[islamabad-agreement|Islamabad agreement]] and was mediated jointly by [[qatar|Qatar]] and [[pakistan|Pakistan]].[16] An Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson said Tehran had not yet made a final decision on the proposal even as Trump anticipated a weekend signing ceremony.[16]

## June 2026 Doha talks dispute
President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said in late June 2026 that Iran had requested a meeting with the United States and announced that talks would be held the following day in Doha, [[qatar|Qatar]], with Special Envoy [[steve-witkoff|Steve Witkoff]] and [[jared-kushner|Jared Kushner]] attending to advance implementation of the US-Iran memorandum of understanding.[17] Iran publicly contradicted the announcement: Foreign Ministry spokesman [[esmaeil-baqaei|Esmaeil Baqaei]] said no negotiations with the United States were scheduled "at any level" in the coming days, while confirming that an Iranian delegation would travel to Doha for technical discussions on the existing agreement.[17] The conflicting statements followed renewed military exchanges in the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] that had challenged the recently established ceasefire, even as both sides pursued diplomacy aimed at preventing escalation.[17]

## US entry into the war (March 2026)

On Friday, February 27, 2026, the United States joined [[israel|Israel]] in military strikes against Iran; President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he ordered US forces to join because he believed Iran was preparing to strike first.[18] Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] said the United States acted because it expected Iran would retaliate against Israel's planned action and potentially target American forces.[18] Iran launched missile and drone strikes in retaliation, targeting locations in Israel and US-linked sites in Gulf states.[18]

[[communist-china|China]] condemned the strikes on Iran and called for an immediate ceasefire and diplomatic negotiations, while [[russia|Russia]]'s Kremlin publicly condemned the US action but continued its diplomatic dialogue with Washington.[18] President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] discussed escalation risks with Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]], both expressing concern the conflict could spread regionally.[18] Israeli Prime Minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] sought clarification from the [[white-house|White House]] after learning US officials might be in contact with the Iranian government, reflecting Israeli concern that Washington might reach a ceasefire before Israel's objectives were met.[18]

Badlands analysis by [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode as a phase of narrative deployment in which the legacy press sought to break the Russia-Iran axis and fracture the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]], and predicted that if the "new" Iranian regime emerging from the conflict closely resembles the old one, Russia and China can save face while Trump locks in a restored status quo.[18]

## Succession and elections after Raisi's death (2024)

The death of President [[ebrahim-raisi|Ebrahim Raisi]] in May 2024 obliged Iran's leadership to hold a presidential election within 50 days as mandated by the Constitution, presenting the political establishment with a decision it did not want to make.[78] For decades Iran's leaders had pointed to high voter turnout as proof of the Islamic Republic's legitimacy, but turnout had fallen sharply — from more than 60 percent in the 2016 parliamentary elections to 42 percent in 2020 and just under 41 percent in March 2024, despite official vows that the figure would rise.[78] Supreme Leader [[ali-khamenei|Ali Khamenei]] faced two risky options: opening the contest to all candidates from hard-liners to reformists, risking a competitive election with an unwanted outcome, or repeating his practice of blocking reformists and even moderate loyal opposition figures, risking still lower turnout that would read as a stinging rebuke of his increasingly authoritarian state.[78]

## December 2024 energy crisis

In December 2024, an energy crisis left Iran's leadership scrambling for solutions after widespread power outages forced schools and government institutions across the country to close.[83] Following a summer of blackouts, the crisis was exacerbated by harsh winter conditions, with severe cold, snow and air pollution compounding the difficulties of an energy sector unable to handle seasonal surges after years of underinvestment and sanctions.[83]

The New York Times reported that another contributing factor was a pair of attacks on major gas pipelines in February 2024, which the paper attributed to [[israel]]; the attacks disrupted gas flows to millions of Iranians and caused widespread outages across five provinces.[83] Israel did not comment on the blasts at the time, and Iran's oil minister Javad Owji also appeared hesitant to assign blame.[83] Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that millions of Iranians had been without reliable power for nearly a year while coverage focused on Israeli suffering, and questioned how [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] could court the Iranian public to overthrow their government while bombing their energy infrastructure.[83]

## "Does the CIA Still Control Iran?" (2023)

In a July 2023 essay, [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argued that the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] never left Iran after 1979 and that the country has functioned as a manufactured enemy of the United States ever since.[87] Carlson's premise is that patriots and [[deep-state|Deep State]] players exist in every country — Russia, China, England, Israel, Brazil and Iran alike — so that the presence of genuinely anti-American factions in Tehran does not exclude the simultaneous presence of CIA-controlled ones.[87]

He recounted that the agency had publicly acknowledged its role in the [[1953-iranian-coup|1953 overthrow]] of [[mohammad-mosaddegh|Mohammad Mossadegh]] roughly a decade before he wrote, an operation headed by [[kermit-roosevelt-jr|Kermit Roosevelt Jr.]] and prompted by Mossadegh's nationalization of the oil industry and expulsion of British Petroleum.[87] [[mohammad-reza-pahlavi|The Shah]] then ruled for 26 years until the [[iranian-revolution|Iranian Revolution]] installed Ayatollah [[ruhollah-khomeini|Khomeini]], during which 52 American diplomats were seized in the [[iran-hostage-crisis|Iran Hostage Crisis]].[87]

Carlson's central evidentiary claim is the absence of an American war of reconquest: he asked why the CIA would let protestors expel it from a resource-rich country without ever attempting to take it back, when the hostage crisis would have supplied ample pretext, and concluded that a contrived enemy was more valuable than a client state — the agency could blame terrorist acts on Iran, generate fear through it and sustain the [[military-industrial-complex|military industrial complex]] through it.[87] He also argued the agency could have made Iran the scapegoat for 9/11 but declined, because doing so would have required a war against an asset it already controlled, whereas Iraq and the Afghan poppy fields did need to be brought under CIA control.[87]

### The Obama money and the Trump reversal

Carlson wrote that in 2015 [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] brokered a deal giving Iran roughly $150 billion not to build nuclear weapons — in his reading, to move the nuclear weapons program to its close ally [[syria|Syria]] — and additionally airlifted $1.7 billion in cash on pallets, a method he compared to mafia transactions rather than wire transfers.[87] He noted that Iran, supposedly America's mortal enemy and then holding four American prisoners, accepted the money and released the prisoners, and argued that the true purpose was to bail out a regime "running on fumes," which made sense only if the United States was heavily infiltrated in Iran's government.[87] The injection of funds, he wrote, gave Iran a new lease on life and a seat again at the terrorism table, enabling it to fund factions such as [[isis|ISIS]] in Syria and Iraq.[87]

He then set out a sequence he presented as fact rather than assumption: [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced withdrawal from the [[joint-comprehensive-plan-of-action|Iran Deal]] on 8 April 2018 and called for the bombing of Syria five days later on 13 April, which Carlson called no coincidence.[87] After the withdrawal and the strikes, Iran shot rockets at US military bases in Iraq on dozens of occasions; John Bolton and Mike Pompeo urged Trump to kill [[qassem-soleimani|Qassem Soleimani]], but Trump refused until Americans were killed, and seven months later, after a contractor was killed and four US servicemen injured, ordered the drone strike in Baghdad on 3 January 2020.[87] Carlson observed that the [[united-nations|UN]] called the assassination "unlawful" and the American media likened Soleimani to a "holy man," while neither had objected during the preceding rocket attacks, and asked what Soleimani was doing in Baghdad at all.[87] He concluded that while there are genuinely evil people doing evil things in Iran, many of them are controlled by the CIA and [[mi6|MI6]], and predicted that once Trump returned to the White House the CIA's strings in Iran would be cut as they had been in [[north-korea|North Korea]].[87]

## The "forbidden cookie jar"

In "The Deep Politics of the '12-Day War'," [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] describes Iran as the forbidden "cookie jar" of the Middle East — brimming with treasure but untouchable for almost fifty years.[90] Iran sits on nearly 20 percent of the world's natural gas supply and holds the third-largest oil reserves, and occupies a geostrategic goldmine centred in the oil-rich Persian Gulf, serving as a potential bridge between Europe and Asia that bypasses Russia, and for Asia a gateway to Europe and Africa.[90] Because Iran had been under heavy sanctions since the 1979 revolution, lifting them under the [[joint-comprehensive-plan-of-action|Iran Deal]] would allow its economy to skyrocket — which DeLarme identifies as the true impetus behind the [[twelve-day-war|12-Day War]] rather than any nuclear program.[90]

On the nuclear question he notes that Iran can enrich uranium to 60 percent, short of the roughly 90 percent needed for weapons-grade material, making it a [[threshold-state|threshold state]] with the legal right under the [[treaty-on-the-non-proliferation-of-nuclear-weapons|NPT]] to do what it is doing.[90] He cites [[john-mearsheimer|John Mearsheimer]] and the [[international-atomic-energy-agency|IAEA]] for the position that there is no evidence Iran was pursuing weapons, and rejects the claim that a nuclear-armed Iran would immediately strike [[israel|Israel]], since doing so would mean the immediate suicide of the regime and potentially the deaths of over 90 million Iranians, not all of whom are allegiant to the current government.[90]

DeLarme reported casualty figures during the war of 610 to 974 Iranian deaths and 2,500 to 5,332 injuries against Israel's 28 to 30 deaths and over 3,400 injuries.[90] On regime change, he wrote that a fanatic Iranian regime whose people live "as though they have a boot on their throat" is not an ideal partner for the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration, but that if there is to be regime change the Iranian people must be the ones to decide what replaces the old regime — and that there is no guarantee a hard regime change would succeed.[90]
