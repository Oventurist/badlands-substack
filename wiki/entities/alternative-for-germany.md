# Alternative for Germany (AfD)

Alternative for Germany (Alternative für Deutschland, AfD) is a German political party widely described in legacy coverage as "far-right" and, by March 2026, the country's most persistently rising electoral force despite systematic exclusion from power.[1]

## Electoral gains

The AfD posted gains in recent German state elections, with its strongest performances concentrated in the eastern regions of the country. Its vote share has continued to grow across successive contests even as establishment pressure against it has intensified.[1]

## The "firewall" and extremist designation

German domestic intelligence authorities have classified the AfD as "right-wing extremists," and mainstream German parties have maintained a longstanding "firewall" policy toward the party — refusing to form coalitions or governing alliances with it regardless of its performance at the ballot box. As a result the AfD remains excluded from governing coalitions even where it wins substantial support.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the AfD story as a case study in the diminishing returns of labeling political opposition as radical, comparing it to [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s so-called "red sermon" speech, which she argued increased MAGA credibility and coincided with a rise in MAGA's share of the vote. She noted the anniversary of Benito Mussolini's founding of the Fascist Party in Italy and his description of fascism as "corporatism… the marriage of government and corporate power," asking how that differed from the present global order.[1]

Her broader argument was that "far right" functions as the globalists' pejorative for nationalists, that nationalism is more popular than ever worldwide as populations conclude they are being exploited for the benefit of a few, and that whether the reported AfD numbers are accurate is beside the point — the narrative itself signals that attacks on the party are failing to neutralize it. She closed with the single word "Accelerate."[1]


## Breton Threat to Cancel the German Election (January 2025)

In January 2025 former French European Commissioner [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] indicated that European authorities were prepared to act against the German election as they had in Romania if [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] supported the AfD, which he characterized as an "extremist" party.[2] Breton was quoted as saying, "We did it in Romania, and we will do it in Germany if necessary," and warned of fines and the possibility of a platform ban, asserting that the EU was "equipped to enforce these laws to protect our democracies in Europe."[2] Commentators framed the episode as an admission that European institutions viewed AfD's rise — and broader "far right" ideas about decentralization and national sovereignty — as an existential threat.[2]

## Prosecution of Marie-Thérèse Kaiser (2024)

In May 2024 the Verden regional court in Lower Saxony upheld a conviction for incitement to hatred against [[marie-therese-kaiser|Marie-Thérèse Kaiser]], the AfD leader in Rotenburg, over a 2021 social media post questioning Germany's acceptance of Afghan refugees in light of gang rape statistics.[3] The 27-year-old was ordered to pay 100 day fines plus a €60 fine, totalling €6,000, a penalty severe enough to confer a criminal record under German law.[3] The case drew international attention when X chief executive [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] questioned whether the penalty had been imposed for repeating accurate government statistics.[3]

## October 2023 record polling high

In October 2023 the AfD reached what was then a new record high, taking 23 percent in the ARD Deutschlandtrend poll and opening an unprecedented eight-point lead over the ruling [[social-democratic-party-of-germany|Social Democratic Party]].[4] At that level the AfD polled stronger than the SPD and the [[free-democratic-party|Free Democratic Party]] combined, while the entire three-party "traffic light" coalition fell to 33 percent — 15 percent for the SPD, 13 percent for the Greens and 5 percent for the FDP, the last sitting exactly at the parliamentary entry threshold.[4] Party co-leader [[alice-weidel|Alice Weidel]] celebrated the result and noted that 50 percent of citizens wanted the AfD in government where it had performed strongly.[4]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described the surge as leaving the German establishment "scrambling for a rebrand in the midst of an identity crisis," observing that a party barely clearing 5 percent in recent years had reached 23 percent.[4] He characterized the AfD as effectively a two-plank party at that time — opposition to illegal immigration and opposition to sanctions on foreign nations, specifically Russia.[4] He also highlighted that only 1 percent of Germans named climate change as a pressing issue while net migration ranked first, arguing this demonstrated that the "true believers" of the "current thing" exist mainly on social media, and concluding that "'The Party' is exposed, and it's crumbling."[4]

## Musk Endorsement and 'Make Europe Great Again' (January 2025)

Ahead of Germany's snap parliamentary election scheduled for late February 2025, [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] endorsed the AfD and hosted co-leader [[alice-weidel|Alice Weidel]] for a live interview on X, repeatedly stating that "only AfD can save Germany."[5] During the interview Weidel described the AfD as a "libertarian conservative party" that sought to reduce bureaucracy and "free the people from the state."[5] On January 15, 2025 Weidel adapted Trump's MAGA slogan into a call to "Make Europe Great Again" in a post on X, responding to commentary drawing parallels between the party's program and the policies of Trump and Musk.[5]

## Ban discussions (2023–2024)

By January 2024, Germany was openly discussing banning the AfD, then its second-largest political party, a step commentators likened to US states attempting to bar [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from ballots and summarized as: "If you don't like the popularity of the opposition, ban it."[6] The debate unfolded against the weakness of the governing "Traffic Light" coalition — the SPD had won the last federal election and eventually came to terms with the Greens and the pro-business FDP — which had fallen from 52% at the election to about 33%, while the FDP itself collapsed from 11.5% to 5.0%, the minimum threshold for parliamentary seats.[6]

In December 2023, Foreign Policy reported that Germany was "thinking of simply banning the far right," after the country's domestic intelligence apparatus classified the Saxony state branch of the AfD as a threat to democracy — a potential first step toward outlawing the party as unconstitutional.[6] Dirk-Martin Christian, president of Saxony's State Office for the Protection of the Constitution, declared that "there can be no doubt about the extreme right orientation of this party."[6] Badlands commentary argued that Germany was moving to ban the fastest-growing party in the nation precisely because it was the fastest-growing party, and because its growth rested on rising sovereign sentiment.[6]

## 2025 candidate deaths in North Rhine-Westphalia

Ahead of local elections in North Rhine-Westphalia scheduled for 14 September 2025, four AfD candidates died suddenly and unexpectedly within a short period, an event covered as the [[2025-afd-candidate-deaths|2025 AfD candidate deaths]].[7] The dead were identified as Ralph Lange (66, Blomberg), Wolfgang Seitz (59, Rheinberg), Wolfgang Klinger (71, Schwerte) and Stefan Berendes (59, Bad Lippspringe).[7] Lange's death on 28 August 2025 invalidated polling cards and postal ballots already sent out in Blomberg, forcing the city to reprint them and to hold the vote as a by-election on the planned date.[7] German economist [[stefan-homburg|Stefan Homburg]] called the cluster "statistically almost impossible," while German police said there was no evidence of foul play.[7] Badlands commentator Ashe in America connected the deaths to the party's slogan "Deutschland; aber normal" — Germany, but normal — calling it "possibly a deadly position."[7]

## Regional strength in the former East

A February 2024 map published by the German poll aggregator Wahlkreisprognose showed the AfD leading in almost every Bundestag constituency in the territory of the former German Democratic Republic, while the west remained dominated by the CDU and its Bavarian sister party the CSU.[8] The pattern, in which party preference tracks the line of the former Iron Curtain, is described at [[german-east-west-political-divide|German east–west political divide]].[8]

## February 2024 crackdown proposals

In February 2024, German Interior Minister [[nancy-faeser|Nancy Faeser]] announced plans to make it easier to trace right-wing extremists' financing and to establish an "early recognition unit" to detect far-right and foreign disinformation campaigns, following large protests against the far right.[9] The proposals reflected concern after a report that extremists had met to discuss deporting millions of immigrants, including some with German citizenship, with some AfD members present.[9] The party's support had doubled since Germany's 2021 election.[9] Germany's domestic intelligence agency, headed by [[thomas-haldenwang|Thomas Haldenwang]], counted 38,800 far-right extremists in 2022, 14,000 of them considered potentially violent, with numbers believed to have risen again in 2023.[9]

## September 2024 Thuringia and Saxony state elections

In state elections held on Sunday, September 1, 2024, the AfD alone won a third of the votes across the two eastern German states of Thuringia and Saxony, taking its first state election victory in Thuringia and a narrow second place in Saxony.[10] Hundreds marched through the streets of Erfurt on the Sunday night amid news that a far-right party had won a state election for the first time since the Nazi era.[10] Some Erfurt residents, too sceptical of the media to speak on camera, told Euronews their votes were a "last resort," citing safety concerns worsened by a slate of knife crimes including a deadly attack in Solingen claimed by the Islamic State group, which had dominated the news ahead of the vote.[10]

Dr Sven Leunig, a political scientist at the Thuringia-based University of Jena, told Euronews that the election showed Germany had "in a way, become normal in the sense that right-wing populists, who are strong everywhere else in Europe, are also asserting themselves strongly in Germany," adding that the leftist Sahra Wagenknecht Alliance (BSW) held broadly similar populist positions.[10]

Analysis by the Elections Research Group found the AfD was the most popular party among young voters: 36 per cent of voters aged 18 to 29 backed the party in Thuringia, by far the most of any party and 11 per cent more than in the previous election in 2019, according to broadcaster NTV.[10] The centre-right Christian Democrats (CDU) and the Left party tied for second place among that group in Thuringia at 13 per cent.[10]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing from a layover in Munich, highlighted that none of the established parties managed to secure an absolute majority or even a coalition majority, and framed the result as further evidence that the people of the world are roundly rejecting globalism.[10] She drew a parallel to [[donald-trump|Trump]] building a coalition with the neglected middle of the United States and predicted that the global regime, freaking out at the thought of losing its grip on power, would generate a great deal more rhetoric about "the far right" on both sides of the Atlantic.[10]

## Saxony software error probe and mail-in ballot opposition (September 2024)

Although the AfD secured a first-place finish in the Saxony election held that Sunday, the party launched an investigation into an alleged computer error that cost it a seat in the state parliament and called for an end to mail-in voting, citing security concerns and shady practices.[11] The alleged software glitch resulted in the AfD and the Christian Democrats (CDU) each losing a seat while the Greens and the Social Democrats each gained one.[11] The party's state and parliamentary group leader Jörg Urban said, "We want to know exactly what went wrong," demanded an exact error analysis and warned, "If there are any irregularities, we will take legal action."[11] The lost seat cost the AfD its blocking majority, which would have allowed it, for example, to block the appointment of certain judges in the state.[11]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] praised the move as the correct model — "audit elections even, especially, if you win" — noting that mail-in balloting and software issues are a global concern.[11] She relayed Holly at Altitude's idea of a "public trust prenup," under which elections would be audited regardless of outcome by independent auditors rather than by government-run risk-limiting audits without oversight, and suggested Americans consider the approach in November.[11]

## Free speech perceptions among AfD voters, 2025

An INSA survey reported in August 2025 found that among German party supporters, AfD voters felt the most restricted in expressing their opinions, at 76%.[12] The same survey found 84% of German respondents overall believe people withhold opinions due to fear of consequences and 54% had personally felt unable to speak freely, a perception up 6 points in eight months.[12] Commentary framed free expression as an issue that could become as politically pivotal in [[germany|Germany]] as migration and the pandemic.[12]

## Extremist designation (May 2025)

In a statement released in early May 2025, Germany's domestic intelligence agency, the [[federal-office-for-the-protection-of-the-constitution|Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution]] (Verfassungsschutz, BfV), formally labeled the AfD a right-wing extremist organization.[13] Reporting indicated the designation opened the door for the German government to intensify surveillance of the party's activities.[13] Badlands coverage framed the designation as a bureaucratic mechanism for surveilling right-wing political opposition more aggressively.[13]

## North Rhine-Westphalia municipal elections (September 2025)

The AfD nearly tripled its support in municipal elections in Germany's most populous state, North Rhine-Westphalia, on Sunday, September 14, 2025, according to initial results, winning nearly 15 percent of the vote and finishing third.[14] The outcome underscored the party's growing appeal to voters outside its strongholds in the states of the former East Germany, where it is the strongest political force.[14] AfD leaders came to see the more populous west of the country — including the declining industrial cities of North Rhine-Westphalia, home to steel factories and a diminishing coal industry — as the key to expanding the party's base, particularly among working-class voters increasingly defecting to the far right.[14]

## Thuringia and Saxony breakthrough (September 2024)

On September 1, 2024 the AfD won its first state election in Germany since the Nazi era, taking 32 to 33 percent in Thuringia according to ARD and ZDF projections and finishing well ahead of the [[christian-democratic-union|Christian Democratic Union]]'s roughly 24 percent — see the [[2024-thuringia-state-election|2024 Thuringia state election]].[15] Projections also showed the party in close contention in neighboring Saxony, where the CDU had governed since reunification in 1990 and where both parties hovered around 31 to 32 percent, in the [[2024-saxony-state-election|2024 Saxony state election]].[15] Newsweek said the outcomes underscored deepening discontent with the government of Chancellor [[olaf-scholz|Olaf Scholz]].[15]

Badlands commentary rejected the "return of the Nazis" framing entirely, arguing that the Nazis never left and that the Nazi high command had ruled continental Europe for 79 years through [[nato|NATO]] and later the [[european-union|European Union]], whose 1993 founding documents the writer said closely resemble the Third Reich's postwar plans for Europe.[15]

## CDU ban motion (October 2023)

In October 2023 [[christian-democratic-union|Christian Democratic Union]] MP [[marco-wanderwitz|Marco Wanderwitz]], who had personally been defeated by an AfD candidate, announced plans to introduce a motion in the [[bundestag|Bundestag]] to ban the party, demonstrating that calls for prohibition were coming not only from the left but from a party often described as conservative.[16] Speaking on the state-run ARD program "Panorama," Wanderwitz said the AfD "seriously endangers our free democratic basic order and the state as a whole" and that "it is high time to ban them."[16] Wanderwitz sought other MPs to back the motion, though the ultimate authority over such a ban rests with the German Constitutional Court, and both the legal hurdles and the political implications were described as enormous.[16]

At the time the AfD was the second most popular party in Germany, standing at 21 percent in Politico's poll of polls behind only the CDU, with some surveys placing it as high as 23 percent and approximately 30 percent of German voters saying they could imagine voting for the party.[16] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode as a "weekly reminder that the Awakening is worldwide," noting the party was founded only in 2013 and had surged every year since 2018 amid rising immigration, associated crime, and inflation he attributed to Western sanctions on Russia imposed since 2022, all under the leftist government of Chancellor [[olaf-scholz|Olaf Scholz]].[16] He described rival MPs calling for a party to be banned "in order to protect Democracy" as evidence of "Clown World," concluding that "the awakening happens very slowly ... and then all at once."[16]

## 2024 surge and exclusion motion

In November 2024 the AfD climbed to 19.5 percent in Insa-Consulere polling published by Bild, a gain of 1.5 points since October and firmly second behind the CDU/CSU at 32.5 percent.[17] The rise coincided with an [[afd-ban-effort|effort by 112 Bundestag members]] from across the political spectrum to exclude the party from the February 23 snap election on the grounds that it is "anti-democratic," a motion submitted to the [[bundestag|Bundestag]] president with the potential to reach Germany's Constitutional Court.[17] Party leader [[alice-weidel|Alice Weidel]] polled at 17 percent as a prospective chancellor, and at 27 percent in eastern Germany.[17]
