# Benjamin West

Benjamin West (born 1737) was an American-born painter who became a founding member and later president of the [[royal-academy-of-arts|English Royal Academy of Fine Arts]] and history painter to [[king-george-iii|King George III]]. In the Badlands Media essay A New American Renaissance, [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presents him as "the greatest living painter during his lifetime" and "a man of untold anomalies" who functioned as a republican incubator and coordinator inside the cultural heart of the British Empire.

## Origins

West was born in 1737 — the same year as George III — into a Quaker family in Swarthmore, Pennsylvania. He was entirely self-taught, having learned to paint using clay and bear grease shown to him by Delaware natives with whom his family was friendly. His father, who ran a boarding house, was a close friend of [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] and in 1751 or 1752 made his home reportedly the first building in the world to be fitted with a lightning rod. West was drawn early into the networks of Franklin's Pennsylvania Philosophical Society.

Because strict Quakers regarded all art as corrupting — awakening passions held to be dangerous for fallen mankind — his community convened an all-night emergency meeting on whether the boy should be allowed to paint. They concluded that if God had given him the skill, he might be the one to raise painting to a level at which it could make people better. Franklin's associates, impressed by his teenage work *The Last Days of Socrates*, sponsored his passage to France in 1769 and then to Italy, where he studied the classical masters for two years.

## England

A planned brief stop in London before returning home became permanent. West caught the attention of an advisor to George III, received royal patronage, became a founding member of the Royal Academy at its 1769 establishment, and was elected its president in 1791. He never returned to America. Americans who lived through the Revolution called him a traitor; the British accused him of being an American spy — a charge the source suggests "may be a bit more true."

From 1769 West spent long evenings at Buckingham Palace in philosophical dialogue with the king, discussing the nature of government and of mankind and making his pro-republican views plainly known.

## Method: the language of ironies

The source characterizes West as an artist of the classical Platonic tradition who embedded deliberate anomalies and ironies in his work to provoke perplexity, then wonder, then discovery. It compares his 1770 and 1776 self-portraits: the later eyes darker and more worldly, the tight English collar tugged in both as a symbol of formalistic English culture, and — most significantly — a rag over the canvas frame that is blank in 1770 but in 1776 bears figures quoted from *The Death of General Wolfe*. West's stated philosophy of history painting was that a society values the memories it keeps, and that the painter's task is to enshrine moments of the collective past so that universal lessons about who we are may be learned.

## Major works discussed

- *The Last Days of Socrates* — his first significant American painting, made as a teenager in Philadelphia.
- *The Departure of Regulus for Carthage* (1769) — purchased by George III for a thousand pounds; a study of Roman republican virtue and honor.
- *The Death of General Wolfe* (1770) — a depiction of the Battle of Quebec featuring [[william-johnson|William Johnson]] in native dress; notable for breaking the convention that history paintings use Greco-Roman costume, a break for which West was threatened with the loss of all future commissions before George III bought the work and settled the argument.
- *Penn's Treaty with the Indians* (1771) — the [[penn-treaty-of-1682|Penn Treaty of 1682]].
- *Saul and the Witch of Endor* (1777) — read in the source as counsel to a king vulnerable to superstition and occultism.
- *King George III* (1779) — the king receiving news of the French-Spanish Armada.
- *The Peace of Paris* (1783) — the American delegation, left unfinished because the British delegation never sat.
- *King Lear* (1791) and other Lear scenes.
- *Pharaoh and Host Lost at Sea* (1792) — a large mural of the Red Sea closing on the pursuing army.
- *The Royal Academicians* (1795) — West at the center of the Academy.
- *Cicero Discovering the Tomb of Archimedes*.
- *Benjamin Franklin Drawing Electricity from the Sky* (1816) — an overt statement of the Promethean principle, painted after George III's removal from power had left West isolated and without patronage.

## Legacy as teacher

West trained a generation of American painters in England, among them [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]], [[john-singleton-copley|John Singleton Copley]], Charles Willson Peale, Rembrandt Peale, Gilbert Stuart and John Trumbull. The source argues his mission was to establish a distinctly American renaissance movement capable of resolving both the European–Native American question and the tension between personal liberty and the general welfare. Much of the interpretation in the source is credited to the historian [[pierre-beaudry|Pierre Beaudry]].
