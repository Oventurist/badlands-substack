# Ipsos

Ipsos is a multinational market research and public-opinion polling firm whose surveys are frequently commissioned by American news organizations to measure electoral sentiment. In June 2024 it partnered with [[politico|Politico Magazine]] on a survey examining how the American electorate was processing [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s criminal conviction in the New York hush-money case.[1]

## Politico–Ipsos survey on the Trump verdict

The June 2024 Politico Magazine–Ipsos survey was fielded using KnowledgePanel, described by Politico as "the largest and most well-established online probability-based panel that is representative of the adult US population," with a sample size of 1,017 respondents.[1] Badlands commentary noted that KnowledgePanel receives a 3.5-out-of-5 rating from the review site Survey Police, pays its respondents, and permits any household member over the age of 13 to participate — factors the Badlands writer treated as caveats on the panel's representativeness.[1]

Politico's write-up emphasized that 21 percent of independents said the conviction made them less likely to support Trump and that it would be an important factor in their vote, while also conceding that efforts by Trump and his allies to discredit the prosecution had cast doubt on the verdict's validity among many respondents.[1] Forty-three percent of all respondents either strongly or somewhat agreed that the case had been brought to help President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[1]

## Findings on trust in the justice system

The Badlands analysis argued that the most significant results were buried in the detailed toplines rather than the narrative summary. Ipsos itself reported that a plurality of Americans — 40 percent — said the verdict had no impact on their likelihood of voting for Trump and was not important to their decision-making.[1]

The detailed results also measured the share of Americans expressing "a great deal of trust" in key judicial roles: Supreme Court justices at 12 percent, state court judges at 8 percent, federal court judges at 9 percent, prosecuting attorneys at 9 percent, defense attorneys at 8 percent, and citizens on juries at 15 percent.[1] Asked who receives special breaks in the justice system, respondents named wealthy people (72 percent) and current and former elected officials (63 percent), while only 2 percent believed that "people like you" received such breaks.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the Politico–Ipsos collaboration as narrative management — "a cleanup on aisle 45" — while arguing that the underlying numbers demonstrated the prosecution had produced effectively no electoral movement.[1] She linked the trust figures to an October 2022 New York Times/Siena College poll in which 68 percent of likely voters identified public corruption as the greatest threat to democracy, treating the Ipsos data as confirmation that concern over asymmetric justice had continued to grow.[1] Badlands framed polls generally as non-predictive but useful as indicators of the direction in which establishment narratives are being steered.[1]

## August 2023 jury-service survey

In late August 2023 Ipsos published a survey of Americans' trust in the broader legal system, polling both the general population and people who had served on juries in the previous ten years about their attitudes toward aspects of the criminal justice system.[2] The poll found that former jurors were more likely than the general population to trust the criminal justice system as a whole, including attorneys across all fields, judges, and state and local law enforcement.[2] Former jurors also held a nearly 20 percent more favorable view of fellow citizens serving on juries: 76 percent reported "a great deal" or "a fair amount" of trust in jurors, compared with 58 percent of non-jurors — the highest level of trust across both groups for all legal positions discussed.[2]

The survey asked 1,017 members of the general population who had not served on a jury in the past decade, "If the cases against Donald Trump go to trial, how confident are you, if at all, that the court will be able to find and seat jurors willing to put aside their prior beliefs about Donald Trump and decide the case based on the evidence presented?"[2] Overall, 71 percent said they were not confident, including 30 percent "not at all confident" and 41 percent "not too confident."[2] Only the classified-documents case, one of four then-ongoing prosecutions of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], was asked about.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that polls are "one of the key psychological manipulation tools of the Establishment" but still signal important narrative trends worth tracking, pairing the Ipsos result with an Associated Press poll finding that 77 percent of Americans considered [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] "too old" to run again in 2024, including 69 percent of Democrats.[2] He argued that taken together the twin polls explained why some viewed a Biden 2024 run as a net win for the [[great-awakening|Great Awakening]], with the collapsing administration representing the Deep State's "death rattle in the Collective Mind" running congruent with the Trump trials.[2]
