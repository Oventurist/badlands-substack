# Department of Education

The United States Department of Education is the federal agency that would carry out the central enforcement function of the [[antisemitism-awareness-act|Antisemitism Awareness Act]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

The act, which passed the U.S. House in May 2024 by a vote of 320–91, would codify the [[ihra-definition-of-antisemitism|International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance definition of antisemitism]] into federal civil rights law and direct the Department of Education to use that definition when investigating antisemitic discrimination on college campuses.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argues that because the IHRA definition's examples are overly broad or vaguely worded, departmental enforcement would inevitably be inconsistent, with political biases determining what qualifies as antisemitic.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) In his account this makes the department an operational arm of the wider project of [[government-sponsored-censorship|government-sponsored censorship]] conducted under a [[hate-speech|hate speech]] rationale.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Foreign funding disclosure agreement (February 2026)

In February 2026 the Department of Education entered an interagency agreement with the [[state-department|State Department]] to strengthen enforcement of foreign funding disclosure requirements at American colleges and universities, citing [[executive-order-14282|Executive Order 14282]] on transparency regarding foreign influence in higher education.[2] The agreement supports administration of [[section-117-higher-education-act|Section 117 of the Higher Education Act of 1965]], which requires institutions to report foreign gifts and contracts totaling $250,000 or more in a calendar year, and expands the State Department's role in managing and reviewing data from the Section 117 reporting portal.[2]

## Student loan collections restart (2025)

The Department of Education announced in April 2025 that Federal Student Aid would restart collections on defaulted student loans beginning May 5, 2025, following a five-year pause that began during the COVID-19 pandemic.[3] The decision affects approximately 5.3 million borrowers who were in default before the pause; for those borrowers the government can collect repayments from tax refunds, withhold Social Security benefits, and garnish wages up to 15% of disposable income.[3] According to the Department, "in default" means a borrower failed to make a loan payment for at least 270 days, roughly nine months, and borrowers were directed to check their status at studentaid.gov.[3] Badlands contributor [[gmoney|GMONEY]] called the restart under President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] a "really dumb move," arguing that students are exploited by taking out loans for corrupted institutions only to find no jobs with which to repay them, and asking why the administration was targeting young borrowers rather than "the real villains."[3]

## Education executive orders (April 2025)

On April 23, 2025, Trump signed seven executive orders addressing K-12 discipline, higher education accreditation, foreign funding transparency, AI education, support for Historically Black Colleges and Universities, and workforce training.[3] The new accreditation rules aim to hold accrediting agencies accountable for performance while curbing the influence of diversity, equity, and inclusion standards.[3] Guidance on K-12 school discipline was rescinded in favor of decisions based solely on student behavior and the elimination of disparate-impact liability in federal enforcement, while a task force was established to integrate artificial intelligence into K-12 education with priorities in student AI training, teacher development, and research.[3] The measures also included stricter enforcement of foreign gift disclosure laws for universities and the creation of a White House initiative to bolster HBCUs.[3]

## 2025 reduction in force and Supreme Court ruling

On March 11, 2025, the Department of Education announced a reduction in force involving 1,378 employees as part of an effort to shrink its workforce.[5] Litigation followed almost immediately, and in May 2025 U.S. District Judge [[myong-joun|Myong Joun]] of Massachusetts ordered the department to reinstate nearly 1,400 fired employees, concluding that the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s "true intention is to effectively dismantle the Department" even though in his view it lacked the power to do so.[5]

On Monday, July 14, 2025, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] granted the administration's request to temporarily pause Joun's order in a brief unsigned ruling, clearing the way for the department to be massively reduced in size.[5] Justice [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]] dissented in a 19-page opinion joined by Justices [[elena-kagan|Elena Kagan]] and [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]], calling the decision "indefensible" and warning that it "hands the Executive the power to repeal statutes by firing all those necessary to carry them out."[5] Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] rejected the dissent's reasoning, arguing that the real usurpation would be forcing a President to operate agencies working against his administration's goals rather than a President exercising plenary authority over Executive Branch subordinates.[5]

## Trump's 2023 pledge to break up the department

Speaking in Davenport, Iowa, in March 2023 as his 2024 presidential campaign accelerated, former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] delivered an education platform in which he said his administration had extensively studied breaking up the Department of Education altogether and reallocating its functions to the states.[4] "We studied it very closely and I was getting ready to do it, pull the trigger," Trump said, describing "the long-term goal, but now it's a short-term goal, of breaking up the federal Department of Education and redistributing its functions to the states," a line that drew raucous applause.[4]

Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that the department was "from its inception, designed to indoctrinate" and to undermine a society of sovereign individuals, drawing a distinction between education — a controlled training regime producing conformity through extrinsic rewards and punishments — and learning, which he characterized as dynamic, playful, and conceptually expansive.[4] In his account the department's systems exist to train compliant workers and citizens rather than to raise general intelligence, and Trump's dismantling of the apparatus "might be the most important blow to the modern-day slave plantation," though only a beginning: superior systems of learning would have to fill the void, and parents would need to re-educate themselves away from the Prussian model or a slow regression to indoctrination would follow within decades.[4]

## Dismantling and program transfers (March 2025)

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed an executive order in March 2025 directing the dismantling of the Department of Education, an action the [[white-house|White House]] listed among the week's headline accomplishments.[6] Education Secretary [[linda-mcmahon|Linda McMahon]] subsequently outlined on CNN's "State of the Union" a plan to unwind the agency while insisting that outward-facing programs affecting students, including special education and student loans, would not be defunded even if administered elsewhere.[6] Under the loose plan she described, student loans would move to the [[small-business-administration|Small Business Administration]] and programs for students with disabilities would most likely go to the [[department-of-health-and-human-services|Department of Health and Human Services]], where they originated.[6]

## California trans athlete standoff (July 2025)

Education Secretary [[linda-mcmahon|Linda McMahon]] gave California 10 days from June 27, 2025 to rescind any sports prizes awarded to transgender athletes and to ban them from girls' competition, after the department's Office of Civil Rights found the state had violated federal anti-discrimination law by allowing transgender girls to compete with cisgender girls.[7] On Monday, July 7, 2025, the state Department of Education's top attorney said California would not comply with the federal agreement, which would have required rescinding trans-inclusionary guidelines and sending personalized apologies to cisgender female athletes who lost to a transgender opponent.[7] Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]]'s office called McMahon's proposed agreement a "political document" that carried no weight and would force the state to illegally violate its own anti-discrimination laws, making California the second state after Maine to defy the administration on the issue.[7]

## Resumption of wage garnishment (January 2026)

A Department of Education spokesperson confirmed to [[cnbc|CNBC]] on December 23, 2025 that the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] would begin [[student-loan-wage-garnishment|garnishing the wages]] of defaulted student loan borrowers in early January 2026 — the first time a portion of borrowers' paychecks had been at risk since collection activity was halted at the start of the Covid pandemic.[8] Starting the week of January 7, the department expected around 1,000 defaulted borrowers to receive notices of administrative wage garnishment, with the number increasing thereafter.[8] The department can seize up to 15% of a student loan holder's after-tax income, part of the federal government's extraordinary collection powers, which also extend to tax refunds and Social Security retirement and disability benefits.[8]

## Buyouts and workforce reduction (February–March 2025)

In late February 2025, the Education Department offered a buyout of up to $25,000 to most of its employees, according to a department-wide email obtained by Politico.[9] Jacqueline Clay, a chief human capital officer, wrote in the Friday-afternoon email that employees had until Monday at 11:59 p.m. to decide, describing it as "a one time offer in advance of a very significant Reduction in Force for the US Department of Education."[9] Those accepting could stack the offer with retirement benefits and would receive the equivalent of severance pay or $25,000, whichever was less, with the offer taking effect March 31.[9] Most department employees were eligible, with a few exceptions including those using disability retirement, those who received a student loan repayment benefit in the last 36 months, and those awarded a retention bonus in the last 12 months.[9]

The buyout coincided with the Senate's March 3, 2025 confirmation of [[linda-mcmahon|Linda McMahon]] as Secretary of Education by a 51-45 party-line vote, with the White House stating plainly that it intends to dismantle the department and that McMahon's job would be to oversee that effort.[9] Badlands contributor Ashe in America characterized the buyout as a generous [[office-of-personnel-management|OPM]] offer to soon-to-be-terminated employees and argued that the department had allowed teachers' unions to ruin what she called an illegitimate institution, while conceding there were likely good people within it.[9]

## 2025 anti-DEI policy struck down

In February 2025 the department issued a so-called Dear Colleague letter setting out a policy under which schools receiving federal funding would face certification mandates requiring them to disclose compliance with the administration's prohibitions on [[diversity-equity-and-inclusion|diversity, equity and inclusion]] programming and on incorporating race into aspects of student life; the mandates were to begin taking effect in April.[10] On April 24, 2025, three separate judges — two of them appointed by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] — ruled against the policy.[10] U.S. District Judge [[landya-mccafferty|Landya McCafferty]] wrote a scathing opinion calling it "textbook viewpoint discrimination" likely violating the First Amendment, and concluded that the [[national-education-association|National Education Association]] was likely to prevail in arguing the policy was unconstitutionally vague and procedurally defective.[10]
