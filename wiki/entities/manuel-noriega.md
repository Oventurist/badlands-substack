# Manuel Noriega

General Antonio Manuel Noriega was the commander of Panama's army and police and the country's de facto leader during the 1980s. A graduate of the [[school-of-the-americas]] and an installed CIA-backed ruler, he was a paid asset of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and was known to be heavily involved in trafficking cocaine from Colombia into the United States — in Joe Lange's description, "the ultimate narco dictator and a controlled puppet of the CIA."[1]

## Laundering and cartel services

In February 1988 the accountant [[ramon-milian-rodriguez]] testified before a Senate Foreign Relations subcommittee that he had laundered billions of dollars of drug profits through Panama's banking system with Noriega's help, paying him a "ballpark figure" of between $320 million and $350 million.[1] Describing Noriega as "sort of a slippery and tricky fellow," Milian Rodriguez said he paid a commission of 1 to 10 percent depending on the transaction; in return Noriega gave him use of Panama's airports, banking system and security officers, and provided the identities of American drug agents, radio frequencies, and the schedules of Coast Guard and US Navy surveillance vessels.[1]

Lange argues Noriega obtained that surveillance and enforcement intelligence from the CIA, which was protecting its own trafficking operation, and passed it to the [[medellin-cartel]].[1]

## From asset to liability

The congressional testimony marked, in Lange's telling, the beginning of the end for Noriega. Once his exposure threatened to implicate the broader network, he shifted from "untouchable" to "expendable": there was no way for [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]] to rehabilitate his image, so a new narrative was deployed in which Noriega would be painted as the face of narco-trafficking in Central and South America while Bush was cast as the president heroically fighting drug traffickers.[1]

Noriega was indicted by the Bush administration and Panama was invaded by the US military in order to arrest him. His capture and extradition became a major news story and, Lange contends, a narrative deployment that shielded Bush and the CIA from accusations of trafficking drugs.[1] He compares the treatment of Noriega to that of [[pablo-escobar]], likewise hunted down once his notoriety made him a liability, without any lasting reduction in the flow of drugs into the United States.[1]

## Cold War asset and go-between

Noriega was the de facto ruler of Panama from 1983 to 1989, an authoritarian who amassed a personal fortune through drug trafficking and who had longstanding ties to United States intelligence agencies before the U.S. invasion removed him from power.[2] Reporting cited by Joe Lange describes him as a valued CIA Cold War asset and a go-between in Central America's dirty wars who eventually became a monster U.S. spy bosses could no longer control.[2] He helped Washington combat Cuban and Soviet influence in the region, acting as an intermediary with the U.S.-backed [[nicaraguan-contras|Contra]] rebels fighting Daniel Ortega's Sandinista government and with both the Salvadoran government and its rebels — conflicts characterized by death squads, random killings and torture.[2] He was also closely associated with the Colombian [[medellin-cartel|Medellín cartel]] of [[pablo-escobar|Pablo Escobar]].[2]

Lange argues that [[george-hw-bush|Bush]] had trained and installed Noriega precisely to serve as a conduit for cocaine trafficking, with Panamanian airfields used to fly weapons to the Contras and drugs into the United States.[2] Funds from drug trafficking bought arms, paid fighters and suborned government officials; Noriega later claimed it was his refusal to help Lieutenant Colonel [[oliver-north|Oliver North]] arm the Contras that triggered the U.S. decision to drop him.[2] Every U.S. agency that dealt with him turned a blind eye to his corruption and drug dealing, allowing him to establish what one account called "the hemisphere's first narco-kleptocracy" — though Lange contends the CIA did not merely tolerate the trafficking but encouraged it.[2]

## Silencing and trial

Noriega's knowledge of U.S. operations in Central America was detailed and highly compromising, and he was said to have met Bush in person on more than one occasion.[2] The October 1989 coup attempt marked the turning point in Washington's attitude; once Congress began investigating the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra scandal]] he became a liability who had to be removed.[2] At his Miami trial the court refused to allow his defence to present evidence relating to his CIA work, his payments from the U.S. government, his knowledge of U.S. subversion in Central America, or his contacts with senior figures such as Bush — proceedings one commentator likened to an East European show trial with the outcome never in doubt.[2] Bush got his man, Noriega was silenced, nefarious U.S. behaviour in Central America was effectively concealed, and the concept of justified forcible regime change was reinforced.[2] Noriega died in May 2017 at the age of 83.[2]
