# United States Census Bureau

The United States Census Bureau is the federal statistical agency whose household survey work supplied the data behind reporting on financial hardship in major American metropolitan areas.[1] A Census Bureau survey highlighted the 15 largest US metro areas where residents face the most financial difficulties, almost all of which are under Democratic leadership.[1]

The survey asked 70,000 respondents in major metro areas, between March 5 and April 1, three questions: whether they had difficulty paying for usual household expenses, whether they experienced food scarcity, and whether they were unable to pay an energy bill.[1] Its findings identified Riverside, California; Houston, Texas; and Chicago, Illinois as the top three metro areas for financial hardship, with the exceptions of Dallas at number four and Miami at number six being the only listed areas not under liberal control.[1]

[[zerohedge]] framed the results against a backdrop of elevated inflation, high taxes, soaring violent crime, and a migrant crisis in blue cities, and [[ashe-in-america]] argued in the [[badlands-news-brief]] that the data showed the results of Democrat policies, adding that Denver — absent from the list — had become a massive homeless encampment and had reallocated $89.9 million from police and fire to migrant services.[1]

## Citizenship question and apportionment fight

In January 2025, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was reported to be quietly reigniting a fight over the census with potentially major implications for power and policy, including the reapportionment of House seats and the distribution of billions of dollars in federal funding.[2] Trump had pushed hard to add a citizenship question to the 2020 census — which critics argued would discourage participation even among people legally in the country — with the ultimate goal of excluding immigrants in the country illegally from the apportionment count.[2] Historically, that has not been the practice: the 14th Amendment requires House seats to be apportioned by "counting the whole number of persons in each State," and non-citizens have never been excluded from apportionment totals.[2]

The proposal was litigated heavily, and the Supreme Court ultimately ruled that the Trump administration could not add the question, largely on procedural grounds.[2] Had Trump prevailed, the consequences would have been substantial, since the constitutionally mandated census resets the number of House seats — and therefore Electoral College votes — allotted to each state and directs billions in federal spending.[2]

Badlands host [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that the true US population is unlikely to be anywhere near 340 million Americans, noting that the census measures total people present in the country regardless of citizenship and that the underlying data is hard to obtain, inaccurate, and subject to manipulation — raising questions about both the fiscal burden and the integrity of elections.[2]
