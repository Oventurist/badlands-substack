# Bluesky

Bluesky is a social media platform that became a point of political friction in October 2025 when the [[white-house|White House]] joined it and was almost immediately mass-blocked by its users.[1]

## The White House account

The White House account of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] joined Bluesky on a Friday evening and, in less than 48 hours, had been blocked by more than 92,000 users according to VQV, an app that tracks Bluesky statistics.[1] That made it the second most-blocked account on the platform, behind only Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]], who had accumulated 166,000 blocks since joining in June.[1] Reporting noted that at the prevailing rate the White House would soon leapfrog the vice president for the position of most-blocked account — infamous or venerable depending on the observer.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands host [[canncon|CannCon]] treated the development as an advance into hostile information terrain rather than a setback, writing that "sounds like we're advancing on BlueSky now" and urging Badlanders to "mount up," noting his own presence on the platform.[1] The episode was presented in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] as one front in the wider [[information-war|information war]].[1]

## Growth among news influencers (2025)

A [[pew-research-center|Pew Research Center]] analysis reported in June 2025 found that the share of "news influencers" with a Bluesky account almost doubled in the four months after Election Day, rising from 21% in November to 43% by March, even as the fast-growing X alternative was still figuring out how best to generate revenue.[2] Adoption was heavily skewed by politics: 75% of influencers identifying as left-wing had a Bluesky account, compared with just 15% of those on the right.[2] Pew counts as a news influencer any private individual with more than 100,000 followers who posts regularly about current events, as distinct from media outlets such as *The New York Times* or *The Wall Street Journal*.[2] Coverage framed the pattern as evidence that social-media news consumption has become a political echo chamber.[2]
