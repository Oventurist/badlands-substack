# John Jacob Astor

John Jacob Astor was a European immigrant who arrived in the United States after the Revolutionary War, made large profits in the fur trade with Europe, and became the first multimillionaire in America.[1] In the Badlands Media article "The Battle for Control," author [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presents Astor as an archetype of the financial elite: a man who profited enormously from crises that impoverished ordinary people, and whose career prefigures that of modern financiers such as [[alwaleed-bin-talal|Prince Alwaleed bin Talal Al Saud]].[1]

## Bailout of City Bank of New York

Astor is credited with rescuing City Bank of New York — the institution later known as [[citibank|Citibank]] — during the [[panic-of-1837|Panic of 1837]].[1] The bank, along with other New York institutions, had suspended redemption of its currency at full face value in gold and silver because it did not hold enough specie to cover its liabilities.[1] Astor, then the wealthiest man in the country, deposited substantial funds in the bank and in doing so effectively took control of one of the most powerful banks in the United States.[1] Lange characterizes this as Citibank's first bailout, and notes it would not be its last.[1]

As part of his intervention, Astor installed [[moses-taylor|Moses Taylor]], an importer of Cuban sugar, on the bank's board of directors.[1] Taylor went on to transform the bank into a national financial institution with international connections.[1]

## Real estate speculation

The Panic of 1837 was one of the worst years ever for property values in New York City; many New Yorkers lost homes to foreclosure, and the state passed a bill granting a year's grace period for delinquent mortgage payments.[1] While investors across the city were ruined — the economist Milton Friedman reportedly compared the five-year crisis in severity to the Great Depression — Astor prospered, buying up $224,000 in Manhattan real estate amid the freefall in prices.[1] When interest rates later climbed to seven percent and owners could no longer pay the mortgages he controlled, Astor foreclosed on scores of them.[1]

He became known as the landlord of New York City, acquiring property at rock-bottom prices and raising rents to force occupants off land they had themselves developed.[1] Lange uses this record to argue that the financial elite deliberately cause panics, recessions, and depressions and then profit by buying assets at fire-sale prices.[1]

## Fur monopoly and congressional favor

After the [[war-of-1812|War of 1812]] ended in 1815, Astor successfully lobbied the U.S. [[congress|Congress]] for a series of protectionist bills designed to prevent a repeat of his financial losses in the Pacific Northwest.[1] An 1816 statute barred non-U.S. citizens from owning fur businesses in U.S. territory, forcing the Canadian company that had bought Fort Astoria to sell its holdings below the Great Lakes to Astor.[1] Six years later Congress voted to close all trading posts operated by foreign governments, leaving Astor with a near monopoly on the fur trade.[1] Lange treats this as one of the first monopolies in America and argues that Congress still passes laws encouraging monopolies from which its members benefit through political contributions.[1]

## Opium trade

Astor died in 1848 hailed as a titan of trade and a philanthropist, but part of his fortune came from smuggling opium into China against imperial orders.[1] The resulting riches made him one of the world's most powerful merchants and, in the account cited by Lange, helped create the world's first widespread opioid epidemic.[1] Lange places this within a broader argument that drug trafficking has always been a major revenue source for the entrenched financial elite.[1]

## Financing Aaron Burr's escape

In a separate Badlands article, [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] records that after [[aaron-burr|Aaron Burr]]'s 1807 acquittal on treason charges, popular rage towards the former vice president made life within the republic impossible, and Burr used a $40,000 "gift" from Astor to make his way in disguise to Canada.[2] From there Burr's nephew George Prevost, then Governor General, supplied letters of introduction to Lord Castlereagh, and Burr sailed from Nova Scotia to London to lodge with [[jeremy-bentham|Jeremy Bentham]].[2]
