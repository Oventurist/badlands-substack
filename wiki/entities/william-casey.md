# William J. Casey

William J. Casey was Director of the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]], serving during the Reagan administration.[1]

## The 1981 disinformation quotation

Casey is widely quoted in alternative-media circles for a remark attributed to him in 1981: "We'll know our disinformation program is complete when everything the American public believes is false."[1] Badlands Media contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] invoked the quotation in June 2023 while criticizing the [[2023-odni-covid-origins-report|declassified intelligence community report]] on the origins of COVID-19, using it to argue that the intelligence community's public assessments should be read as narrative management rather than disinterested analysis.[1]

## Architect of the NGO layer

Badlands Media credits Casey, as [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]]'s CIA director, with the creative restructuring of American covert influence work in the 1980s: the administration successfully appealed to both sides of the aisle to create a series of new institutions carrying on the CIA's work ostensibly to fight the Cold War, but at the NGO layer rather than the government-agency level.[2] The premise was that additional plausible deniability layers would make operations harder to trace back to the CIA, the Department of Defense, or the [[state-department|State Department]], and the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] was the flagship result.[2]

## OSS background and the 1980 Reagan campaign

During World War II Casey worked for the [[office-of-strategic-services|Office of Strategic Services]], the precursor to the CIA, where he became head of its Secret Intelligence Branch in Europe.[3] He served in the United States Naval Reserve until December 1944 before remaining in his OSS post as a civilian until resigning in September 1945; as an officer he attained the rank of lieutenant and was awarded the Bronze Star Medal for meritorious achievement.[3]

Casey took over as manager of [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]]'s 1980 presidential campaign after Reagan lost the Iowa caucus and then ousted campaign director John P. Sears during the New Hampshire primary.[3] Contemporary coverage in the Washington Post recorded a rocky start — Casey misremembered the name of the campaign finance director, misplaced the date of a key primary, and cancelled airplane charters without telling the press, stranding three network crews in Atlanta — but noted that within three weeks he was firmly in charge.[3] As campaign manager he helped broker Reagan's unlikely alliance with vice presidential nominee [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]], then served on the transition team before being named director of the CIA.[3]

Joe Lange presents this sequence as a deliberate cabal manoeuvre: an OSS intelligence veteran installed atop the campaign who steered Bush onto the ticket and then took the agency's directorship, placing the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] in control of the Reagan administration and cementing [[shadow-government|shadow government]] influence over the executive branch.[3] In his phrasing, Reagan was "surrounded by a den of vipers, and I don't think he realized it."[3]

## Creation of the NED

Badlands Media argues that although the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] was technically created in 1983 by President [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]], it was "really birthed" in the office of then-CIA director William Casey.[4] Casey's aim was to shift covert [[cia|CIA]] operations, such as funding anti-communist movements, to an overt organization that would escape the scrutiny the agency faced after the intelligence scandals of the 1970s.[4] When Congress inserted language barring current or former CIA officials from the endowment, the agency's congressional liaison delivered Casey's demand that the provision be struck to Rep. [[dante-fascell|Dante Fascell]] late in the 1983 session; Fascell consented.[4]

## The 1980 October Surprise allegations

"The Shadow Game" details allegations that Casey, as [[ronald-reagan|Reagan's]] 1980 campaign chairman, negotiated with Iran to delay the release of the American hostages until after Election Day.[5] The fate of the hostages was a pivotal issue in the 1980 election; they had been seized when followers of Ayatollah [[ruhollah-khomeini|Ruhollah Khomeini]] took the United States Embassy in Teheran in November 1979, a rescue mission failed in the Iranian desert in April 1980, and Reagan campaign officials feared their return could swing the election to [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]].[5]

[[gary-sick|Gary Sick]], a Middle East specialist and Carter White House staffer, first reported that Casey met Hojatolislam [[mehdi-karrubi|Mehdi Karrubi]], a representative of Khomeini, twice at the Ritz Hotel in Madrid in late July 1980; his principal source was the Iranian arms dealer Jamshid Hashemi, who said he and his brother Cyrus arranged the meetings, attended by Casey, the Hashemi brothers and an unnamed American intelligence officer.[5] Sick published his findings in the 1991 book *October Surprise*.[5]

Separately, [[ben-barnes|Ben Barnes]] said that [[john-connally|John Connally]] took him through Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Israel between July 18 and August 11, 1980 to pass Iran the message not to release the hostages before the election because Reagan would give a better deal, and that on returning Connally briefed Casey in an airport lounge.[5] The alleged arrangement, finalized in Paris that October, was that a future Reagan administration would ship arms to Tehran through Israel in exchange for the hostages being held back.[5] The captives were released on the day of Reagan's inauguration, minutes after his address concluded.[5]

[[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues this 1980 deal — made on behalf of [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] — was separate from and prior to the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] scandal, and established the Bush–Casey working relationship with Iran that later made Iran-Contra possible.[5] Lange also notes Casey's later alliance as CIA Director with [[john-poindexter|John Poindexter]], through which the agency built a secure hotline funnelling intelligence from across the government through a CIA filter after the [[1983-beirut-barracks-bombings|Beirut barracks bombing]].[5]

## BCCI and the Afghan campaign

"Who Is The Real Enemy?" cites WhoWhatWhy in describing Casey's use of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] in the 1980s to promote his own plans for [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] as one of the most significant cases in which the agency became a front for sanctioned violence.[6] His Afghan initiatives aroused the concern of the CIA's professional operatives and analysts, including deputy directors Bobby Ray Inman and John McMahon, but Casey continued making high-level decisions about the Afghan campaign outside regular channels while meeting in secret with foreigners.[6]

One such counterpart was [[agha-hasan-abedi|Agha Hasan Abedi]], a close adviser to General [[muhammad-zia-ul-haq|Zia]] of [[pakistan|Pakistan]] and head of the [[bank-of-credit-and-commerce-international|Bank of Credit and Commerce International]].[6] Badlands Media notes that Casey led the CIA in the Reagan administration at the time [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] was establishing the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] that would control the entire executive branch.[6] BCCI offered Casey a vehicle for off-the-books operations such as the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] arms deal, but the largest by far was support to the [[mujahideen|Afghan mujahideen]] resistance.[6] Abedi arranged repeated meetings between Casey and Zia, and with Saudi intelligence chiefs [[kamal-adham|Kamal Adham]] and [[turki-al-faisal|Prince Turki al-Faisal]], both BCCI shareholders.[6]

The article characterizes Casey's involvement with BCCI as a multi-billion-dollar backdoor operation with a criminal bank accused even by its own insiders of global involvement with drug shipments, smuggled gold, stolen military secrets, assassinations, bribery, extortion, covert intelligence operations and weapons deals — with the lasting consequence that BCCI's drug clients, notably [[gulbuddin-hekmatyar|Gulbuddin Hekmatyar]], became dominant figures in the expanded Afghan heroin trade.[6]
