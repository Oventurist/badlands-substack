# Facebook Agile Silicon Team (FAST)

The Facebook Agile Silicon Team, known internally as FAST, was the custom-silicon unit inside [[meta-platforms|Meta]]'s metaverse-oriented [[reality-labs|Reality Labs]] division.[1] The unit, which employed roughly 600 people, worked on developing custom chips intended to equip Meta's devices to perform unique tasks and operate more efficiently, differentiating them from other entrants in the nascent augmented- and virtual-reality market.[1]

## October 2023 layoffs

Reuters reported on October 3, 2023, citing two sources familiar with the matter, that Meta planned to lay off employees in the FAST unit the following day.[1] Employees were informed of the coming layoffs in a post on Meta's internal discussion forum, Workplace, on Tuesday, and were told they would learn their individual status with the company by early Wednesday morning.[1] A Meta spokesperson declined to comment on the plans, and Reuters was unable to determine the extent of the cuts.[1] A restructuring of FAST had been expected since the spring of 2023, when Meta hired a new executive to lead the unit.[1]

## Competitive position

Meta had struggled to produce chips capable of competing with silicon made by external providers and had turned to the chipmaker [[qualcomm|Qualcomm]] to supply the processors used in the devices it already had on the market.[1] Reuters framed deep cuts as a potential obstacle to chief executive [[mark-zuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]]'s effort to build augmented- and virtual-reality products — particularly the AR glasses he predicted "will redefine our relationship with technology."[1]

## Commentary

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] disputed that analysis, arguing that sourcing silicon externally requires no overhead for Meta while the in-house unit carried 600 employees and their fully loaded costs, so the layoffs would instead free significant capital for reinvestment in the company's long-term vision.[1] She characterized the move as a strategic sourcing decision rather than a reaction to a moment in the markets, and situated it within a broader trend of corporate layoffs attributed to "industry shifts" as firms accelerate digital and AI transformations.[1] She added that, unlike Hollywood writers or Michigan automakers, the "laptop class" does not strike, and that in an age of transformation businesses adapt or die while employees rarely have a say.[1]
