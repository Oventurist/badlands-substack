# Jonathan Turley

**Jonathan Turley** is an American legal scholar and commentator whose analysis of constitutional questions is frequently cited in Badlands coverage.[1]

## Commentary on Democratic war-powers reversals

In a March 8, 2026 post highlighted as a bonus item in the March 10, 2026 [[badlands-brief|Badlands Brief]], Turley observed that several Democratic leaders then demanding congressional authorization before any military action against [[iran|Iran]] had previously endorsed expansive unilateral presidential war powers during earlier administrations.[1] The Brief framed this as a "face-plant," noting that Democratic lawmakers were facing questions about their past statements supporting broad presidential authority to conduct military operations without new congressional approval.[1]

The scrutiny arrived amid live debate over whether President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was constitutionally required to seek authorization from [[congress|Congress]] before striking Iran.[1]

## Commentary on the Blanche ethics complaint

In a May 31, 2026 commentary, Turley reported that a public interest group had filed an ethics complaint against Acting Attorney General [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] following the dismissal of the Tennessee criminal case against [[kilmar-abrego-garcia|Kilmar Abrego Garcia]].[2] The [[badlands-brief|Badlands Brief]] cited Turley's piece as the source for the complaint's existence while noting that the underlying complaint PDF could not be accessed in-browser and was not used to establish independent facts.[2] The case had been dismissed on May 22, 2026 by Judge [[waverly-crenshaw|Waverly Crenshaw]] on grounds of vindictive prosecution.[2]

## Commentary on the Afroman verdict (2026)

Following the unanimous March 19, 2026 jury verdict in favor of rapper [[afroman|Afroman]] in the Ohio civil suit brought by seven Adams County Sheriff's Office deputies, Turley called the outcome an important affirmation of the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] right to parody and criticize public officials.[3] He noted that a contrary result would have chilled artistic and political speech nationwide, and observed that "since the founding of the Republic, parody and songs have been used to criticize government officials and policies."[3]

## Commentary on Trump's 14th Amendment disqualification (2023)

In December 2023, Turley criticized media coverage of the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]]'s decision disqualifying [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the 2024 election ballot, writing that remarks by [[new-york-times|New York Times]] editorial board member [[mara-gay|Mara Gay]] on [[msnbc|MSNBC]] "captured the unhinged coverage" of the ruling.[4] He noted that the underlying theory under the [[fourteenth-amendment|14th Amendment]] had previously been rejected by jurists in various states, including many Democrats, and objected to Gay's framing in which anyone raising democratic or constitutional objections was cast as a modern-day confederate.[4] Turley was among those who had publicly questioned the constitutional basis for using the amendment to bar Trump from state ballots.[4]

## Commentary on comedy and cancel culture (2023)

In a May 29, 2023 post, Turley wrote about [[john-cleese|John Cleese]]'s refusal to accept the cutting of a scene from [[monty-pythons-life-of-brian|The Life of Brian]], framing it as the moment activists "met their match in a legend of comedy."[5] He situated the dispute in a longer record of comedians objecting that "woke activists are killing comedy," describing a complaint that "a group of perpetually pissed off, humorless people are remaking the world in their own image."[5]

Turley traced the trend from college campuses, which comedians now describe as "dead as venues" because no joke is safe unless it targets white straight males, Christians or conservatives, through hate-speech laws under which comedians have been prosecuted for "malicious communications" or for insulting groups and religious figures, to survey findings that six out of ten students view offensive jokes as hate speech.[5] He noted the selectivity of the objection to The Life of Brian: activists were not upset by the film's jokes about Italians, Christians and Jews, but by the scene in which a man announces he wants to become a woman and have a child.[5]

## Commentary on the New York hush-money trial (April 2024)

Appearing on Fox News on Monday, April 22, 2024, after opening statements in the Manhattan prosecution of [[donald-trump]] over allegedly falsifying business records, Turley said the case left him "in utter disbelief."[6] He argued that "in this case, Trump is right," calling it "an embarrassment" that the case was being presented in a New York courtroom and saying the day's arguments "did in fact capture all the problems here."[6] Turley described the legal theory as a lapsed state misdemeanor that prosecutors "zapped back into life by alleging that there was a campaign finance violation under the federal laws that doesn't exist," tied back to the 2016 election.[6]

Turley further argued that the [[trump-hush-money-case|case]] should not have been brought and, if brought, should not have been scheduled immediately before the election, describing "the split screen election" in which Trump sits in a courtroom while his opponent campaigns in key states such as Pennsylvania.[6] He concluded that the prosecution "brings home something that bothers a lot of Americans, including people who don't particularly like Trump, that this is the weaponization of the criminal legal system," which "we should all be able to condemn."[6] His remarks were quoted at length in the [[badlands-news-brief]].[6]

## Commentary on the SFSU response to the Riley Gaines assault (2023)

In an April 9, 2023 post, Turley criticized [[san-francisco-state-university|San Francisco State University]] for its response to the alleged assault on former swimmer [[riley-gaines|Riley Gaines]], who was reportedly attacked when she tried to speak about her views on transgender issues and had to be removed by police to a locked room for hours.[7] He wrote that the university's statement was "conspicuous in its failure to condemn the mob or promise accountability for these actions," and argued that it explained why students believed they had license to engage in such disruptions.[7] Turley described the event as the latest example of protesters "deplatforming" speakers with opposing views by shouting them down, and said that denying free expression and threatening those with opposing views "is the very antithesis of core principles" for any institution of higher education.[7] Instead of condemnation, he wrote, SFSU issued a statement that "seemed to express sympathy for the protesters and those who were exposed to her views."[7]

## Commentary on the June 2023 Harvard/Harris poll

In June 2023 Turley analyzed a Harvard/Harris survey showing that 55 percent of Americans believed [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s indictment was politically motivated and 56 percent believed it constituted election interference, calling the results bad news for Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] and the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]].[8] He noted that the view of the case appeared to be worsening, with only 44 percent regarding the prosecution as "the fair application of the law," and that 83 percent of Republicans and 55 percent of independents saw it as a political exercise.[8] Turley also highlighted that 65 percent believed President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] had mishandled classified material and 72 percent said the same of [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s email scandal, concluding that the Justice Department and the media had "lost the room" with the American people.[8] See [[harvard-harris-poll-on-the-trump-indictment|Harvard/Harris poll on the Trump indictment]].[8]

## Commentary on the Jenna Ellis plea

In October 2023 Turley analyzed the guilty plea of former Trump attorney [[jenna-ellis|Jenna Ellis]] in the Georgia election case brought by Fulton County District Attorney [[fani-willis|Fani Willis]], writing that while the impact of earlier pleas by figures such as [[sidney-powell|Sidney Powell]] was hard to judge, the Ellis plea carried "more ominous implications for the former president."[9] He observed that Powell had pleaded to relatively minor and easily provable charges involving unauthorized access to voting machines and areas, whereas Ellis pleaded guilty to false statements that could conceivably implicate [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] if she claimed he knew of their falsity and facilitated the crime.[9] Turley called it "the type of plea that tends to concentrate the mind," and noted that Ellis had recently broken with Trump, calling him a "malignant narcissist" who cannot admit mistakes.[9]

## Commentary on the Biden investigations (2023)

Turley was quoted in July 2023 Badlands commentary for his observation that the FBI and the Justice Department appeared to be the place "where investigations go to die," a remark invoked in relation to the stalled inquiries into the Biden family's finances.[10]

## Criticism of the Fourteenth Amendment disqualification theory (January 2024)

In a January 25, 2024 column titled "The Maine Event That Wasn't," Turley wrote that Maine Secretary of State [[shenna-bellows|Shenna Bellows]] had lost another attempt to force review of her disqualification decision before the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] heard argument on February 8.[11] The Maine Supreme Court declined to review the matter, and with Massachusetts also ruling that week against disqualification, Turley concluded that Colorado would "remain the outlier as the only state supreme court willing to embrace this dangerous and anti-democratic theory."[11]

Turley reiterated his long-standing criticism of what he called an unfounded theory, noting that its advocates had been rejected in a dozen states while continuing to seek receptive judges, and that they "had Bellows at hello" given her prior public statements about the "Jan. 6 insurrection."[11] He judged her decision "poorly written and conclusory in virtually every respect," and explained that because Superior Court Justice [[michaela-murphy|Michaela Murphy]] had only deferred rather than ruled, the Maine high court dismissed Bellows's appeal "as interlocutory and not justiciable."[11]


## Commentary on Ireland's speech crackdown (2023)

In a post published November 30, 2023, Turley argued that the Irish government was moving to crack down on free speech following an anti-immigration riot in Dublin, describing the measure as one of the most chilling speech rollbacks to date.[12] He noted that he had previously discussed the growing anti-free-speech movement in [[ireland|Ireland]], and that in his book *The Indispensable Right: Free Speech in the Age of Rage* he argued such crackdowns tend to arrive during periods of public panic or anger, which anti-free-speech advocates exploit to persuade the public to surrender the right to censors and prosecutors.[12] The proximate excuse, he wrote, was a riot leading to the arrest of 34 people and extensive property damage during protests challenging government policy on undocumented migrants; the resulting [[criminal-justice-incitement-to-violence-or-hatred-bill|bill]] criminalizes "preparing or possessing material likely to incite violence or hatred against persons on account of their protected characteristics," including national or ethnic origin and "transgender and a gender other than those of male and female."[12]

## "Win by attrition" analysis (February 2024)

In a February 11, 2024 column titled "Could Trump Win By Simple Attrition Rather Than Vindication?", Turley invoked Woody Allen's line that "80 percent of success is showing up," arguing that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had proved the same could be said about "just sticking around."[13] He wrote that Trump had enjoyed one of his best weeks as cases and critics seemed to implode — from the ballot disqualification effort in Washington to the scandal in Georgia — while cautioning that Trump was "not out of the woods" and faced significant threats in what was becoming a war of attrition.[13]

Turley noted that the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] had given the disqualification effort "a chilly reception that bordered on the glacial," that law professors such as Harvard's [[laurence-tribe|Laurence Tribe]] had called the Fourteenth Amendment theory "unassailable" while the justices seemed utterly unconvinced, and that even liberal justice [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]] called the effort anti-democratic.[13] He described the Georgia case as floundering amid allegations against District Attorney [[fani-willis|Fani Willis]] over her relationship with special prosecutor [[nathan-wade|Nathan Wade]], and cited calls for the removal of Manhattan District Attorney [[alvin-bragg|Alvin Bragg]] in New York over his soft-on-crime record.[13]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] noted that the mainstream press had branded Turley a maverick, which he found amusing "since he's considered rather tame by our standards," and praised the framing that Trump need not win his cases so much as outlast them as they crumble under their own corruption and incompetence — a view he linked to the "narrowed definitions and authority" analysis advanced by [[just-human|Just Human]] and [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]].[13]


## Commentary on the Trump Twitter subpoena

In an August 10, 2023 post, Turley criticized the secret subpoena of Twitter obtained by Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] for records tied to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s account, calling the $350,000 fine imposed by U.S. District Court judge [[beryl-howell|Beryl Howell]] for a three-day delay "hefty."[14] He identified the two most surprising and concerning elements as the secrecy of the subpoena and Howell's partial justification of it on the ground that Trump was a flight risk, neither of which he considered warranted even if the subpoena was otherwise appropriate.[14] Turley noted that the nondisclosure order barred the company from telling Trump or anyone else about the warrant despite Trump already knowing he was under investigation, and observed that the court's concern that Trump might change his course of conduct was implausible because he had remained "entirely undeterred" in his posting habits despite widespread criticism.[14]

## Analysis of the Jack Smith report (January 2025)

In a January 2025 Fox News opinion column, Turley assessed the first volume of special counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]]'s final report, released at midnight on Tuesday, January 14, and compared it to the Supreme Court's Dobbs decision in the sense that "we had seen it before."[15] He argued there was little new in the document and that its critical omission was any explanation of how Smith had destroyed his own cases against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[15] Turley singled out Smith's reliance on what he called a dubious concurrence by Justice [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]], an interpretation he had previously described as too clever by half.[15]

Turley characterized much of the report as "vintage Smith" in dismissing countervailing precedent while insisting he could "obtain and sustain a conviction at trial," conceding Smith might obtain a conviction before a Washington, D.C. jury and a motivated judge but arguing he could never have sustained one.[15] He noted the report's repetition of conclusory evidence such as Trump saying "fight" ten times in his January 6, 2021 speech, its minimization of the immunity decision, and above all its treatment of the obstruction claims as indicative of a prosecutor who repeatedly lost cases by overextending constitutional and statutory authority.[15] He observed that the Supreme Court's ruling in [[fischer-v-united-states|Fischer v. United States]] would potentially affect hundreds of January 6 cases through dismissals or resentencings, including Trump's own pending four-count prosecution with its two obstruction counts.[15]

## Commentary on the Trump conviction appeal (2024)

Writing in [[the-hill|The Hill]] under the headline "Bragg's thrill kill in Manhattan could prove short-lived on appeal," Turley argued that the conviction of former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] on 34 felonies produced "citywide celebrations" and a "thrill-kill environment" extending into the media, and that "the celebrants would be wise to think twice before mounting this trophy kill on the political wall."[16] In his assessment the trial was "a target-rich environment for an appeal, with multiple layers of reversible error."[16] He was less convinced by arguments that Trump could not receive a fair trial in a district that voted roughly 90 percent against him, writing that "the problem was not the jury, but the prosecutors and the judge."[16]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] emphasized that Turley had been in the courtroom every day of the trial, live-tweeting developments, and that the fact a legal scholar still had questions about the charges and legal standards after observing the entire proceeding first hand "should give all of us pause."[16]

## New York Times hypocrisy on troop deployments (March 2024)

On March 7, 2024, Turley published a column arguing that [[new-york-times|The New York Times]] faced claims of hypocrisy over its coverage of the deployment of troops.[17] He noted Senator [[tom-cotton|Tom Cotton]]'s observation that the paper reported matter-of-factly on Governor [[kathy-hochul|Kathy Hochul]]'s plan to send [[national-guard|National Guard]] troops into New York City's subway system, while four years earlier it had publicly denounced Cotton for an op-ed urging the same remedy against violent riots in Washington.[17] Turley, who had previously written on the affair, observed that the Cotton column was historically accurate and that critics never explained what in it was false or outside the range of permissible interpretation.[17] He described the paper's response as "a breathtaking surrender," in which it apologized, promised an investigation into how an opposing view reached its pages, and pledged to reduce the number of editorials it ran; when Democratic officials actually ordered such a deployment, he wrote, the Times offered "little more than a journalistic shrug."[17]
