# Donald Gregg

Donald Phinney Gregg (born December 5, 1927) is a retired American politician, [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] employee and U.S. Ambassador to South Korea who worked for the agency for 31 years, from 1951 to 1982.[1] He served as a [[national-security-council|National Security Council]] advisor from 1979 to 1982 and then as National Security Advisor to Vice President [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] from 1982 to 1989.[1] From 1975 to 1980 Gregg served at CIA headquarters in Washington, D.C., where his responsibilities included responding to the "Pike Committee" investigation of the agency.[1]

Joe Lange's account emphasizes that Gregg was a career CIA officer who sat on the National Security Council alongside [[oliver-north|Oliver North]] and John Poindexter before moving to the Vice President's staff, and that he was at CIA headquarters during Bush's tenure as Director of Central Intelligence in 1976, helping fend off the congressional investigations of the agency then under way.[1]

## Supervision of Felix Rodriguez

Special Counsel Lawrence Walsh's report on [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] records that during the Vietnam War Gregg supervised CIA officer [[felix-rodriguez|Felix Rodriguez]] and that the two kept in contact after the war.[1] The report states that Gregg introduced Rodriguez to Vice President Bush in January 1985, that Rodriguez met the Vice President again in Washington in May 1986, and that he met Bush briefly in Miami on May 20, 1986.[1] Lange disputes the framing of the word "introduced," arguing that Bush had known Rodriguez for a very long time and that Rodriguez was heavily involved in [[shadow-government]] operations well before 1985.[1]

## The October 1986 phone calls

When a [[southern-air-transport|Southern Air Transport]] C-123K carrying 10,000 pounds of ammunition failed to return from a drop over [[nicaragua]] on October 5, 1986, Rodriguez did not notify the Defense Department or the CIA, but instead attempted to reach Gregg.[1] Failing to reach him, Rodriguez telephoned Gregg's deputy, Army colonel Samuel Watson, who relayed the information to the White House Situation Room, prompting a search-and-rescue mission toward the Nicaraguan border.[1] Lange treats this chain of calls as the first documentary evidence linking the office of the Vice President directly to the illegal Contra resupply operation.[1]
