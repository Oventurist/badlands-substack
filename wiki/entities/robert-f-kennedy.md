# Robert F. Kennedy

Robert F. Kennedy served as United States attorney general and as the closest adviser to his brother, President [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]].[1] He is not to be confused with his son [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]], a prominent figure in the 2024 Trump coalition.[1]

## ExComm and the war for peace

In the 1960s, the Kennedy brothers formed the unofficial advisory council known as [[executive-committee-of-the-national-security-council|ExComm]] to keep peacemaking efforts siloed from the [[joint-chiefs-of-staff|Joint Chiefs of Staff]] and the intelligence agencies.[1] Officials excluded from the arrangement mocked the group of Kennedys and their Boston-area friends, including [[kenneth-odonnell|Kenneth O'Donnell]], as the "Irish Mafia."[1]

Working around the [[military-industrial-complex|Military Industrial Complex]] and the press, these men opened backchannels to Soviet leader [[nikita-khrushchev|Nikita Khrushchev]] in order to avert war.[1] As tensions escalated, Robert Kennedy is remembered for the blunt instruction "can the f***ing chiefs" as tempers flared during what Badlands Media calls the war for peace.[1]

## Attorney general and senator

Kennedy served as attorney general first under his brother and then under [[lyndon-b-johnson|Lyndon Johnson]] until 1965, and his life throughout was defined by a strong commitment to peace, development and cooperation with justice for all races.[2] Speaking to crowds in apartheid South Africa in 1961 after the ruling government refused to meet him, he declared that "few have the greatness to bend history itself, but each of us can work to change a small portion of events," and that each act of courage "sends forth a tiny ripple of hope" whose combined current "can sweep down the mightiest walls of oppression and resistance."[2]

After leaving the Justice Department in 1965 to become a senator and later a presidential candidate, Kennedy concentrated on reviving his brother's Peace Corps, attacking the growing war in [[vietnam-war|Vietnam]], opposing racism at home and ending the despair of ghetto poverty that no one wished to look at.[2] Asked during the [[1967-detroit-riots|1967 Detroit riots]] what he would do as president, he answered that he would force the media to show America the hopelessness of ghetto life directly.[2] In a November 1967 appearance on Face the Nation he asked whether the United States had the right "to say we're going to kill tens of thousands of people… kill women and children," concluding that those who stay at home "must feel it when we use napalm."[2]

## Indianapolis and assassination

In the summer of 1967 Kennedy and [[martin-luther-king-jr|Martin Luther King Jr.]] began a close collaboration, with King telling associates the civil rights movement would put its full support behind Kennedy in the run-up to the 1968 election; Kennedy had earlier intervened in King's October 1960 arrest in Atlanta.[2] When King was murdered on April 4, 1968, Kennedy ignored advice to cancel a rally and spoke unprotected from a flatbed truck in the Indianapolis ghetto, and the city became the only major American city where no riots occurred.[2]

One day after winning the California Democratic primary, Kennedy was shot and killed at the Ambassador Hotel in Los Angeles on June 4–5, 1968.[2] Badlands Media argues that the official lone-gunman account naming [[sirhan-sirhan|Sirhan Sirhan]] collapses under the forensic and documentary record, and that the [[assassination-of-robert-f-kennedy|assassination]] was an elaborate operation to prevent him from reaching the White House.[2] Badlands calls him "the man who should have been president."[2]

## The Marilyn Monroe allegations

Kennedy is the central figure in a widely circulated narrative alleging that he and his brother arranged the death of [[marilyn-monroe|Marilyn Monroe]] in August 1962.[3] [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] argued that this narrative rests on unseen diary pages known only through [[mike-rothmiller|Mike Rothmiller]]'s anonymous sources and on wiretap recordings claimed only by [[fred-otash|Fred Otash]], a private investigator who worked for the [[fbi|FBI]], [[cia|CIA]], the Mafia and Jimmy Hoffa.[3] The diary is said to contain admissions that Kennedy directed the invasion of Cuba, oversaw a global assassination complex, and planned a false-flag killing of American civilians — the last of which was in fact [[operation-northwoods|Operation Northwoods]], proposed by General [[lyman-lemnitzer|Lyman Lemnitzer]] and rejected by [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]].[3]

Ehret noted that Kennedy did communicate with Monroe during the period when [[20th-century-fox|Fox Pictures]] was suing her over her contract and she was creating her own production company, but argued there is no basis for reading that contact as a sexual relationship.[3] Kennedy was also close to the actress Judy Garland, and with his brother worked with director John Frankenheimer on Seven Days in May.[3] Rothmiller organized and testified at the secret grand jury on Kennedy's own murder, which never addressed [[mk-ultra|MK Ultra]] or the close-range wounds in the back of his neck.[3]
