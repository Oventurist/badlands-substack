# James Jesus Angleton

James Jesus Angleton (December 9, 1917 – May 11, 1987) was an American intelligence operative who served as chief of counterintelligence for the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] from 1954 to 1975, and whom Director of Central Intelligence [[richard-helms|Richard Helms]] described as "recognized as the dominant counterintelligence figure in the non-communist world."[1]

Angleton served in the [[office-of-strategic-services|Office of Strategic Services]], the wartime predecessor of the CIA, in Italy and London during the Second World War. After the war he returned to Washington, D.C. and became one of the founding officers of the CIA, initially responsible for the collection of foreign intelligence and for liaison with counterpart services in allied countries. Beginning in 1951 he was responsible for "the Israel desk," acting as liaison with Israel's [[mossad|Mossad]] and Shin Bet. In 1954 Allen Dulles promoted him to chief of the Counterintelligence Staff.[1]

## Dismissal and the Church Committee era

In December 1974, CIA Director [[william-colby|William Colby]] fired Angleton after the [[new-york-times|New York Times]] revealed that the then-unknown counterintelligence chief had overseen a massive program to spy on Americans involved in anti-war and black nationalist movements — a violation of the CIA's charter. Coming four months after the resignation of [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]], Angleton's fall was described as the denouement of the [[watergate|Watergate]] scandal, propelling Congress to probe the CIA for the first time.[1]

The subsequent Senate investigation headed by Senator [[frank-church|Frank Church]] — the [[church-committee|Church Committee]] — exposed assassination conspiracies, unauthorized mail opening, collaboration with human rights abusers, infiltration of news organizations, and the [[mk-ultra|MK ULTRA]] mind-control experiments. The exposure of Angleton's operations set off a political avalanche that engulfed the agency in 1975 and after: the post-Watergate Congress established the House and [[senate-intelligence-committee|Senate intelligence committees]] to oversee covert operations, and the [[foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act|Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act]] required the CIA to obtain warrants before spying on Americans.[1]
