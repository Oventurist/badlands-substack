# Charlemagne

Charlemagne, the Frankish king and emperor, was singled out by the Volkish archaeologist [[wilhelm-teudt|Wilhelm Teudt]] of [[heinrich-himmler|Himmler's]] [[ahnenerbe|Ahnenerbe]] as the cause of Germany's loss of its pure pagan spirit through the Christianisation of the Germanic tribes.[1]

Teudt's 1929 *Ancient German Sanctuaries* urged Germans to "unlearn our belief that the Roman Church under the Frankish Emperor Charles brought civilization to the barbaric tribes of Germany," presenting the pre-Christian Germans instead as materially refined and culturally accomplished.[1] The Nazi project of "undoing the damage of Charlemagne" thus formed part of the wider Ahnenerbe effort to restore a pagan Germanic religion.[1]

## The Carolingian ecumenical program

Part 7 of the same series presents Charlemagne in an entirely different light, as one of four great ecumenical leaders alongside [[harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]], [[king-bulan|King Bulan]] of [[khazaria|Khazaria]] and the Tang Emperor Xuanzong.[2] The Venetian Empire and the Ultramontane Church, heirs of the collapsed Roman oligarchy, are said to have hated the rise of the Carolingian Empire and the Augustinian humanist educational and economic reforms enacted during his reign, and above all the alliances he built with Harun al-Rashid and Bulan.[2]

Charlemagne ended the anti-Jewish policy dominant in Europe for centuries and granted Jews rights to land ownership and titles unprecedented for the age.[2] Whenever he or his father [[pepin-the-short|Pepin the Short]] established diplomatic embassies with the Muslim [[abbasid-dynasty|Abbasids]], the envoys chosen were always Jewish.[2] He married the daughter of Natronai al Makhir, the Jewish king of [[septimania|Septimania]] descended from the House of David, and later divided Septimania's governance into thirds under Archbishop Thomas of Normandy, an Islamic Viscount and Jewish rule.[2] In 800 CE Harun al-Rashid gave him control of the Holy Land under Muslim protection, an entente negotiated by Charlemagne's Jewish ambassador to Baghdad, Isaac of Rachen, according to the records of the Monk Zacharias.[2]

Ultramontane Pope Stephen III attacked this policy in 768 CE in a letter to Archbishop Aribert complaining that "Christians work the vineyards and the fields of these Jews… What communion hath light with darkness and what concord hath Christ with Balial?"[2] Both Pepin and Charlemagne ignored the Vatican's many demands to renounce their ecumenical program.[2]

## The Carolingian Renaissance

The article credits the Carolingian Renaissance to bold reforms in infrastructure — vast roads, bridges over the Rhine, canals, cathedrals and schools — the Irish monastery movement, and financial reforms under which private financiers lost control as Charlemagne's government took charge of coinage and credit.[2] The philosophical basis for his break with anti-Jewish hatred is identified as the [[doctrine-of-witness|doctrine of Witness]] formulated by St. Augustine in the early 5th century.[2] The alliance he built lasted some ninety years before Venetian intrigues drew his grandchildren into civil war, breaking the empire apart through the 842 Oath of Strasbourg into regions that became the borders of modern Europe.[2]
