# Joseph Fischer

Joseph Fischer is a January 6 defendant whose legal challenge to the federal government's use of the "obstructing an official proceeding" charge reached the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] as [[fischer-v-united-states|Fischer v. United States]].[1] Fischer's case argues that prosecutors adopted an overly broad reading of the statute — a provision of the [[sarbanes-oxley-act|Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002]] governing financial record keeping and documentation — in order to bring hundreds of cases stemming from the breach of the U.S. Capitol, including the charges against former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[1]

The Supreme Court's decision to take up Fischer's challenge had immediate practical consequences: at least two January 6 defendants were released from custody pending the ruling, which was expected by the end of June following spring argument.[1] Roughly 152 defendants had been charged under the provision at the time, with more than 100 sentenced and a statutory maximum of 20 years' imprisonment.[1]

## Supreme Court review granted, December 2023

On December 13, 2023, the Supreme Court agreed to hear Joseph Fischer's appeal, making his case the vehicle for deciding whether January 6 defendants can be charged under the obstruction provision of the 2002 Corporate Fraud Accountability Act.[2] The provision, a product of the Enron collapse, had been used against roughly 327 Capitol riot defendants as well as Donald Trump, with the dispute turning on whether the phrase "otherwise obstructs, influences, or impedes any official proceeding" reaches conduct beyond document tampering.[2] Judge Carl J. Nichols had adopted the narrower reading in Fischer's favor before the D.C. Circuit reversed in a split decision, setting up the Supreme Court's review.[2] Badlands commentary described the grant of certiorari as a potential means of "torpedoing the entire J6 narrative from the ground up."[2]
