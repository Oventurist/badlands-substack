# Tang Dynasty

The Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE, given in the article variously as 618–912) is described by Matthew Ehret as a Confucian civilization that revived the Silk Road trade routes and served as the eastern anchor of an ecumenical alliance linking Asia and Europe.[1]

## Revival of the Silk Road

The Tang emperor revived the [[silk-road|Silk Road]] corridors that had fallen apart after the collapse of the Han Dynasty around 200 CE, and with that revival Buddhists, Hindus, Nestorian Christians, Zoroastrians, Muslims and Jews flocked to China.[1] The first recorded influx of Jews into China occurred in 618 CE with the start of the dynasty, and the resulting Jewish presence was anomalously large.[1] Professor Pan Guang is quoted saying that Jews in Tang China "could preserve their native customs and religious beliefs… In education, work, buying and selling of land, marriage and the right to move, they enjoyed the same rights and treatment as Han Chinese. They never faced discrimination."[1] This tolerance stood in stark contrast to persecution and forced conversions across the West under Byzantine emperors Heraclius, Justinian II, Leo III and Romanos I.[1]

## Suzerainty over the Turks

In 643 CE the Taizong Emperor of the Tang defeated the western Turkish Khaganate militarily, the eastern branch having already been defeated by Li Jing in 630.[1] With this victory the Chinese Emperor was made "Tengri Khagan," or Heavenly King, supreme authority over all Turks.[1] Some 100,000 Turks migrated into China's expanded realm and 10,000 Turkish elites settled in the capital; letters from Turkish leaders to the Tang court continued to recognize China's emperors as "Heavenly Khagan" as late as 741 CE.[1] It was the most western remnant of the [[gokturk-empire|GokTurk]] realm, newly independent of its eastern parent, that founded [[khazaria|Khazaria]] in the mid-7th century.[1]

## Culture and decline

During the 300 years of Tang rule the arts rose to new heights and the poet-statesman became an actualized ideal, with the greatest poets and painters — Wang Wei, Li Bai and Du Fu — playing major political roles.[1] Torture and death penalties were nearly abolished and public schools were built in record numbers.[1] The dynasty was gravely weakened by the [[an-shi-rebellion|An-Shi Rebellion]] of 755 CE, never recovering its pre-civil-war prestige, and the Silk Road lost vitality just as the Christian-Jewish-Muslim alliance reached its apex.[1] Wars with Muslims, Turks and Tibetans, along with internal struggles, further eroded Tang power.[1]
