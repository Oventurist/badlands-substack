# Royal Academy of Arts

The Royal Academy of Arts — referred to in the Badlands Media essay A New American Renaissance as the English Royal Academy of Fine Arts and, in places, the Royal Society of Fine Arts — was established in 1769 under the patronage of [[king-george-iii|King George III]] as the central institution of British artistic life.

[[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]], a self-taught Quaker born in Pennsylvania, was a founding member at its establishment and became its president in 1791, a position he still held in 1816. His 1795 group painting *The Royal Academicians* depicts him at the center of the institution, surrounded by its artists and artworks; [[john-singleton-copley|John Singleton Copley]] is among the figures present.

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] treats West's occupancy of the Academy's presidency as the essay's central irony: an American republican serving as "a shaper of cultural, moral and aesthetical senses within the very heart of the empire." The Academy also appears as an enforcer of artistic convention — West was warned he would "never get a commission again" if he broke the rule that history paintings use Greco-Roman costume, a threat overridden when George III purchased *The Death of General Wolfe*. Through the Academy, West trained a generation of American painters including [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]], Copley, Charles Willson Peale, Rembrandt Peale, Gilbert Stuart and John Trumbull.
