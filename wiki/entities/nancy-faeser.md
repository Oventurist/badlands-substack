# Nancy Faeser

Nancy Faeser is the German Interior Minister who announced in September 2024 that [[germany|Germany]] would impose tighter controls at all of the country's land borders.[1]

Faeser said the controls — imposed inside what is normally the free-movement area of the European Schengen zone — would begin on September 16 and initially last six months.[1] She stated that the government had also designed a scheme enabling authorities to reject more migrants directly at German borders, without providing details on what the report described as a controversial and legally fraught move.[1]

"We are strengthening internal security and continuing our hard line against irregular migration," Faeser said, noting that the government had notified the European Commission and neighboring countries of the intended controls.[1] The measures formed part of a broader tightening of German policy on irregular migration following a surge in arrivals of people fleeing war and poverty in the Middle East, and were framed as an attempt by Chancellor [[olaf-scholz|Olaf Scholz]]'s government to reclaim political initiative from rising far-right and conservative opposition parties.[1]

## February 2024 extremist financing proposals

On Tuesday, February 13, 2024, Faeser — described as Germany's top security official — said she aimed to make it easier to trace right-wing extremists' financing and planned to set up an "early recognition unit" to detect far-right and foreign disinformation campaigns as early as possible.[2] Her proposals followed large protests against the far right in Germany in preceding weeks, and reflected growing concern after a report that extremists had met to discuss deporting millions of immigrants, including some with German citizenship, with members of the far-right [[alternative-for-germany|Alternative for Germany]] party present; AfD support had doubled since the 2021 election.[2]

Germany's domestic intelligence agency reported that the number of far-right extremists had been rising, reaching 38,800 in 2022 with 14,000 considered potentially violent, and its head, Thomas Haldenwang, said the numbers were believed to have risen again the following year.[2] Faeser said efforts to shut down extremists' financing had been hampered because financial investigations were limited to "inciting and violence-oriented" movements, and suggested the law be changed to make a group's "threat potential" grounds for such investigations, with faster and less bureaucratic proceedings.[2] "No one who donates to a right-wing extremist organization should be able to rely on remaining undiscovered," she said.[2]
