# Tina Peters

Tina Peters is the figure whose sentence commutation served as the lead subject of the eighteenth episode of [[the-blitz|The Blitz]], published 23 May 2026 under the title "Tina Peters Coming Out; Speech Laws Coming In."[1]

## Coverage in the Badlands Brief

On that episode, GhostofBasedPatrickHenry — see [[ghost-of-patrick-henry|Ghost of Patrick Henry]] — and [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] discussed the commutation of Peters' sentence alongside the coming ban on antisemitism and other [[speech-laws|speech laws]].[1] The episode paired her release with a broader discussion of geopolitics and expectations for the golden age, framing the commutation as a marker of shifting momentum in the stories the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands Brief]] tracks Monday through Friday.[1]

The source article does not elaborate on the underlying case, the authority that issued the commutation, or the terms of her release; it records only that the commutation had occurred and that it was treated as significant enough to headline the weekly recap.[1]

## Colorado conviction and nine-year sentence

Peters, a Republican former county clerk in Colorado, was convicted of attempting to influence a public official and, as a non-violent first-time offender, received a nine-year prison sentence.[2] Her prosecution arose from conduct undertaken in the course of investigating concerns about election administration and the voting systems used in her jurisdiction.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that prosecutors in Peters' case used a recipient-based theory of the influence statute and maximized multiplicity by treating each communication as a separate statutory violation, an approach that produced nearly a decade of prison time.[2]

## Sentencing-disparity controversy (March 2026)

The disparity between Peters' sentence and that of former state Senator [[sonya-jaquez-lewis|Sonya Jaquez Lewis]] — convicted of four felonies including the same influence charge but sentenced to probation, a fine, and 150 hours of community service — became a national controversy in early March 2026.[2] On Monday, March 2, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] posted "Free Tina Peters" on Truth Social.[2] The following day, Colorado Governor [[jared-polis|Jared Polis]] signaled on X that he was open to commuting her sentence, citing the need for justice to be "applied evenly" and announcing that he had extended the state's clemency application deadline to April 3.[2]

On Wednesday, March 4, Peters' attorney [[john-case|John Case]] shared a statement with Badlands Media's Ashe Epp saying Peters was grateful to Polis and naming Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]], Attorney General [[phil-weiser|Phil Weiser]], and [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]]-aligned official Matt Crane as political enemies demanding that the governor show no mercy.[2] Asked whether a clemency application had been filed, a member of her team replied only, "There is no missing paperwork" — language Epp suggested might reflect the team's theory that a presidential pardon alone would suffice.[2]

Epp described Peters as the Colorado establishment's "most valuable political scalp" and the last support for the narrative that [[election-denial|election denialism]] endangers democracy, and reported unconfirmed indications that Peters was or would be charged over a prison altercation the previous month.[2]

## Commutation (May 2026)

Colorado Governor [[jared-polis|Jared Polis]] commuted Peters' prison sentence in May 2026, with the Associated Press reporting the decision on 15 May; she was expected to be released after serving about two years.[3] Peters had been convicted in 2024 for allowing unauthorized access to [[mesa-county|Mesa County]] voting systems tied to efforts to challenge the 2020 presidential election results, and prosecutors said the equipment and data she exposed later circulated among election-denial activists.[3] An April 2026 Colorado appeals court decision upheld her convictions but vacated the sentence and ordered resentencing, citing issues with the trial judge's sentencing comments.[3]

Polis said the commutation reflected concerns that Peters, a first-time nonviolent offender, had received an unusually severe sentence, while stating that the decision did not endorse her conduct or her false claims about the 2020 election.[3] The case had become a national rallying point among supporters of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], who repeatedly called for her release and claimed in 2025 that he had pardoned her, despite state officials noting that presidential pardons do not apply to state convictions.[3] Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] and other critics said the commutation risked weakening deterrence against election-system tampering and could damage public trust in election administration.[3]

## Terms of release and personal aftermath

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] reported that on the Friday of that week Polis granted clemency in the form of a reduced sentence and a grant of parole, cutting the sentence in half and moving up Peters' eligibility date, with the conditions of parole to be set by a governor-appointed parole board holding broad statutory discretion.[3] She wrote that she embraced Peters that Saturday, and that Peters' first priority upon release was seeing her 97-year-old mother, followed by reclaiming her health through fresh food and rest; Peters planned to eat a thick steak and a salad with real tomatoes and avocado, and wished to see Minka only once she could be certain she would never leave again.[3]

Peters told Epp that the first night she genuinely slept while incarcerated was the Friday after the grant of clemency came down.[3] Addressing speculation that Peters had been forced or coerced into issuing a statement of contrition, Epp said she asked Peters directly and that Peters approved the statement and stands by it, consistent with their previous conversations.[3] Epp added that much more would be released in the coming weeks and that Peters was ready to tell her story, closing that "June 1 can't come soon enough."[3]

## Weaponization disbursements (2026)
Peters was cited by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] as the archetypal intended beneficiary of the Justice Department [[weaponization-fund|weaponization fund]], whose disbursements leftist politicians including Senator [[michael-bennet|Michael Bennet]] and Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]] were reportedly proposing to tax at 100 percent specifically to deny such recipients any benefit.[4]

## Invoked in the Hannah Dugan sentencing debate (2026)

Peters' sentence was widely cited in July 2026 as a benchmark for asymmetrical justice after former Milwaukee County Circuit Judge [[hannah-dugan|Hannah Dugan]] received only a $5,000 fine and no prison time for a felony conviction for obstructing a federal immigration arrest.[5] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] contrasted the eight years and three months Peters received for what she described as a single misrepresentation made with the earnest intent of fulfilling her duty against Dugan's fine for abdicating her duty and aiding a criminal defendant's evasion of federal enforcement.[5]

## January 2026 appeal and clemency consideration

Division Five of the [[colorado-court-of-appeals|Colorado Court of Appeals]] heard oral arguments on Peters' August 2024 criminal conviction in Denver in January 2026, before a three-judge panel led by Craig R. Welling and flanked by Ted C. Tow III and Lino S. Lipinsky de Orlov.[6] The hearing was reported to have gone unexpectedly well for Peters, with the panel appearing outraged at times by the state's conduct and hostile to both parties, and with the question of jurisdiction dominating discussion even though Peters' own counsel had elected not to argue it.[6]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who has covered the case since Peters' office was raided in August 2021, described herself as shocked by the posture of the court and predicted — expressly as a non-attorney speculating for entertainment — that the conspiracy felony and the official misconduct misdemeanor would be thrown out and the remaining counts resentenced before a different judicial officer, potentially releasing Peters on time served.[6] She characterized that as the minimum required to remedy what she called overt injustice in [[mesa-county|Mesa County]] in August and October of 2024, and argued that the State's refusal to acknowledge its own errors strongly supported the allegation, and an ongoing [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] investigation, that Colorado had weaponized government against Peters — a finding that in her view should produce full vacatur, immediate release, and prosecution of the State and County for prosecutorial misconduct.[6]

In parallel, Governor [[jared-polis|Jared Polis]] reiterated in a January 2026 television interview that he was seriously considering commuting Peters' sentence, citing her status as a first-time nonviolent offender and an intention in his final year to "lean into that value of mercy."[6]

## Appeal oral arguments (January 2026)

Peters' appeal hearing oral arguments were held in mid-January 2026 and were covered by Badlands. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] reported that one of the judges rejected the State's argument that destroying election records was permissible so long as a backup of the records existed somewhere, a ruling she summarized with the principle "all means all."[7] She said there was "a lot there" and promised a detailed analysis of the hearing in the following day's Brief, while discussing it with [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] on The Daily Herold.[7]

## Appeals court decision (April 2026)

On 2 April 2026 a Colorado appellate court upheld Peters' convictions but ordered that she be resentenced, finding that her original sentencing had violated her constitutionally protected speech under the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]]; the sentence was overturned and the matter remanded to the trial court.[8] Peters had been convicted in 2024 of three felony counts of attempting to influence a public official, one felony count of conspiracy to influence a public official, and three misdemeanor counts of professional conduct violations.[8]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the decision vindicated her Christmas 2025 column describing Peters as, to some, a villain and threat to democracy and, to others, a hero, a canary in the coal mine, and a political prisoner of weaponized government and asymmetrical justice — and "a sick, septuagenarian woman spending the holidays in prison because, as she tells it, she believed that her sworn duty mattered more than the personal consequences."[8] She highlighted the appellate panel's finding that, before the Trusted Build, Brown had already made several backups of the election records and, at Peters' behest, Hayes had created a forensic image of the voting equipment server, and that Peters was not charged for any crimes arising out of either act — meaning her statutory duty to retain and preserve election records had no bearing on the illegal conduct actually charged.[8]

Ashe argued that nearly every version of the public narrative, from every faction, assumes the opposite of that finding and casts the case as election meddling, when in fact Peters did not meddle in an election; she likened the dynamic to the "oathbreaking insurrectionist" framing of 2023–2024 and noted the same actors were involved.[8] She and [[canncon|CannCon]] had maintained since covering the trial that the case — the facts, the evidence, and the law — was never about elections but about storytelling.[8] While describing parts of the decision as worth celebrating and others as puzzling and frustrating, she published a fuller breakdown on her own Substack and said the ruling would be discussed on [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]].[8]

## Presidential pardon (December 2025)

On Thursday, 11 December 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced on Truth Social that he was granting Peters "a full Pardon for her attempts to expose Voter Fraud in the Rigged 2020 Presidential Election."[9] In the accompanying statement Trump accused Democrats of ignoring violent crime while relentlessly targeting Peters, "a Patriot who simply wanted to make sure that our Elections were Fair and Honest," and said she was "sitting in a Colorado prison for the 'crime' of demanding Honest Elections."[9]

Colorado officials rejected the pardon's validity. Governor [[jared-polis|Jared Polis]], Attorney General [[phil-weiser|Phil Weiser]], Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] and the Colorado Democratic Party responded with variations on a single statement: that Peters was convicted by a jury of her peers, prosecuted by a Republican district attorney, and found guilty of violating Colorado state laws including criminal impersonation, and that no president has jurisdiction over state law or the power to pardon state convictions.[9]

Peters' counsel [[peter-ticktin|Peter Ticktin]] had earlier raised the constitutional question of whether the federal pardon power could reach a state conviction, an argument reported by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] as resting on Reconstruction-era ideology and requiring the federal government to claim more power than it conventionally holds.[9] Ashe observed that a Supreme Court ruling on the question might move American law toward originalism while simultaneously foreclosing Peters' own path to release, and Ticktin discussed the post-pardon procedure with [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] the same week.[9]

A parallel controversy concerned a heavily redacted 12 November transfer order for Peters released by the [[colorado-department-of-corrections|Colorado Department of Corrections]]; the redacted paragraph was the one citing the federal government's asserted authority for the transfer request, a fact confirmed by Kyle Clark of Colorado's 9News.[9] [[rasmussen-reports|Rasmussen]] publicly asked what federal officials had told Colorado officials about Colorado elections that "suddenly now has to be kept secret," and Ashe filed open-records requests for the cited authority, reasoning that the decision to redact it suggested the federal claim had merit.[9] She judged this effort separate from the pardon and whistleblower tracks, though noted the pardon might connect to a viable release path depending on the language of the relevant intergovernmental agreement.[9]

The same day, the [[justice-department|Justice Department]] sued Griswold to force production of detailed voter information without redactions, following its announcement earlier that week of an investigation into complaints about conditions in the Colorado correctional system.[9] Badlands framed the escalating conflict as a "Rocky Mountain Showdown" that might both open a lawful route to move Peters out of Colorado and strengthen states' rights protections under the Tenth Amendment through challenges to the pardon power.[9]

## October 2025 federal habeas hearing

Peters returned to court in October 2025 for arguments on several pending motions before U.S. Magistrate Judge [[scott-varholak|Scott Varholak]], who is considering her [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] claims on the habeas corpus motion she filed earlier that year.[10] Peters argues that her detention is unconstitutional because she was denied bond pending appeal, and her federal petition cites the trial court's inflammatory statements about her incarceration, including Judge [[matthew-barrett|Matthew Barrett]]'s remark that "Prison is for those folks where we send people who are a danger to all of us, whether it be by the pen or the sword or the word of the mouth."[10] The question before the federal court is whether Barrett jailing Peters over what she might say violates her First Amendment rights, while claims of bias and over-sentencing remain in the Colorado courts.[10]

During the short hearing, Varholak ruled immediately on several motions: he granted admission of an amicus brief from Peters' supporters, deeming it novel but useful; granted motions to strike foreign whistleblower testimony about U.S. election systems as irrelevant; and denied Peters' motion to take notice of the [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]] sale, also as irrelevant.[10] The stricken testimony would have supported Peters' argument that what she might say is true, strengthening her First Amendment-based habeas claim.[10] Peters remained incarcerated, though the judge indicated an expedited ruling on the full petition, possibly by early November.[10]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who with @canncon had performed a dramatic reading of the Venezuelan whistleblower's deposition transcript on two episodes of *Why We Vote*, said she could see the ruling both ways.[10] The government argues the case concerns the actions Peters took in securing forensic images of the machines and her misrepresentation of credentials to multiple government employees, making the validation of her intent by what she found irrelevant — a framing Ashe rejected, noting that if Peters is correct, the people persecuting her are likely guilty of treason.[10] She argued the reviewing court has broad discretion to remedy manifest injustice but that Varholak, having ruled on the expanded claims in July, would not revisit them.[10] Conversely, she contended that because the petition was narrowed to the First Amendment claim, the substance of the "danger" of Peters' speech should be in play: the whistleblower has firsthand knowledge of the inception of the source code and of how it confirms the Mesa County images, whose forensic reports Peters commissioned and which documented wireless connections, duplicate databases, and deleted logfiles.[10] Noting that two of Barrett's three list items were forms of speech, she asked whether Colorado elections are so bad that telling the truth about them is considered incitement to violence, closing with "Free Tina Peters."[10]

## Trump's demands for her release (August 2025)

On Thursday, August 21, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] renewed his call for Colorado to free Peters, a state election official convicted of multiple felonies after breaching voting equipment in the 2020 election, warning he would take "harsh measures" if she were not released.[11] On Truth Social he called her "a brave and innocent Patriot who has been tortured by Crooked Colorado politicians, including the big Mail-In Ballot supporting the governor of the State," said she "did nothing wrong, except catching the Democrats cheat in the Election," described her as an old and very sick woman, and demanded her immediate release.[11] He did not specify the measures, though he had previously threatened to withhold funding from states that do not align with his policies.[11]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that federal funding is the primary lever available to Trump, judging novel theories such as declaring Peters a federal witness a long shot.[11] She noted that funding cuts were already existential for Colorado — by some reports as much as 40% of the state's budget comes from federal subsidies — and that Trump's post landed just as the Colorado Legislature opened a special session to address a $1.2 billion deficit caused by federal cuts, calling the timing "perfect" and "the art of the deal in action."[11] She argued that the truth about what Peters found, and what was done to her because of it, "will bring down the whole corrupt temple in Colorado and the nation," and connected the case to the secret March 2025 installation of former [[perkins-coie|Perkins Coie]] attorney [[andrew-kline|Andrew Kline]] as deputy running Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]]'s office — the office that tried to remove Trump from the ballot and that was the allegedly injured party in Peters's prosecution.[11]

## Contrast with the Durham findings

In June 2023, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] cited Peters's conviction on what she called "a silly charge of 'obstruction of government'" involving an iPad as an example of the weaponization of law enforcement, recalling that obstruction of government "felt like a made-up crime."[12] She argued that while citizens might claim a constitutional right to obstruct unconstitutional government from covering up stolen elections, there is no constitutional protection for the bureaucratic state to obstruct a duly elected president from governing — conduct closer to treason, which is not a made-up crime, and which she said the [[durham-report|Durham report]] proves occurred during [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s first term.[12]

## Trial timing in the 2024 lawfare cycle

Writing at the start of January 2024, Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] listed Peters' upcoming trial — then scheduled for early February 2024 — alongside the [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]] appeal and a separate trial challenging the lawful First Amendment activities of the Colorado grassroots scheduled the same week, as evidence that [[colorado|Colorado]] would "remain a battlefield in the election lawfare domain."[13]

## Denied media visit (December 2025)

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] reported in December 2025 that the head of "diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging" at the [[colorado-department-of-corrections|Colorado Department of Corrections]] had denied her request to see Peters, and doubled down after she pushed back on how she was being treated as a member of the media.[14] She cited the episode as evidence that [[diversity-equity-and-inclusion|DEI]] remained "alive and well in Colorado" and was mutating into DEIB despite Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]]'s declaration that the practice had been relegated to the dustbin of history.[14]

## Justice Department review of the conviction (March 2025)

On Monday, March 3, 2025, the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] said it would review the conviction of Peters, the former clerk of Mesa County, Colorado, who had been found guilty of state charges the previous summer of tampering with voting machines under her control in a failed attempt to prove they had been used to rig the 2020 election against President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[15] The New York Times characterized the decision as the latest example of the Justice Department under Trump's control seeking to use its powers to support those who acted on his behalf and to go after those who criticized or opposed him, and as part of the president's effort to rewrite the history of his attempts to overturn the election results.[15]

Three weeks earlier, Peters — who had been sentenced to nine years in prison on the state election tampering charges — filed a long-shot motion in Federal District Court in Denver effectively challenging the guilty verdict she received in August at the end of a trial in Grand Junction.[15] In a surprise move, [[yaakov-m-roth|Yaakov M. Roth]], the acting assistant attorney general for the Justice Department's civil division, filed a statement of interest declaring that "reasonable concerns have been raised about various aspects of Ms. Peters's case."[15] The brief cited the Attorney General Memorandum "Restoring the Integrity and Credibility of The Department of Justice" (February 5, 2025) and Executive Order 14147, "Ending the Weaponization of The Federal Government" (January 20, 2025), and said the review would evaluate whether Colorado's prosecution was "oriented more toward inflicting political pain than toward pursuing actual justice or legitimate governmental objectives."[15]

Badlands contributor Ashe in America called the statement of interest "absolute fire" and argued that Peters's detention was cruel and unusual and that she should be released on bond immediately pending appeal, while cautioning that the matter was unlikely to progress quickly because Peters is "quite the trophy" for what the author called the Colorado-based regime.[15] Article III Project founder [[mike-davis|Mike Davis]] summarized the saga on X, writing that Peters investigated voter fraud after the 2021 Grand Junction municipal election and that Colorado Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]], Attorney General [[phil-weiser|Phil Weiser]], and local District Attorney Dan Rubenstein brought what he called trumped-up felony charges accusing her falsely of unauthorized access to voting machines, while Griswold allowed voting-machine passwords to remain publicly posted online for four months.[15] Davis added that Judge [[matthew-barrett|Matthew Barrett]] stated on the record that he sentenced the 69-year-old Peters to nine years in prison because of her First Amendment-protected views on election integrity.[15]

## DOJ push for transfer to federal custody, November 2025

In November 2025 the [[justice-department|Justice Department]] asked the [[federal-bureau-of-prisons|Bureau of Prisons]] to examine ways to move Peters out of Colorado state prison and into a federal facility, following her conviction and after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s public calls for her release.[16] The Office of the Deputy Attorney General, led by [[todd-blanche|Todd Blanche]] — second-in-command to Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] — sent an email on Wednesday evening, November 5, 2025, to Bureau of Prisons Director William Marshall requesting action.[16] The email stated: "At the request of the Deputy Attorney General, please have the Bureau of Prisons explore any and all avenues within BOP's authority to seek and request the transfer of Ms. Tina Peters from the [[colorado-department-of-corrections|Colorado Department of Corrections]] to a federal BOP facility," and asked that BOP send a request to Colorado as soon as possible and begin preparations for a possible transfer.[16] The correspondence was first reported by [[just-the-news|Just the News]].[16]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] assessed that Peters's strongest route out of state custody is the [[weaponization-of-government|weaponization of government]] theory, arguing that what is happening to her is cruel and unusual and is driven by her politics rather than her conduct.[16] She noted that Blanche was not advancing a specific legal argument but exploring available avenues, and specifically was not characterizing Peters as a federal election whistleblower — not because she is not one, in her view, but because that argument is legally weak.[16] She also observed that the forum in which the Department announced its investigation into weaponization in Colorado was Peters's own appeal, closing with the appeal "Free Tina Peters."[16]

## DOJ weaponization investigation (2025)

In May 2025, Badlands writer Ashe in America argued that the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] investigation into the weaponization of government against Peters, if conducted properly, would prove high crimes and misdemeanors in the Colorado executive branch beyond a reasonable doubt.[17] She framed Peters' prosecution in Colorado as directly tied to what she described as an interstate and ultimately global conspiracy to defraud the American people by rigging elections, and connected it to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s promise to restore election integrity.[17]

## Trump's December 2025 clemency demand and Badlands commentary

President Donald Trump publicly demanded Peters' release in a December 2025 Truth Social post attacking Colorado Governor Jared Polis, saying she had served more than a year in jail with eight years to go, was a cancer survivor, and had been preserving election records she was obligated under federal law to protect.[18] Badlands host Ashe in America endorsed Trump's reading of the participants' intent but added precision: Peters was convicted of misrepresenting to the people she was investigating who was in her office, a response to arbitrary COVID-era rules issued by Secretary of State Jena Griswold about who could be present in county offices, and none of her charges or convictions were for cheating or meddling in elections.[18] Ashe argued the law was changed after the fact to criminalize what Peters did, called her ongoing treatment 'cruel, unusual, and unconstitutional,' and announced a marathon airing of a three-part series on the Peters story on her Rumble channel alongside coverage of the Peters-commissioned Mesa County reports with Colonel Shawn Smith on Why We Vote.[18]

## December 2025: prison safety claims and federal bond denial

In December 2025, Peters' attorney [[peter-ticktin|Peter Ticktin]] told President Trump and his pardon counsel in a letter that Peters, aged 70, was not safe in Colorado state prison, alleging that inmates had threatened to stab and kill her six months earlier and that she had been attacked by other inmates after transferring units, with guards needing to pull them off of her.[19] Ticktin said a request to move her to a safer unit had been denied six times; the letter offered no supporting evidence and the state corrections department could not immediately verify the claims.[19]

On Monday, December 8, 2025, a federal judge declined to release Peters, who remained the only Trump ally imprisoned for crimes related to efforts to overturn the 2020 election.[19] A state jury had convicted the former Republican clerk of Mesa County of participating in a criminal scheme with fellow election deniers to breach her county's secure voting systems, and she was one year into a nine-year sentence.[19] Trump has championed her case, calling her an "innocent political prisoner," and was reportedly being urged to intervene more aggressively.[19]

Her federal petition for bond was dismissed without prejudice under the [[younger-abstention-doctrine|Younger abstention doctrine]], the court holding that it could not answer whether the trial court had improperly punished her more severely for protected First Amendment speech while that question remained pending in Colorado courts.[19] Judge Varholak acknowledged the importance of the constitutional claims but did not reach their merits, and noted that Peters' counsel had never argued for an exception to Younger despite months of briefing.[19] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] and [[blue-eyes|Blue Eyes]] reviewed the full order on the program Culture of Change, arguing that Peters should be released because her treatment was the product of weaponized government and asymmetrical justice.[19]

## December 2025 DOJ investigation of Colorado prisons

On December 8, 2025 the Justice Department announced an investigation into 33 facilities run by the [[colorado-department-of-corrections|Colorado Department of Corrections]] and the state's Department of Youth Services.[20] *Westword* reported that the probe appeared politically motivated rather than a response to years of complaints by [[disability-law-colorado|Disability Law Colorado]], framing it as an extension of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s continued efforts to free Peters, who is serving a nine-year sentence for an election-tampering scheme aimed at proving discredited claims that Trump rather than [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] won the 2020 election.[20] Trump offered what the outlet called an illegitimate pardon the previous week, noting he has no power to free her because she was convicted on state rather than federal charges.[20] Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]], who announced the probe, reshared several X posts celebrating it as "how we FREE TINA PETERS."[20]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] said sources close to the matter reported staff at the La Vista Correctional Facility, where Peters is held, hurriedly cleaning the place up, and described degraded air handlers at the CDOC mental health hospital in the same Pueblo government complex.[20] She also announced she was set up to interview Peters and invited reader questions, cautioning that everyone monitoring the case was likely listening.[20]

## Colorado lawfare context (December 2023)

Writing shortly after the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] disqualified [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the state ballot, Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] cited Peters' imminent return to court as evidence that the insurrection finding was intended as a predicate for further prosecutions, imagining the argument "Trump's insurrection led to all these other crimes in CO!"[21] In her assessment the Colorado litigation had never really been about ballot access but about stage setting for continued criminal action arising out of January 6.[21]

## Space Command relocation framed as "harsh measures" (2025)

Badlands host [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] linked President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s September 2025 decision to move [[united-states-space-command|U.S. Space Command]] out of [[colorado|Colorado]] to Peters' continued imprisonment, noting that Trump cannot pardon her because her conviction is a state-level crime, leaving funding decisions and investigations as the constitutional "harsh measures" available to him.[22] She characterized stripping Colorado of a command that supports roughly 1,700 direct jobs and about $1 billion a year in Colorado Springs economic activity as exactly such a measure.[22] Ashe also connected Trump's stated rationale — Colorado's all-mail voting producing "automatically crooked elections" — to [[mesa-county|Mesa County]], where mail-in ballot irregularities were alleged and where Peters' "crucible began."[22]

## Twitter Spaces appearance (2023)

Peters appeared with [[mike-lindell|Mike Lindell]] on the first [[twitter-spaces|Twitter Spaces]] event hosted by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who had covered her case extensively.[23] The discussion addressed the RNC chair election, the Maricopa audit, the [[mesa-county|Mesa County]] election reports, and Peters' then-upcoming trials.[23]
