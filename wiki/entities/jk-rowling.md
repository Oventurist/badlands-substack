# J.K. Rowling

J.K. Rowling is a British author who, by 2024, had become one of the most prominent public figures opposing the legal and cultural entrenchment of transgender self-identification in the United Kingdom.[1] Her position placed her at direct risk of prosecution when Scotland's new hate crime legislation took effect.[1]

## Exposure under the Scottish hate crime law (April 2024)

On April 1, 2024, the day the [[hate-crime-and-public-order-scotland-act-2021|Hate Crime and Public Order (Scotland) Act 2021]] came into force, an [[scottish-national-party|SNP]] minister admitted that Rowling could be prosecuted for "misgendering" transgender people under the new statute.[1] The Act created an offence of "stirring up hatred" in relation to age, disability, religion, sexual orientation, transgender identity or being intersex, carrying a maximum sentence of up to seven years' imprisonment for "insulting" behaviour toward protected groups, with prosecutors required to show only that hatred was "likely" rather than intended.[1]

[[siobhian-brown|Siobhian Brown]], the SNP's community safety minister, had initially said that calling a "trans woman" "he" would not be a crime, but after the law commenced she stated that such conduct "could be reported and it could be investigated," leaving the question of criminality to [[police-scotland|Police Scotland]].[1]

Rowling vowed to continue calling biological males men and said she expected to be targeted for telling the truth.[1] The episode was reported in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] of April 2, 2024, alongside notes that senior police officers anticipated a deluge of complaints over online posts and that officers had been trained to treat even the content of plays and comedy gigs as potential hate crimes.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] framed the Rowling case within a transnational pattern, observing that the Scottish measures were being implemented "in lockstep" with [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]]'s Online Harms Bill in [[canada|Canada]], evidence in his reading of an "apolitical, globalist enemy attacking the very concept of the sovereign nation state."[1] Esler argued that such laws are more symbolic than practical, citing Ontario statutes in force since roughly 2017 treating misgendering as a hate crime that had not produced mass arrests, and concluded that their principal function was psychological rather than as functional lawfare — while still insisting they be "fought against vehemently."[1]
