# Technocracy Inc.

Technocracy Inc. was a North American movement advocating rule by technical experts rather than elected politicians. Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] invokes it as the ancestor of what he sees as a present-day danger to the American political revival.[1]

## The Haldeman connection

Ehret notes that Technocracy Inc. was once led by Joshua Haldeman, the grandfather of [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]].[1] He raises the possibility of a "dystopic revival" of the movement in the form of a perverted version of [[agenda-47|Trump's Agenda 47]], and warns against ignoring the dangers of a transhumanist Silicon Valley billionaire cult infiltrating MAGA — a warning he elsewhere directs at both Musk and [[peter-thiel|Peter Thiel]].[1]

## Place in the argument

The Technocracy theme functions as a counterweight in the article's otherwise favourable assessment of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s second term.[1] Ehret credits Trump with resisting new wars, halting the post-9/11 regime-change program, seeking cooperation with Russia, and reviving pro-tariff policy against decades of [[world-trade-organization|WTO]] and [[city-of-london|City of London]] free-trade orthodoxy — while insisting that the technocratic infiltration of that movement is a real and separate hazard.[1]

## The 1933 North American Technate

In 1933 Technocracy Inc. proposed a North American "Technate," to be ruled by engineers rather than elected officials.[2] Its vision included replacing money with "energy credits," eliminating elections, and monitoring every human action through a centrally-managed data regime.[2] The Canadian head of the movement was Joshua Haldeman, who ran Technocracy Inc. in Canada from 1936 to 1941.[2]

Prior to that, Haldeman led the [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]]'s Cooperative Commonwealth Federation of Saskatchewan from 1933 to 1936, with the goal of imposing "scientific management of society" onto the world; the Canadian branch of London's Fabian Society had itself been established by six Rhodes Scholars in 1931, one of whom, [[escott-reid|Escott Reid]], went on to spearhead the creation of [[nato|NATO]].[2]

Badlands contributor Matthew Ehret argues that the dream of a "North American Technate," long dismissed as fringe, is being re-branded for the 21st century — tied to [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s "X" project to replace traditional banking with a behavior-linked global digital currency platform, his promotion of [[universal-basic-income|Universal Basic Income]], and his pushing of biometric data and digital IDs.[2]

## Founding and doctrine

Technocracy Inc. was formed in New York in 1932 under the leadership of [[howard-scott|Howard Scott]], whom Ehret describes as a snake oil salesman who lied about being an engineer his entire adult life.[3] The organization promised to reorganize both human society and human nature itself around a top-down "Technate of America" run by experts untainted by democratic institutions or capitalist profit motives.[3] Under this world government, sub-technates would outlaw prices, markets and comparable practices, replacing them with "energy credits" assigned equally to every individual.[3] Scott had built the organization out of the [[technical-alliance|Technical Alliance]], the body created by [[thorstein-veblen|Thorstein Veblen]] around the [[new-school-for-social-research|New School for Social Research]], which he had joined in 1919.[3]

Though it publicly advocated pacifism and non-participation in foreign wars, Ehret argues the appeal to peace was disingenuous, since the borders of the promised Technate extended far beyond the fifty American states to encompass all of Mexico, Canada, Greenland, Central America and much of South America.[3] Members were given numbers to use in official meetings instead of their birth names, often infusing the letter "x" into the sequence: [[joshua-haldeman|Joshua Haldeman]] was known as "10450-1," and one speaker at a California rally introduced himself as "1x1809x561."[3]

Scott and his colleagues believed automation, computers and robots would soon render most jobs worthless, leaving the majority of humanity neither working nor participating in government, privileges reserved for initiated technicians.[3] CBC News described the movement's position in the period: "While other political parties and protest groups were touting plans for putting people back to work, Technocracy's response was: don't even bother. The world has changed and the jobs destroyed by machines were not coming back."[3] Scott himself declared that "one of the lowest social diseases is the belief in the morality of work."[3] The proposed remedy was the energy credit — an early form of [[universal-basic-income|universal basic income]] — quantified through the Energy Survey of North America, which sought to measure all energy used in continental production so that the total could be divided among citizens over the age of 25.[3] No member could accumulate more credits than another, invest, start a business for profit or save; in return, adherents were promised a 16-hour work week and retirement at 45.[3]

## The 1934 Technocracy Study Course

The organization published a manifesto in 1934, the Technocracy Study Course, principally authored by the geologist [[marion-king-hubbert|Marion King Hubbert]].[3] It banned moral considerations from social organization and, following Thomas Huxley's Darwinism and B. F. Skinner's behaviourism, denied the existence of mind, conscience or will, defining the human being as an engine converting chemical potential energy into heat, work and body tissue in accordance with the laws of thermodynamics.[3] Ehret argues that the movement's rhetoric of abundance masked an absolute submission to entropy and universal decay, and reads the choice of the yin-yang monad as its emblem as representing a belief in static Manichean balance.[3] [[harold-loeb|Harold Loeb]] supplied the movement's eugenics dimension, writing in *Life in a Technocracy* that breeding "with specific individuals for specific purposes" should "in time produce a race of men superior in quality to any now known on earth."[3]

## Suppression, rebranding and ban

In January 1933 leading members left the organization over its dictatorial proclivities, and pressure on [[columbia-university|Columbia University]] president [[nicholas-murray-butler|Nicholas Murray Butler]] led to the expulsion of Scott's group on 18 January 1933.[3] The same month Butler announced a special task force on technological progress that included Edmund Day of the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]], the Fabian socialist [[walter-lippmann|Walter Lippmann]], Alvin Johnson of the New School and Benjamin Anderson of Chase National Bank — an inquiry into the price-based economy that the researcher Carl Teichrib, quoted by Ehret, characterizes as technocracy under another name.[3]

Membership nonetheless swelled past a million, with the movement adopting grey suits, navy ties and cars painted in a General Motors hue called "Technocracy Gray," earning it the nickname "The Grey Shirts."[3] Ehret notes that William Knight, the director of operations and an aeronautical engineer employed by a German subsidiary, selected the uniform out of admiration for the paramilitary look of the German SS.[3] Leaders publicly rejected both communism and fascism as "too populist," though Scott boasted that "we're so far left that we make communism look bourgeois."[3] In 1941 the organization was outlawed in Canada as a pro-fascist threat and Haldeman was arrested; Prime Minister [[william-lyon-mackenzie-king|William Lyon Mackenzie King]] said its goal was to overthrow the government and constitution by force.[3]
