# India

India emerged in March 2026 as a central actor in the reordering of global energy trade and currency settlement triggered by the Iran crisis.[1]

## Russian crude purchases and non-dollar settlement

Indian refiners booked approximately 60 million barrels of [[russia|Russian]] crude for the upcoming month, a volume more than double February levels.[1] The increase followed supply-disruption concerns tied to the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], through which roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption transits.[1]

Indian refiners increasingly settled some Russian oil trades in non-dollar currencies, including UAE dirhams and Chinese yuan, with workarounds that convert Indian rupees into dirhams or yuan to facilitate transactions with Russian suppliers.[1] Market analysts said the crisis could accelerate a broader shift away from dollar-denominated oil trade toward alternative settlement systems.[1]

## Russian oil and the 2026 sanctions waiver

During the March 2026 energy crisis the United States issued a temporary waiver of certain oil-related sanctions allowing India to purchase Russian oil cargoes, a measure Washington framed as preventing shortages and easing pressure on global markets.[2] Indian officials responded that the waiver did not determine national energy policy, stating that India "has never depended on permission from any country to buy Russian oil" and would continue purchasing crude wherever available to meet domestic demand.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read this exchange as uncharacteristic sovereign defiance and grouped India with [[russia|Russia]] and [[china|China]] as a "Sovereign Trinity" — with [[iran|Iran]] as economic facilitator — sparring at the narrative level while cooperating in practice to reroute supplies and insulate their populations from the shocks radiating out of the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]].[2] India was identified as Russia's largest oil buyer.[2]

## 2026 tariff agreement and energy realignment

On February 2, 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] confirmed that the United States had cut its reciprocal tariff on Indian imports from 25% to 18%, effective immediately, after a phone call with Prime Minister [[narendra-modi|Narendra Modi]]; the move also eliminated an additional 25% punitive tariff imposed in 2025 over India's purchases of Russian crude oil, reducing the effective burden from as high as roughly 50%.[3] As part of the agreement India committed to halting purchases of Russian crude and shifting imports toward US suppliers and Venezuelan crude, and agreed to reduce tariffs and non-tariff barriers on US products with the stated goal of approaching zero.[3] Trump separately said India was "coming in" to buy Venezuelan oil and welcomed Indian and Chinese investment in [[venezuela|Venezuela]]'s oil sector.[3] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized India as a "bridge state" and "sovereign-coded relay" within the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] framework rather than a capitulating party; see [[2026-us-india-tariff-agreement|the 2026 US-India tariff agreement]].[3]

## Summoning of Iran's ambassador (July 2026)

India summoned [[iran|Iran]]'s ambassador in July 2026 amid the disruption of traffic through the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], which fell to six vessels on one day — the lowest in five weeks — raising concerns for freedom of navigation, energy supplies and seafarer safety.[4] The [[united-arab-emirates|UAE]] separately reserved the right to respond, and analysts warned of prolonged disruption if tit-for-tat exchanges continued.[4]

## The March 2026 Russian oil waiver

In early March 2026 the United States issued a temporary 30-day waiver allowing Indian refiners to purchase [[russia|Russian]] oil already at sea, as global energy markets faced disruption from the conflict in the Middle East and shipping risks around the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]].[5] U.S. officials said the measure was intended to maintain oil supply to global markets and prevent further price spikes during the crisis.[5]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the waiver as a deliberate "Sovereign Signal": with tanker traffic collapsed, Washington carved out a window for stranded Russian crude so that India — "another central pillar of the emerging [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]]" — received breathing room while Russia secured an outlet for its supply.[5] He contrasted this with Europe, to which no equivalent courtesy was extended even as [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] weighed redirecting Russian gas away from the continent entirely.[5]

## Apple manufacturing dispute and outsourcing debate (2025)

In May 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he had told [[apple|Apple]] chief executive [[tim-cook|Tim Cook]] that he objected to the company's plans to manufacture iPhones in India, telling him: "You can build in India if you want to take care of India," but "we're not interested in you building in India. India can take care of themselves."[6]

Badlands commentary agreed with the premise that India is no longer an emerging economy, citing a 2023 CATO Institute report noting the country's 1.4 billion people, roughly 9.7% share of global GDP and 49.4% share of global trade in GDP.[6] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that India's prosperity had come at American expense as jobs were moved abroad over two decades — IT outsourcing giving way to business process outsourcing and then creative and consulting outsourcing — under the claim that access to lower-cost foreign talent pools was a "competitive imperative," following an earlier wave in which manufacturing jobs left and trades ceased being taught in American high schools.[6]
