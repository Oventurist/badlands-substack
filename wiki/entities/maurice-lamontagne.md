# Maurice Lamontagne

Maurice Lamontagne was a Canadian senator, former President of the Privy Council, founder of Environment Canada and a member of the [[club-of-rome|Club of Rome]].[1] [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes him as a long-time London-trained asset and close collaborator of Canadian Prime Minister [[pierre-trudeau|Pierre Elliott Trudeau]], and as the Club of Rome member most candid in identifying the earth's greatest enemy as human creativity itself.[1]

## Senate Committee Reports, 1968–1972

Lamontagne's Senate Committee Reports of 1968–1972 reformed Canadian science policy funding and planning.[1] In them he wrote that nature imposes definite constraints on technology, that ignoring them would reduce rather than increase nature's potential as a provider of resources and habitable space, and then posed the question: "How can we stop man's creativeness?"[1] Recognizing that the yearning to discover the unknown is built into the human condition, he answered himself: it is impossible to destroy existing knowledge or to paralyze man's inborn desire to learn, invent and innovate, since technology is merely a tool created by man in pursuit of his infinite aspirations — "It is material growth itself that is the source of conflict between man and nature."[1]

Ehret reads this as establishing that creativity and technological progress are acceptable only if they reduce the assumed conflict between man and nature: "bad" technology increases humanity's powers of productivity, while low energy flux density technologies such as windmills, solar panels and biofuels, which reduce man's powers to exist, are redefined as "good."[1]

## Redirection of science funding

In preparation for the post-industrial order unleashed by the 1971 floating of the US dollar and the destruction of the [[bretton-woods-system|Bretton Woods]] monetary system, Lamontagne prescribed that the "new wisdom" should no longer aim at discoveries in atomic, medical and space sciences, but focus on more "practical" engineering endeavours.[1] He also proposed diminishing funding to advanced science by widening the definition of "science" itself to embrace the humanities, monetary economics and the social sciences — programs which then began absorbing funds formerly directed to pure research.[1] Volume one of his Report stated that additional R&D effort should be devoted to the life sciences and social sciences rather than the physical sciences, and to economic and social objectives "rather than curiosity and discovery."[1]

## Collaborators and critics

Lamontagne collaborated on the Senate Report with fellow Club of Rome member [[omond-solandt|Omond Solandt]], who declared that "there is no longer any need to advance science."[1] He was attacked by Dalhousie environmental science professor [[ronald-hayes|Ronald Hayes]], who in his 1973 book *The Chaining of Prometheus* cast Lamontagne as a minion of Zeus and accused him of seeking to destroy the National Research Council, the body that had nurtured much Canadian government research and launched university graduate programs — a fault Hayes ascribed to the Trudeau administration which Lamontagne echoed.[1]

Lamontagne also belonged to the network of Rhodes Scholars and Privy Councillors — centred around [[alexander-king|Alexander King]], [[maurice-strong|Maurice Strong]], Michael Pitfield and Governor General Roland Michener — that presided over the 1971 Montebello, Quebec meeting at which Trudeau allocated taxpayer money to begin the study that became [[limits-to-growth|*Limits to Growth*]].[1]
