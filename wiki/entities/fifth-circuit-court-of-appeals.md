# US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit

The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit is the federal appellate court whose rulings on immigration detention became a focal point of the second [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration's removal agenda in early 2026.[1]

## Buenrostro-Mendez v. Bondi (2026)

On Friday, February 6, 2026, the Fifth Circuit reversed two district-court orders that had required bond hearings or release for detained noncitizens, holding that the government may detain certain "applicants for admission" without bond during removal proceedings under 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b)(2)(A).[1] The ruling came in *Buenrostro-Mendez v. Bondi*, consolidated with *Padron Covarrubias v. Vergara*.[1]

Following the decision, Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] said [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] attorneys had secured a "crucial legal victory" supporting the administration's immigration agenda.[1] [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]] Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]] noted that courts had previously ordered releases based on claims that DHS was breaking the law, and characterized the ruling as vindicating the department's position.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the ruling as a restoration of basic sovereignty, arguing that a state cannot exercise supreme authority over its territory without enforceable borders, and that consistent consequences for broken law are the substance of the "rule of law."[1] In that reading, the post-Biden migration wave made "every state a border state," giving the federal government a duty — not merely a right — to remove those in the country unlawfully, and the Fifth Circuit's holding ensures detainees "remain detained until their one-way flight to their final destination."[1]

## Dominance of the 2023 Supreme Court docket

As the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] opened its October 2023 term, Bloomberg reported that an outsize share of the term's biggest cases originated in the Fifth Circuit, whose far-reaching rulings had become impossible for the high court to ignore.[2] The pending and likely cases spanned federal regulatory power, firearms, abortion, and social media regulation, and were seen as a test of how far the Supreme Court's conservative majority would go in remaking the legal landscape.[2]

Among the Fifth Circuit decisions already accepted for review were a ruling declaring the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's funding system unconstitutional, one rejecting the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]]'s use of in-house administrative judges, and a third holding that people subject to domestic violence restraining orders retain a constitutional right to carry a firearm.[2] The justices were also expected to consider circuit rulings permitting Texas to regulate social media content moderation, limiting access to the abortion pill mifepristone, and striking down the federal ban on bump stocks — devices that convert semiautomatic rifles into machine guns.[2]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the docket as an underappreciated win, arguing that amid the noise of a looming government shutdown and the [[biden-impeachment-inquiry|Biden impeachment inquiry]], the Court would be weighing a slate of cases from "the most Conservative, pro-Trump circuit in the nation," touching Second Amendment protections, abortion enforcement powers, and anti-censorship directives against social media conglomerates.[2]

## Bypassed in the April 2025 Alien Enemies Act litigation

In April 2025, the Fifth Circuit was at the center of a procedural controversy when the [[american-civil-liberties-union|ACLU]], seeking to halt removals of Venezuelan detainees under the [[alien-enemies-act|Alien Enemies Act]], went to the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] on an emergency basis.[3] Badlands commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the ACLU "essentially skipped the Fifth Circuit by giving the court an ultimatum to rule within 42 minutes," a demand she said parties cannot ordinarily make, and criticized the Supreme Court for rewarding it with unprecedented relief.[3]

A contemporaneous Reason essay on [[process-formalism|process formalism]] contrasted this with the circuit's earlier conduct, noting that during the Biden administration the Fifth Circuit had moved promptly and decided cases on its emergency docket precisely so that timely appeals to the Supreme Court remained possible.[3]

## Appeal in Missouri v. Biden (July 2023)

On Wednesday, July 5, 2023, the U.S. Justice Department filed a notice of appeal with the Fifth Circuit in New Orleans challenging the injunction issued in [[missouri-v-biden|Missouri v. Biden]], which would block federal agencies and officials from communicating with social media companies about content on their platforms.[4] The underlying 155-page opinion by U.S. District Judge [[terry-doughty|Terry Doughty]] held that federal officials likely violated the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] in their contacts with Twitter, Meta, and other technology firms regarding the pandemic, the 2020 election, and other political subjects.[4]
