# Transportation Security Administration

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) is the United States federal agency responsible for screening passengers and baggage at commercial airports, a mission it assumed as part of the security architecture built after the September 11, 2001 attacks.[1]

## Shoe removal policy

The TSA introduced its shoe removal rule in 2006, following the 2001 attempted bombing by Richard Reid, who tried to ignite explosives hidden in his shoes during a trans-Atlantic American Airlines flight. The incident led the agency to focus heightened attention on screening footwear for concealed threats, and the requirement quickly became a staple of post-9/11 airport security procedures across the United States.[1]

The policy was relaxed incrementally rather than all at once. In 2011, the TSA officially updated its rules to exempt children aged 12 and under, allowing them to leave their shoes on during screening unless an alarm triggered additional screening. In 2012, passengers aged 75 and older were granted the same conditional exemption.[1]

In July 2025, news outlets including CBS News and The New York Times reported that passengers at certain US airports were no longer required to remove their shoes at security checkpoints. Coverage framed the change as a response to long-standing traveler complaints about inconvenience, slower processing lines and perceived burdens, and suggested the change could speed up security lines for millions of travelers each year.[1]

## Criticism and the equity rationale

Badlands commentary treated the reversal as "the end of a stupid and inconvenient era," arguing that the blanket requirement existed because behavioral or threat-based profiling had been framed as racist, forcing a universal rule on the entire travelling public. The commentary cited a 2010 statement by former TSA administrator Kip Hawley — "We apply the same rules to everyone because it's more equitable and more effective than trying to guess who looks suspicious" — as confirmation that equity, rather than security efficacy, drove the design of the policy. Fifteen years elapsed before passengers were allowed to keep their shoes on.[1]
