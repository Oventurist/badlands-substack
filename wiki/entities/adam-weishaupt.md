# Adam Weishaupt

Adam Weishaupt (February 6, 1748 – 1830) was the Bavarian law professor who founded the Order of the [[bavarian-illuminati|Illuminati]] in 1776. The Badlands Media feature "Legacy of the Bavarian Illuminati – Part 2," by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], compiles a chronological account of his life from period sources including Augustin Barruel, [[vernon-l-stauffer|Vernon L. Stauffer]], James H. Billington, Nesta H. Webster and [[john-robison|John Robison]].[1]

## Early life and education

Weishaupt was born in Ingolstadt, Bavaria, to Westphalian parents; the Weishaupt family name first appeared in Baden and was anciently associated with tribal conflicts in that area.[1] His father George died in 1755, after which the boy was turned over to his liberal godfather, [[johann-adam-ickstatt|Baron Johann Adam Ickstatt]] (1702–1776), curator of the [[university-of-ingolstadt|University of Ingolstadt]] and a member of the Privy Council.[1]

Although educated by the [[jesuits|Jesuits]], Weishaupt was "accorded free range in the private library of his godfather," where "the boy's questioning spirit was deeply impressed by the brilliant though pretentious works of the French 'philosophers' with which the shelves were plentifully stocked."[1] He studied law, economics, politics, history and philosophy, voraciously devouring every book he came across.[1]

## Academic career

Weishaupt graduated from the University of Ingolstadt in 1768 and served four years as a tutor and catechist.[1] In 1772 he was appointed professor of civil law.[1] After Pope Clement XIV dissolved the Jesuit Order in 1773, Weishaupt became the first layman to occupy the chair of canon law, a prestigious position held by a Jesuit for the previous ninety years; that same year he married against Ickstatt's wishes.[1] He was promoted to dean of the faculty of law in 1775, and his wife died on February 8, 1780.[1]

## Founding of the Illuminati

On May 1, 1776, Weishaupt founded the Order of the Illuminati with an original membership of five. The Order was secret, hierarchical and modeled on the Jesuits.[1] The names "Perfectibilists" and "Bees" were both considered before Weishaupt settled on "Illuminati," chosen perhaps for the "image of the sun radiating illumination to outer circles."[1] The Order was accordingly represented in members' communications by a circle with a dot at its center — symbolism the article links to Weishaupt's fascination with the [[eleusinian-mysteries|Eleusinian]] and Pythagorean Mysteries, likely acquired through Ickstatt's library.[1]

Fire worship was central to the Order's otherwise eclectic symbolism: "The Zoroastrian-Manichaean cult of fire was central to the otherwise eclectic symbolism of the Illuminists; their calendar was based on Persian rather than classical or Christian models."[1] Weishaupt himself explained that "the allegory in which the Mysteries and Higher Grades must be clothed is Fire Worship and the whole philosophy of Zoroaster," so that in the further degrees the Order was called the "Fire Order" or "Persian Order."[1] He built the Illuminati calendar on the era of the Persian King Yazdegerd III, with a year beginning on the first of Pharavardin (March 21) and months bearing Persian names, and required novices to write to superiors in cipher.[1]

Weishaupt was initiated into [[freemasonry|Freemasonry]] at the Lodge Theodore of Good Counsel in Munich, and by mid-1779 his "Insinuators" had wrested control of that lodge, which was thereafter regarded as part of the Order.[1]

## Conflict with Knigge and suppression

[[baron-von-knigge|Baron von Knigge]], initiated in July 1780, restructured the Order and greatly widened its recruitment, but resigned on April 20, 1784 after quarrels with Weishaupt over direction and management reached a boiling point; jealousy was apparent on both sides, and Weishaupt "certainly was a Machiavellian, by all accounts."[1] Knigge, convinced of Weishaupt's Jesuitism, accused him of being "a Jesuit in disguise."[1]

After [[carl-theodore|Duke Carl Theodore]] issued edicts against secret societies, Weishaupt left his university post two weeks before the March 2, 1785 ban, "obviously knowing about the approaching storm."[1] He fled across the border to Regensburg and finally settled at Gotha under the protection of the Illuminati member Duke of Saxe-Gotha.[1] Thirteen years later Augustin Barruel wrote that Weishaupt, "now banished from his country as a traitor to his Prince and to the whole Universe, peacefully at the court of Ernest Lewis, Duke of Saxe Gotha, enjoys an asylum, receives a pension from the public treasury, and is dignified with the title of Honorary Councilor to that Prince."[1]

Following the 1786 police seizure of documents at [[xavier-zwack|Xavier Zwack]]'s residence, Weishaupt wrote nine apologetic pamphlets in the space of a few months to save face, most notably *Apologie der Illuminaten* and *Vollständige Geschichte der Verfolgung der Illuminaten in Bayern*, both published at Frankfort and Leipzig in 1786.[1]

## Assessment

DeLarme concludes that the aims of Weishaupt and his associates "are still being ravenously pursued and expanded upon by today's subversive puppet masters," but argues that using the term "Illuminati" for those actors marks the speaker as relying on pop-cultural terminology rather than accurate historic record.[1] He likewise doubts that modern organizations marketing Illuminati membership online have "anything to do with the order of Adam Weishaupt from 250 years ago."[1]

## Part 1 account: religion, Plato and the philosophy of the Order

Part 1 of the same Badlands series gives a partly divergent biography, describing Weishaupt as a Jesuit and professor of canon law at the [[university-of-ingolstadt|University of Ingolstadt]] who was born in 1748 to Jewish parents but raised in the Catholic faith, and stating that his father George died in 1754, after which his godfather Baron Ickstatt turned him over to the [[jesuits|Jesuits]].[2] He converted to Protestantism at Ingolstadt before rebelling against the Abrahamic religions altogether, a pattern the article says is common among prominent ex-Jesuits such as [[karl-marx|Karl Marx]].[2] He famously wanted to replace Christianity, Judaism and Islam with a global religion of "reason," and his members came to call themselves "Perfectibilists."[2]

Weishaupt was guided by the model of the Republic laid out by [[plato|Plato]], dividing the society into three classes with a mystery class at its summit comprising the grades Priest, Regent, Magus and King.[2] Under his hypothetical utopia, marriage and the family would be eliminated, children born of promiscuous unions would be raised anonymously by the state, and the state would eliminate all defectives.[2] His recruitment spread across Bavaria and into Masonic lodges.[2]

Part 1 records that after the Order was exposed Weishaupt was discovered to be its head, deprived of his professor's chair and banished from Bavaria, going to Regensburg where he continued his activities, while [[xavier-zwack|Zwack]] and the Marquises Constanza and Savioli were banished alongside him.[2] Confronted with the incriminating material found among Zwack's papers, he said: "These things were not carried into effect, only spoken of, and were justifiable when taken in proper connection."[2] In a memo from Spartacus to Cato he argued that the blue degrees of [[freemasonry|Freemasonry]] were essentially concealed Christianity and that "Christ" should be substituted with the word "reason," calling for a new religion and a new state government.[2]

[[john-robison|John Robison]] described him under the code name Spartacus, after the leader of the slave insurrection that kept Rome in terror for three years.[2] The article characterizes him as having been, by various accounts, an atheist, a Cabalistic magician, a rationalist, a mystic, a democrat, a socialist, an anarchist, a fascist, a Machiavellian amoralist, an alchemist, a totalitarian, and — in [[thomas-jefferson|Thomas Jefferson]]'s verdict — an "enthusiastic philanthropist."[2] For a more favorable treatment it recommends Amelia Gill's 2008 translation of Weishaupt's *Die Lampe von Diogenese* and Peggy Pawlowski's 2004 doctoral thesis.[2]
