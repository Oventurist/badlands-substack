# Evan Gershkovich

Evan Gershkovich (also rendered "Gerskovich" in some reporting) is a Wall Street Journal reporter who was jailed in Russia on espionage charges that he and the United States government denied.[1] In July 2024 he was sentenced to 16 years in prison, and on August 1, 2024 Bloomberg News reported that Russia had agreed to release him as part of a major multi-country prisoner swap with the United States.[1]

## Release

According to the report, Gershkovich and former U.S. Marine [[paul-whelan|Paul Whelan]] were en route to destinations outside Russia, with the United States and its allies returning prisoners held under the deal.[1] It was not disclosed which Russian prisoners were released in the exchange, nor what other concessions Washington made.[1]

Gershkovich's case had earlier drawn attention when it was discussed by Russian President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] and Tucker Carlson in an interview earlier in 2024.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands commentary argued that journalists should press the [[white-house|White House]] for the undisclosed terms of the negotiation, while predicting the press would instead frame the swap as evidence of the Biden administration's competence.[1] The commentary further suggested Russia's change of heart was tied to the anticipated return of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], who had promised at the Republican National Convention to secure the prisoners' release, warning: "we want our hostages back—and they better be back before I assume office, or you will be paying a very big price."[1]

## Indictment and trial announcement (June 2024)

On Thursday, June 13, 2024, Russian prosecutors said in a statement that Gershkovich would stand trial on espionage charges.[3] The office of Russia's General Prosecutor said it had approved an indictment against him, claiming an investigation found he had collected information about a tank factory in March 2023 "on instructions from the [[cia|CIA]]."[3] Russian authorities had presented no evidence to support the claims and did not schedule the trial for a specific date.[3] Axios noted that a trial would likely resemble other sham proceedings against Americans detained in Russia and against political opponents of the Kremlin, and that Moscow might use it to increase pressure on the United States in ongoing prisoner swap talks.[3] Gershkovich was the first journalist arrested and held on spying charges in Russia since the Cold War, and had been jailed for over a year without formal charges or a trial.[3] The [[state-department|US Department of State]] designated him as wrongfully detained, and the [[wall-street-journal|Wall Street Journal]] repeatedly defended his reporting, rejected Russia's espionage claims, and called for his release.[3]

## Reporting on the release negotiations

A Wall Street Journal account of the negotiations for Gershkovich's release from Russian prison, summarized by Badlands News, described President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] as nearly botching the prisoner exchange through a lack of understanding of the situation, and suggested that Secretary of State [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]] was effectively running the process.[2]

At the White House Correspondents' Dinner, Gershkovich's mother Ella approached Biden to urge him to call German Chancellor [[olaf-scholz|Olaf Scholz]], a request she had been instructed to make by [[roger-carstens|Roger Carstens]], the special presidential envoy for hostage affairs.[2] Biden claimed he had already made the call; Blinken corrected him, saying they would make it.[2] Biden ultimately did not call, and a formal letter bearing his signature was sent to Scholz to advance the deal.[2] Mainstream journalists including CNN's Edward-Isaac Dovere portrayed the exchange as evidence of Biden's energy and diplomatic skill.[2]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that the story of Gershkovich and [[paul-whelan|Paul Whelan]] coming home vanished from headlines quickly and that the White House had been portrayed as confused and caught unaware, asking how the questions raised about who is truly managing US diplomacy did not constitute a national crisis.[2]
