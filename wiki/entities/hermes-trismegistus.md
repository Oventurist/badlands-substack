# Hermes Trismegistus

Hermes Trismegistus is the legendary figure to whom the teachings of [[hermeticism|Hermeticism]] are attributed.[1] The name comes from ancient Greek and translates to "Hermes the Thrice-Greatest."[1] He is typically described as a legendary Hellenistic figure originating as a syncretic combination of the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian god [[thoth|Thoth]].[1] He was profiled by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] in the February 2023 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] survey "Exploring Hermeticism."[1]

## Greek and Egyptian syncretism

DeLarme frames the fusion within the broader universality of ancient Greek theological vision: unlike their Semitic counterparts, the Greeks claimed no uniqueness for their deities and freely acknowledged that the Olympians often had exact analogs among the gods of other nations.[1] This was particularly true of Egypt, whose pantheon the Greeks revered as prototypes of their own, with cultured Greeks recognizing that some Egyptian gods, such as Isis, united within themselves a host of Greek deities.[1] The Romans, aware that their own gods were repurposed Greek deities, followed suit as the empire expanded across the Mediterranean, including Egypt.[1]

Greeks in the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt recognized the equivalence of Hermes and Thoth through [[interpretatio-graeca|interpretatio graeca]], the "Greek translation" discourse used to interpret the mythology and religion of other cultures, and consequently the two gods came to be worshiped as one.[1] The Temple of Thoth at Khemenu accordingly became known in the Hellenistic period as Hermopolis.[1]

## Attributed achievements and writings

Determining what is truth and what is legend regarding the being immortalized as Hermes and Thoth leads, in DeLarme's account, "through a labyrinth of dense and sometimes conflicting information."[1] Hermes/Thoth — who may or may not have been a real individual, or multiple individuals — is considered the founder of science, religion, mathematics, geometry, [[alchemy|alchemy]], philosophy, medicine and magic.[1]

Hermes is credited with tens of thousands of writings of high standing.[1] Plato's *Timaeus* and *Critias* state that the temple of Neith at Sais held secret halls containing historical records kept for 9,000 years, and Clement of Alexandria understood the Egyptians to possess forty-two sacred writings by Hermes encapsulating the whole training of Egyptian priests.[1]

## The epithet "Trismegistus"

The origin of the descriptor Trismegistus, "thrice great," is unclear.[1] Some reports place its first appearance in the minutes of a meeting of the council of the Ibis cult held in 172 BCE near Memphis in Egypt; other scholars assert the earliest occurrence was in the *Athenagora* by Philo of Byblos, c. 64–141 CE; another explanation derives it from an epithet of Thoth found at the Temple of Esna, "Thoth the great, the great, the great."[1]

According to the fathers of the Christian church, Hermes Trismegistus was either a contemporary of Moses or the third in a line of men named Hermes — Enoch, Noah, and the Egyptian priest-king known as Trismegistus — thrice great on account of being the greatest priest, philosopher and king.[1] That last account derives from statements in the [[emerald-tablet|Emerald Tablet]] that he knows the three parts of the wisdom of the whole universe: alchemy, astrology and theurgy.[1] The [[divine-pymander|Divine Pymander]], from which [[marsilio-ficino|Marsilio Ficino]] formed his opinion, states that "they called him Trismegistus because he was the greatest philosopher and the greatest priest and the greatest king."[1]

## Christian reception

Many Christian writers — Lactantius, Augustine, [[giordano-bruno|Giordano Bruno]], Marsilio Ficino, Campanella and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola — considered Hermes Trismegistus a wise pagan prophet who foresaw the coming of Christianity.[1] They believed in [[prisca-theologia|prisca theologia]], the doctrine that a single true theology threads through all religions, given by God to man in antiquity and passed through a series of prophets including Zoroaster and Plato, and appropriated Hermetic teachings to demonstrate its verity.[1]

## DeLarme's interpretation

Rather than thinking of Hermes as an actual individual, DeLarme offers his own view that it is better to think of Hermes as "an archetype of the truthseeker, the ultimate goal of truthseekers, or hidden knowledge personified."[1]
