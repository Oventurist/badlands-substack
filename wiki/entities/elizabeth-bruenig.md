# Elizabeth Bruenig

Elizabeth Bruenig is a staff writer for [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] who became the center of a media-ethics controversy in February 2026 over a heavily reported but fictional essay about an unvaccinated child dying of measles.[1]

## The measles essay controversy

The Atlantic published Bruenig's piece written from the perspective of a mother whose two unvaccinated children fall ill with measles; in the narrative the 11-month-old child dies.[1] The essay reads as a first-hand account, but it was a work of fiction: an editor's note at the end stated that "This story is based on extensive reporting and interviews with physicians, including those who have cared directly for patients with measles."[1] Bruenig's central figure was described as a composite narrative character constructed from medical literature on how measles progresses in severe cases, interviews with physicians who have treated fatal complications, and documented historical cases.[1]

[[laura-hazard-owen|Laura Hazard Owen]] of [[niemanlab|NiemanLab]] publicly identified the piece as fiction, writing that she was initially "stunned" to believe an Atlantic staff writer's unvaccinated child had died of measles, and that on discovering the editor's note she felt "very conflicted" about whether readers who do not vaccinate their children would simply "brush it off as fiction, or fake."[1] Multiple journalists questioned the truthfulness of the reporting, which reportedly led to the disclaimer, and [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] covered the episode as The Atlantic being "busted fabricating" a dead-child measles story.[1]

## Criticism

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that Bruenig reportedly has two daughters with her husband and questioned the construction of an invented dead child to convey authority, quoting passages describing labored breathing and "shadows pooling between her ribs" that a reader would take as maternal testimony.[1] Ashe argued that defending the work as "creative nonfiction with heavily-researched composite characters" is Orwellian, situating it in the lineage of [[new-journalism|New Journalism]] and concluding that "inventing dead, unvaxxed children to drive social change is not journalism, new or old. It's emotionally manipulative propaganda."[1]
