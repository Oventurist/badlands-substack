# Eurofer (European Steel Association)

Eurofer, the European Steel Association, is the industry body representing steel producers across the European Union. It functions as the sector's principal voice in trade disputes, publishing forecasts of export volumes and lobbying on questions of overcapacity, import competition, and the costs of the EU's industrial decarbonisation agenda.[1]

## Response to the 2025 US steel tariffs

In February 2025 Eurofer emerged as one of the loudest European critics of United States trade policy after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed an order enacting a 25% tariff on steel imports. Eurofer President Henrik Adam said on 11 February 2025 that the measure would negatively affect the EU steel industry, warning that EU steel exports could fall by as much as 3.7 million tonnes.[1] The association noted that the United States was the second-largest export market for European producers, accounting for 16% of total EU steel exports in 2024, and that losses on that scale could not be offset by redirecting shipments to other markets.[1]

Adam framed the tariffs as an accelerant on pre-existing structural problems rather than their sole cause. He argued that global steel overcapacity was already being "off-loaded massively on the vulnerable EU steel market at very cheap prices, mainly from Asia, North-Africa and the Middle East," a dynamic he said was undermining the sector's ability to invest in the green transition and driving the de-industrialisation of Europe.[1] Eurofer cited the closure of 9 million tonnes of EU capacity during 2024 alone, accompanied by more than 18,000 announced job cuts, and predicted that the Trump executive order would "inevitably further exacerbate the situation."[1]

## Commentary

Badlands commentary treated Eurofer's warning as confirmation of the leverage inherent in tariff policy, arguing that if American duties genuinely threatened to bring European industry "to its knees" while European leaders sought to sustain the Ukraine war without United States backing, then tariffs represented the strongest bargaining instrument available to Washington.[1]
