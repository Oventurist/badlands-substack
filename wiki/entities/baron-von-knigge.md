# Baron von Knigge

Baron von Knigge was the German nobleman and prominent Strict Observance freemason who reorganized the [[bavarian-illuminati|Bavarian Illuminati]] after joining it in 1780, and whose subsequent break with [[adam-weishaupt|Adam Weishaupt]] preceded the Order's collapse.[1]

## Role in the Order

Knigge was initiated into the Order in July 1780. He was connected to the court of Hesse-Cassel and was already a prominent freemason of the Strict Observance rite.[1] He restructured the Order and recruited many prominent members: "the notion of restricting the field of recruiting solely to the young was abandoned, and this phase of the propaganda was widened so as to include men of experience whose wisdom and influence might be counted upon to assist in attaining the objects of the order."[1] Largely because of Knigge's circle of influence, the Illuminati had "between two and three thousand members" by 1784.[1]

Knigge represented the Order of the Illuminati as its delegate to the [[congress-of-wilhelmsbad|Congress of Wilhelmsbad]] convened on July 16, 1782 — the most important Masonic congress of the eighteenth century, and, in the account of Nesta H. Webster, the occasion on which "the alliance between Illuminism and [[freemasonry|Freemasonry]] was finally sealed."[1]

## Resignation

On April 20, 1784, Knigge resigned from the Illuminati after his quarrels with Weishaupt over the Order's direction and management reached a boiling point, with a certain amount of jealousy apparent on both sides.[1] On July 1 he signed a formal agreement to return all property, rituals and initiations belonging to the Order and to maintain silence about its secrets.[1] Knigge had become convinced of Weishaupt's Jesuitism, accusing him of being "a Jesuit in disguise."[1]

## In Robison's account

[[john-robison|John Robison]] gives Knigge's full name as Baron Adolf Franz Friedrich Knigge (1752–1796) and mentions him constantly throughout *Proofs of a Conspiracy*.[2] Robison traces the [[bavarian-illuminati|Illuminati]] to an aberration in Freemasonry started by Weishaupt and assisted by Knigge, and reports at the end of his first chapter, "Schisms in Free Masonry," that Knigge and other cosmo-political brethren formed a scheme for uniting radical Masonic lodges in Germany, Knigge being dissatisfied with all the existing Masonic degrees.[2]

Within the Order Knigge bore the code name Philo, part of a system of aliases drawn from classical Greek and Roman personalities known for ruthlessness and cynicism.[2] In a letter from Philo to Cato — [[xavier-zwack|Zwack]] — he described religious authorities as cheats who bow to superstition and fanaticism and advocated a slow overthrow of religion so that the fanatics would not be alarmed or aware of what was going on.[2] Of Jewish mysticism he declared: "The Jewish Theosophy was a mystery like the Eleusinsian, or the Pythagorean, unfit for the vulgar."[2]
