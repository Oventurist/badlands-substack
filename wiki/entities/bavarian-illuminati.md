# Bavarian Illuminati

The Bavarian Illuminati was a secret society founded in 18th-century Europe. In the account given by [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], its founders were among the influential Europeans with whom [[jacob-frank|Jacob Frank]] "palled around" and rubbed elbows.[1]

Deschamps notes that the Illuminati and comparable geopolitical movements of the era also, "strangely enough," carried the ambition of restoring the Holy Land to God's people — remarking sardonically that the Illuminati presumably "cared so deeply about helping religiously oppressed people."[1] He treats this convergence as evidence that the Holy Land restoration project served elite political ends rather than religious ones, part of a lineage running from [[neoplatonism|Neoplatonism]] and the Crusades through [[sabbateanism|Sabbateanism]] and Frankism into modern [[zionism|Zionism]] and the framing of the [[october-7-attacks|October 7 attacks]].[1]

## Terminology and scope

A Badlands Media feature by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], "Legacy of the Bavarian Illuminati – Part 1," argues that the term "Illuminati" is used inaccurately as a catch-all for any and all groups operating in a clandestine fashion, and carries more baggage than more precise terms like "[[deep-state|Deep State]]" or "Cabal."[2] The Deep State, he writes, is better understood not as a single secretive institution but as a loose-knit amalgamation of groups reluctantly working hand in glove to achieve full-spectrum dominance of humanity, otherwise known as [[synarchism|synarchism]].[2]

DeLarme stresses that the group in question is the 18th-century Bavarian Illuminati of [[adam-weishaupt|Adam Weishaupt]], and is not to be confused with the Alumbrados of 15th- and 16th-century Spain or with modern online imitations that merely revive the name; reviving a name, he argues, is not the same as being *of* that thing.[2] The Order has supposedly been traced back to the Knights Templar, to the Greek and Gnostic initiatory cults, to Egypt and sometimes even to Atlantis, all unprovable claims; the one safe generalization is that Weishaupt's intent to maintain secrecy worked, since no two students of Illuminology have ever agreed totally about what the Order's inner secret or purpose was.[2] Sensational exposés have appeared every generation since 1776, blaming the Bavarian conspirators for everything wrong with the world.[2]

## Origin and structure

The widely held date of origin is May 1, 1776, though it is contested whether this is the true founding date or simply the date the public became aware of the group; May Day is in any case a date long honored by communists, whose philosophy some claim was based on Illuminati doctrine.[2] The Bavarian Illuminati was an Enlightenment-era secret society whose own statutes stated its goal as "to put an end to the machinations of the purveyors of injustice, to control them without dominating them."[2]

Weishaupt, a [[jesuits|Jesuit]]-educated professor of canon law at the [[university-of-ingolstadt|University of Ingolstadt]], spread recruitment across the cities of Bavaria and made connections with a number of Masonic lodges, where the group gained a strong foothold in the same fashion as other powerful institutions operating within the secretive confines of the European lodges.[2] Members came to call themselves "Perfectibilists," and Weishaupt famously wanted to replace Christianity, Judaism and Islam with a global religion of "reason."[2]

The society was carefully structured into three main classes, modeled on [[plato|Plato]]'s *Republic*; its highest class was a mystery class comprising the grades Priest, Regent, Magus and King, a hierarchical scheme echoed in later occult groups such as the Rosicrucians and Ordo Templi Orientis.[2] Over time the movement acquired a rigorously complex constitution and an internal communication system conducted in cipher, and at its apex it operated across a very large area extending from Italy to Denmark and from Warsaw to Paris.[2] Because Masonry was itself a secret society, the Illuminati constituted a secret society within a secret society.[2]

## Discovery and suppression

The sensational story of the Order's discovery holds that an Illuminati courier named Johann Jakob Lanz, a former Catholic priest, was killed by lightning in 1785 while riding through Regensburg, and that police examining his saddle bag found evidence of the Order along with detailed plans for the coming [[french-revolution|French Revolution]] — a story refuted by the Masonic lodges.[2] In 1783, four professors of the Marianne Academy were summoned before a court of inquiry over their allegiance to the Order, and it emerged that the Order abjured Christianity and refused admission to its higher degrees to all who adhered to Catholicism's three confessions.[2]

In 1785 the Illuminati were suppressed by the Bavarian government for allegedly plotting to overthrow all the kings in Europe and the Pope, a point generally agreed upon by all historians; Weishaupt was stripped of his professorship and banished, along with the Marquis Constanza, the Marquis Savioli and the lawyer [[xavier-zwack|Zwack]], while other members were imprisoned.[2] No evidence of the Bavarian order appears in the historical record after 1785, though non-affiliated groups have tried to resurrect the name.[2]

## Critics and legacy claims

The two major early critics were [[john-robison|John Robison]], author of *Proofs of a Conspiracy* (1798), and Abbé Barruel, both of whom published in English; the German source documents have only recently been translated, and DeLarme cautions that these translations are gatekept by Freemasons.[2] [[george-washington|George Washington]] read Robison shortly before his death and affirmed his belief that Illuminati doctrines and Jacobin principles had spread in the United States.[2] [[thomas-jefferson|Thomas Jefferson]], by contrast, judged Weishaupt an "enthusiastic philanthropist."[2]

The Order has variously been credited with managing the French and [[american-revolution|American]] revolutions behind the scenes, taking over the world, being the brains behind [[communism|Communism]], continuing underground up to the 1970s, secretly worshipping the Devil, and being lizard-like extraterrestrials from the Orion system — claims that remain unproven.[2] There is no argument, DeLarme writes, about the relationship between the Illuminati and the [[jacobins|Jacobins]].[2] The [[league-of-the-just|League of the Just]], the secret society that directed [[karl-marx|Karl Marx]], is described as the Illuminati's spiritual successor or direct collaborator.[2] The Order also paralleled the [[frankism|Frankists]] in time and geography, both seated in Germany — the Illuminati in Ingolstadt and the Frankists in Offenbach after 1786.[2]

## Founding, symbolism and calendar

The second part of DeLarme's feature dates the Order's founding precisely to May 1, 1776, with an original membership of five, and describes it as secret, hierarchical and modeled on the [[jesuits|Jesuits]].[3] "Perfectibilists" and "Bees" were both considered as names before [[adam-weishaupt|Weishaupt]] settled on "Illuminati," chosen perhaps for the "image of the sun radiating illumination to outer circles," and the Order was thereafter represented in members' communications by a circle with a dot at its center — imagery reflective of Weishaupt's fascination with the [[eleusinian-mysteries|Eleusinian]] and Pythagorean Mysteries.[3]

The Zoroastrian-Manichaean cult of fire was central to the Order's otherwise eclectic symbolism, and its calendar was based on Persian rather than classical or Christian models, commemorating the era of the Persian King Yazdegerd III; in the higher degrees the Order was known as the "Fire Order" or "Persian Order."[3] Novices had to learn to date letters by the "Illuminized Hegira," whose year began on the first of Pharavardin (March 21), and to write to superiors in cipher, with hieroglyphic ciphers reserved for the higher Scotch Knight degrees.[3] Weishaupt was initiated into [[freemasonry|Freemasonry]] at Munich's Lodge Theodore of Good Counsel, and by mid-1779 his "Insinuators" had wrested control of that lodge, which was thereafter regarded as part of the Order.[3]

## Growth, doctrine and internal conflict

[[baron-von-knigge|Baron von Knigge]], initiated in July 1780, restructured the Order and abandoned the practice of recruiting solely among the young, so that by 1784 membership stood at "between two and three thousand."[3] The Order was represented by Knigge at the [[congress-of-wilhelmsbad|Congress of Wilhelmsbad]] in July 1782, described by Nesta H. Webster as the occasion on which the alliance between Illuminism and Freemasonry was finally sealed.[3] Knigge resigned on April 20, 1784 after quarrelling with Weishaupt, signing a formal agreement of restitution and silence and accusing Weishaupt of being "a Jesuit in disguise."[3]

The 1785 juridical deposition of [[joseph-utzschneider|Joseph Utzschneider]], Grünberger and Cosandey characterized the lower degrees as a training and espionage system operating on the maxim *Oderint dum metuant*, and set out the Order's teachings: freedom from all religion as a precondition of advancement, the doctrine of *Patet Exitus* on suicide, the principle that "the end sanctifies the means," submission to Illuminati superiors over sovereigns and magistrates, and the incompatibility of patriotism with "the ultimate ends of the Order."[3]

## Suppression and afterlife

[[carl-theodore|Duke Carl Theodore]] issued three edicts against the Order — on June 22, 1784, March 2, 1785 and August 16, 1787 — the last prescribing death by the sword for continued recruiting and confiscation and perpetual banishment for those recruited.[3] Police searches of [[xavier-zwack|Xavier Zwack]]'s residence in 1786 and Baron Bassus' castle at Sandersdorf in 1787 produced membership rosters, counterfeit government seals, poison and secret-ink recipes and other material that the Bavarian government published as *Einige Originalschriften des Illuminaten Ordens* and *Nachtrag von weitern Originalschriften*.[3]

DeLarme concludes that the Illuminati was a short-lived order and but a single clandestine group in a long series operating under what [[william-stuart|William Stuart]] calls "[[red-masonry|Red Masonry]]," of which hundreds of orders were formed; modern organizations trading on the name, complete with recruitment websites and e-shops, are dismissed as grifters.[3] Stuart holds that later incarnations of the same agenda include the [[bilderberg|Bilderberg Group]] and the [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]].[3] Using the term "Illuminati" for today's subversive actors, DeLarme argues, marks the speaker as relying on pop-cultural terminology rather than accurate historic record.[3]
