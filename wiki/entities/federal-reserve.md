# Federal Reserve

The Federal Reserve is presented in Erik Carlson's essay as the beneficiary of a transfer of banking control away from the [[city-of-london|City of London]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-journey-of-ever-changing-truths)

## Role in the article

Carlson writes that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], fighting a silent war against the City of London since early in his first presidency, replaced the [[libor|LIBOR]] system with the [[sofr|SOFR]] system — taking control of the American banking system away from the City of London and giving it to the American Federal Reserve.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-journey-of-ever-changing-truths)

The Fed thus appears not as an antagonist in this article but as the domestic institution to which authority over US benchmark rates was repatriated, one element of a broader campaign that also includes onshoring manufacturing from [[communist-china|Communist China]] and the use of tariffs against globalist power.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-journey-of-ever-changing-truths)

## Banker to the banks, and the FedAccounts proposal

A separate Badlands Media essay describes the Federal Reserve as currently serving as a bank for bankers, facilitating inter-bank payments, managing bank accounts and paying interest on those accounts — a service that could be extended to individuals and small businesses under the [[fedaccounts|FedAccounts]] proposal, creating "a FedAccount for everyone."[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Because deposits at a Federal Reserve Bank cannot be defaulted on, such accounts would lessen if not eliminate the need for widespread deposit insurance, and a debit card issued by the Fed would hold "pure sovereign money" — the liability of the issuing institution itself — rather than the liability of a private bank.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Members of Congress have proposed legislation requiring the Fed to offer such accounts in conjunction with the [[united-states-postal-service|U.S. Postal Service]], with deposits at banks holding more than $10 billion in assets passing through to the Fed's balance sheet rather than the bank's.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

Badlands Media argues that readers should mentally substitute "Treasury" for "Federal Reserve" throughout that reasoning, on the grounds that the Fed is itself unconstitutional and that the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury]] is properly in charge of the people's money.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) The same article states that the Postal Savings System was created in 1911, before the Fed was founded, and accuses the Fed of decades of back-door bailouts handing taxpayer money to Wall Street banks.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) It further claims that [[donald-trump|Trump]] took control of the Federal Reserve by appointing every member of the board of governors except one, a governor retained because of her support for the digital dollar.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Founding and the 1913 convergence

A further Badlands essay dates the Fed's creation to around 1913 and calls it unconstitutional, noting that the [[federal-income-tax|federal income tax]], the founding of the Federal Reserve and the formation of the [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]] all occurred in that same year — a convergence the author insists is not coincidental, since it granted big bankers the ability to print money, set interest rates and direct the flow of the money supply simultaneously with the imposition of income taxation.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) Between the American Civil War and 1913, the essay observes, America became an economic giant with no federal income tax at all.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Money creation and inflation

In this account the Fed prints money out of thin air to fund itself and the wider financial system, passing that money to banks and corporations and thence among corporations, governments and politicians, so that government represents banks and corporations rather than the people.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) The resulting [[inflation|inflation]] is described as the largest financial scam of all: newly printed money lent to banks dilutes the purchasing power of existing money, raising house prices, while banks collect interest on the same money and are able to write larger loans precisely because prices rose.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Interest rate history since 2006

The essay traces the Fed's post-2006 conduct as evidence of Deep State control over the financial system.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) In 2007, recognizing the economy was near collapse, the Fed cut rates by 5% over the course of the year ahead of the autumn 2008 banking collapse; once the banking system became insolvent it cut rates to zero and bought assets onto its balance sheet to create liquidity.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) Such measures are characterized as a defibrillator requiring eventual normalization, yet across eight years under [[barack-obama|Obama]] the Fed never raised rates to a normal level or reduced the balance sheet — a suppression the author attributes to the need for Facebook, Amazon, Apple, Netflix and Google to expand into mega-cap companies with [[cia|CIA]] backing.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

The first rate hike followed [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s 2016 election win, with Trump obliged to retain [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] for two years before [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] took over in 2018 and raised rates aggressively, producing a 20% stock market drop that autumn and cuts in 2019.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember) When the Covid lockdowns arrived in early 2020, the essay holds that Trump forced the Fed to print money at an unprecedented rate to save the economy — "fighting the [[cabal|Cabal]] with their own weapon."[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-week-to-remember)

## Lack of audit

In an April 2024 essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] offered the Federal Reserve as his central example of an institution that cannot be questioned or held accountable, noting that it has never been audited — "not once" — despite having existed for more than a hundred years.[[4]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) He tied its creation to the ratification of the [[sixteenth-amendment|Sixteenth Amendment]] on February 3, 1913, arguing the near-simultaneity of the two was no coincidence and that American politicians knew the public would never vote in a Federal Reserve System run by the bankers themselves, so the power to decide was taken away from the people.[[4]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

Carlson also identified [[inflation|inflation]] — money printed "out of nothing, backed by nothing," diluting the purchasing power of existing money — as the largest tax of all, and argued that because the government can print at will it does not actually need tax revenue; taxation persists instead as a key component of creating and maintaining debt slaves.[[4]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

## CBDC and the digital dollar

The article "Are CBDCs The Path To Fear, or Freedom?" places the Federal Reserve at the center of the American [[central-bank-digital-currency|CBDC]] effort, reporting that [[jared-kushner|Jared Kushner]] was quietly advocating in 2019 for the Fed to launch its own central bank digital currency.[5] Joe Lange writes that he has previously argued Trump controls the Fed and the dollar and is using them to bankrupt the global debt system, and that Trump retains control of both the Treasury and the Fed through [[devolution|devolution]] and [[continuity-of-government|Continuity of Government]] plans.[5]

The Fed's [[fednow|FedNow]] service is described as a peer-to-peer payment platform — not a digital currency — that every financial service in the country can access and which eliminates intermediary fees.[5] Lange further contends that Trump is engineering a court fight between the Federal Reserve and states that have banned CBDCs, aimed at forcing the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] to rule on the definition of money and on whether an all-powerful central bank is constitutional under the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act of 1913]].[5]

## 2026 rate-cut outlook and the independence fight

The Federal Reserve was expected to begin cutting interest rates in 2026 as economic data pointed to slowing labor-market conditions and easing inflation.[6] Projections from the [[congressional-budget-office|Congressional Budget Office]] put the federal funds rate at approximately 3.4% by the end of 2026, with inflation remaining above the Fed's 2% target in the near term before gradually converging, and longer-term Treasury yields staying elevated or rising modestly so that mortgage and other long-term borrowing costs could remain high even as short-term rates fall.[6] Payroll growth was expected to slow, with forecasts placing unemployment in the mid-4% range and weekly initial jobless claims low by historical standards but edging higher — a cooling rather than a sharp downturn.[6] Fed policymakers expressed differing views on the pace of easing, and recent rate decisions included dissents reflecting debate over balancing inflation risks against labor-market softening.[6]

Attention also focused on leadership changes: President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] stated that a decision had been made on a nominee to succeed Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] when Powell's term ends in May 2026, though no formal nomination had been announced.[6] Badlands contributor Burning Bright wrote that Trump had been publicly leaning on the Fed to supercharge the "Golden Age" economy since before taking office, that the Fed had stalled, and that Trump's mortgage buy-back program was the first in an expected cascade of moves demonstrating he does not need Powell.[6]

Separately, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] was set to hear the case involving Trump and Fed Governor [[lisa-cook|Lisa Cook]], widely framed as a test of Federal Reserve independence; Badlands contributor Ashe in America argued instead that the case turns on whether [[congress|Congress]] may insulate executive officers from presidential removal, and that nothing in the Constitution creates or protects an independent central bank since the Fed exists entirely by statute.[6]


## 2026 removal litigation outcome
In a 5-4 decision issued in late June 2026, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] declined to immediately allow President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to remove Governor [[lisa-cook|Lisa Cook]], remanding the dispute and leaving her in office pending further litigation.[7] The Court rejected the administration's contention that judicial review of such removals is nearly unavailable, an outcome described as temporarily preserving the Federal Reserve's independence.[7] The decision stood in contrast to a companion 6-3 ruling the same day granting presidents broad authority to remove Senate-confirmed leaders of executive branch agencies.[7]

## Leadership succession and the subpoena fight, March 2026

By mid-March 2026, tensions over interest-rate policy and Federal Reserve independence had intensified ahead of the expected nomination of [[kevin-warsh|Kevin Warsh]] to succeed Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]], whose term ends May 15, 2026.[8] Warsh previously served as a Federal Reserve governor from 2006 to 2011, and Republican Senator [[thom-tillis|Thom Tillis]] was reported to be involved in the confirmation discussions.[8] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] repeatedly and publicly criticized Powell over interest rates and broader monetary policy.[8]

In a parallel legal dispute, Federal Judge [[james-boasberg|James Boasberg]] dismissed the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]]'s Federal Reserve subpoenas, which had sought documents about the central bank's building renovation and Powell's allegedly false testimony concerning it.[8] Boasberg wrote that he was "left with no credible reason to think that the Government is investigating suspicious facts as opposed to targeting a disfavored official."[8]

Court filings later reported by Barron's indicated that Powell's lawyer told federal prosecutors in January that the chair believes he would remain on the Fed's board of governors past the expiration of his chairmanship if the criminal investigation into him stays open — raising the prospect of an unresolved leadership overlap at the central bank.[8]

## January 2026 rate pause amid the Powell investigation

At its January 2026 Federal Open Market Committee meeting the Federal Reserve left its benchmark interest rate unchanged, announcing no change in policy stance and providing no forward guidance signaling an imminent move.[9] Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] held a press conference afterward, his first since reports emerged of a [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] investigation into his congressional testimony about the Fed's headquarters renovation project and allegations that he lied during it.[9] Federal prosecutors issued criminal subpoenas connected to the investigation, and reports indicated the Fed had failed to comply with them.[9] Powell's term as chair ends on May 15, 2026.[9]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described the Fed as "perhaps the institution most emblematic of the System itself" and characterized President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s campaign against it as the core animating engine of his broader program rather than one initiative among many.[9] In that framing, Trump has the Fed caught in a pincer between freezing and cutting rates, with cuts destroying the remaining purchasing power of the Federal Reserve note while boosting asset portfolios and cultivating public mandate for a transition to a real money system — reinforced by a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve, stablecoin regulation that requires private issuers to hold proportional reserves, and tariffs that collapse false valuations for conglomerates resisting on-shoring.[9] The predicted result is a crash contained to the old system alongside a soft landing for the new.[9]

## Headquarters renovation probe and the confirmation blockade (January 2026)

In January 2026 the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] opened a criminal investigation into Chairman [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] over his congressional testimony concerning renovations to the Federal Reserve's headquarters, which Powell confirmed publicly on Sunday, January 11.[10] Senator [[thom-tillis|Thom Tillis]] responded by saying he would oppose confirmation of new Federal Reserve nominees while the probe remained open, a stance [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted would leave the central bank "rudderless and incapacitated."[10] House Financial Services Chair [[french-hill|French Hill]] defended Powell's integrity, while the [[white-house|White House]] denied involvement in the probe.[10]

Badlands treated the episode as an opportunity rather than a crisis, arguing that a Fed left without confirmed governors and a chairman under criminal investigation moves the institution closer to dissolution — "Ending the charade leads to ending the Fed."[10]

## June 2026 inflation data and the July decision

June 2026's consumer price index fell 0.4 percent — the largest monthly decline since April 2020 — driven mainly by lower energy prices, while services inflation excluding energy stayed flat; annual inflation eased to 3.5 percent from 4.2 percent in May, still well above the Fed's 2 percent target.[11] The reading arrived after officials including board member [[christopher-waller|Christopher Waller]] had signaled willingness to hike as soon as July, and it weakened the immediate case for doing so while leaving the outlook fragile and tied to Middle East and energy-market developments.[11]

Chair [[kevin-warsh|Kevin Warsh]] cautioned against complacency, saying he would not "overread or cherry-pick data," rejecting any "mission accomplished" framing, and insisting that "inflation is a choice" and that the Fed "can and will deliver price stability."[11] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued the institution remained trapped in a game-theory pincer — hiking risked a true recession, cutting too quickly risked another inflation wave — and predicted rate cuts in 2026 timed so the next liquidity wave would reach Main Street rather than Wall Street, echoing the Cantillon Effect critique that proximity to the money printer confers disproportionate power over the market.[11]

## Leadership transition (January 2026)

A change in Federal Reserve leadership became imminent as Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]]'s term approached its May 2026 expiration.[12] Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] said President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had narrowed the list of successors to four and would announce a choice in January: [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]], former Council of Economic Advisers chair and then National Economic Council director; [[kevin-warsh|Kevin Warsh]], a former Fed governor focused on institutional credibility and financial stability; [[christopher-waller|Christopher Waller]], a sitting governor identified with the Fed's inflation-fighting posture; and [[rick-rieder|Rick Rieder]], the [[blackrock|BlackRock]] executive who oversees global fixed income.[12]

The appointment was expected to come as US economic growth continued at a moderate pace, supported by tax cuts, ongoing investment in AI and technology, and reduced tariff uncertainty — while political pressure on the Fed intensified and the institution suffered a crisis of public trust.[12]

## "Monetary tightening through bank collapse"

During the April 2023 failure of [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]], ZeroHedge characterized the Fed as running a policy of "monetary tightening through bank collapse," having failed to contain inflation and tighten policy using conventional means.[13] On that reading, a wipeout of the $30 billion in large-bank deposits placed into First Republic would spark a systemic crisis.[13] Badlands commentary treated the episode as further exposure of the fiat central banking system, which contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] counted as a good thing even if the road got a little bumpy.[13]

## May 2024: rates held at a 23-year high

On May 1, 2024, the Federal Reserve held interest rates at a 23-year high as persistent inflation continued to push back the expected timing of a rate cut.[14] US markets closed mixed after a volatile session: stocks jumped when Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] indicated at his press conference that policymakers believed it unlikely they would raise rates again in this cycle, but investors could not sustain the rally.[14] The Dow ended higher by 87 points, or 0.2 percent, while the S&P 500 and the tech-heavy Nasdaq each fell 0.3 percent; all three major indexes had closed April lower after five months of gains, with the Dow notching its worst month since September 2022.[14]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the tightening cycle's pain as productive, writing that "widespread pain prompts widespread awakening" because the economy is the shared communication vector through which all people interact, and that short-term pain was arguably necessary to accelerate that awakening even as a deeper transition in the US economy proceeded.[14]

## March 2023 rate decision

At its March 22, 2023 meeting the Federal Open Market Committee hiked the benchmark rate by 25 basis points to a 4.75%–5% target range, as expected, and said it would continue reducing its Treasury and mortgage-backed securities holdings at the same pace, maintaining quantitative tightening.[15] The statement dropped the language that "ongoing increases in the target range" would be appropriate, replacing it with "some additional policy firming may be appropriate" — a dovish shift on future policy.[15] The Fed also added a nod to the banking turmoil then underway, stating that "the U.S. banking system is sound and resilient" while warning that recent developments were likely to result in tighter credit conditions for households and businesses and to weigh on economic activity, hiring and inflation, with the extent of these effects uncertain.[15] More hawkishly, it removed the phrase that inflation "has eased somewhat," leaving only "remains elevated," and added that "the Committee will closely monitor incoming information and assess the implications for monetary policy"; the terminal rate implied by the dot plot was unchanged from December.[15]

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued the decision was unsurprising to anyone who understood "the real purpose of the Fed," and that unrealized losses on long-dated paper held by banks would continue to be a problem.[15] He framed the hike as an active component of a controlled implosion of the economy intended to bring about a [[great-reset|Great Reset]] economy, describing the Fed as historically "a weapon of economic warfare on the part of international bankers": raising rates might slow inflation by pulling money out of the money supply and restricting bank lending, but would render over-leveraged banks insolvent, while returning to a quantitative-easing posture would sustain inflation alongside stagnating productivity.[15] Both paths, in his account, lead to destruction, the latter being a slower and more obscured demolition that would not generate the public buy-in required to reboot the system.[15]

Deschamps placed the moment in a cycle in which the rules governing the international economy are rewritten every 60 to 80 years, the last such shift being the [[bretton-woods-system|Bretton Woods]] agreement just before the end of World War II, which created the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]], the [[world-bank|World Bank]] and the dollar as global reserve currency — itself built, he argued, on a quiet global gold bankruptcy between 1910 and 1950 orchestrated by bankers who manipulated gold markets and sovereign holdings for decades before the departure from a fixed gold standard.[15] The current push, in this framing, is toward [[central-bank-digital-currency|Central Bank Digital Currencies]] and the control apparatus attached to them, an agenda he said would succeed unless the public becomes literate in law, money and finance and opts out by building a superior monetary system.[15]

## Rate hold and the July 2024 jobs report

The [[july-2024-us-jobs-report|July 2024 jobs report]], released on August 2, 2024, showed the US economy adding 114,000 jobs against expectations of about 176,000 and unemployment rising to 4.3% from 4.1%, triggering a market sell-off.[16] Because the release came only days after the Federal Reserve decided to hold interest rates steady rather than cut them in an attempt to restabilize the economy, it fuelled fears that the central bank was behind the curve and that a recession might be on the horizon.[16] Badlands commentary went further, rejecting the official position that the country was not yet in recession and arguing that a recession had begun in late 2021, obscured by manipulated metrics and redefined terms.[16]

## Badlands critique of monetary boom-and-bust cycles (2023)

Commenting in March 2023 on the surge of 30-year fixed mortgage rates back above 7 percent — which sent homebuyer applications to a 28-year low — [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] described "the controlled demolition of the economy," attributing record [[inflation|inflation]] to the money printed during the COVID-19 pandemic combined with a collapse of confidence in the economy and government.[17] He argued that the Fed's apparent incompetence is a ruse and that international bankers had used economic warfare tactics to destabilize markets long before the institution's creation.[17] In his account, boom and bust cycles can be artificially generated through money printing, criminal activity, supply-line interruptions and destabilizing events such as plagues, fires and train derailments; in 2020 and earlier the Fed flooded some markets with capital while depriving others of credit, producing artificial supply-and-demand differentials that magnified inflation.[17] The subsequent phase, he wrote, is monetary constriction, in which rising interest rates make money hard to obtain and slow the velocity of money — a dynamic he said could only be countered by building parallel systems resting on an informed citizenry.[17]

## Pressure and the 2025 reset thesis

In August 2025, as Chairman [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] prepared his final Jackson Hole speech, the Federal Reserve faced mounting pressure from the [[white-house|White House]] to lower interest rates while inflation remained above the 2% target and the labor market weakened, with July's jobs report showing fewer additions than expected and downward revisions erasing prior gains.[18] Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the central banking system as "the invisible enemy propping up the fiat regime" and argued that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had cornered it on both Actual and Narrative fronts, forcing every decision to accelerate the system's exposure and eventual reset.[18]

He described the episode as part of a controlled demolition of the Fed and the fiat apparatus, exposing hypocrisy at the top and cultivating a public mandate to question Fed independence while revealing how insiders had gamed the system for personal gain.[18] Trump, in this account, amplified the narrative push without intervening in the actual squeeze, letting market pressure build long enough to trigger capitulations among fiat-dependent players.[18] Signals of a coming reset included the fast-tracking of [[stephen-miran|Stephen Miran]] to a board vacancy and Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]]'s open anticipation of a "global economic reordering," with the [[lisa-cook|Lisa Cook]] controversy spotlighted as a key fracture point.[18]

## Headquarters renovation controversy (2025)

In July 2025 the Fed's planned renovations to its headquarters became a political flashpoint.[19] [[bill-pulte|Bill Pulte]], director of the [[federal-housing-finance-agency|Federal Housing Finance Agency]], asked [[congress|Congress]] to investigate Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] over Senate testimony about the project that Pulte called "deceptive," asserting it was grounds for removal "for cause."[19] Badlands commentary put the renovation's cost at a reported $2.5 billion and treated the episode as further evidence for ending the central bank altogether.[19] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], long critical of the Fed's reluctance to cut rates, amplified Pulte's call for Powell to resign.[19]

## March 2023 bank backstop and the liquidity pincer

On the night of March 12–13, 2023, the Federal Reserve acted jointly with the Treasury and the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] to backstop depositors following the failures of [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]] and [[signature-bank|Signature Bank]], but regional bank shares fell dramatically the next session amid multiple trading halts, and credit-default-swap spreads rose at the global majors including [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]].[20] Commentary asked why systemic signals kept worsening if the Fed's backstop was genuinely sufficient and the banking system genuinely resilient.[20]

Analyst [[brandon-smith|Brandon Smith]] argued the outcome depended entirely on the Fed's response: it might drag out the crash for months with stop-gaps, and while a return to stimulus would rescue banks and equities for a time, inflation would then suffocate consumers within a year.[20] He predicted the Fed would rely on contained interventions without reversing rate hikes, using jawboning and false hopes of aggressive quantitative easing to lift markets intermittently while credit and stocks trended steadily downward.[20] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described the resulting position as a pincer move — raise rates and destroy insolvent institutions, or pivot and cut and reignite inflation — concluding that time was running out on the current iteration of the US financial system.[20]

## Treasury cash rebuild and private credit warnings (2025)

The [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury]]'s July 2025 decision to rebuild its cash balance to $500 billion through heavy Treasury bill issuance operates through the [[treasury-general-account|Treasury General Account]] held at the Federal Reserve: buyers pay for bills from commercial bank accounts, the funds move into the TGA at the Fed, and bank reserves — electronic liquidity rather than physical cash — are correspondingly reduced.[21] Separately, the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston warned that the rapid growth of [[private-credit|private credit]] presents potential systemic risk to the U.S. financial system, noting that commercial and industrial loans reached $2.8 trillion in 2023 against $1.3 trillion in 2000 on an inflation-adjusted basis and asking whether direct private lending would displace banks as a key source of business credit.[21]

## September 2024 rate cut

On September 18, 2024, the Fed's [[federal-open-market-committee|Federal Open Market Committee]] cut the benchmark rate by 50 basis points to a target range of 4.75%–5.0%, the first cut in more than four years, in a move that surprised most surveyed economists and came less than two months before the presidential election with stocks and home prices at record highs.[22] Governor [[miki-bowman|Miki Bowman]] dissented in favor of a smaller cut, the first dissent by a governor since 2005.[22] Critics questioned the central bank's claim to being apolitical, and Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued the decision represented the "entirety of the Central Banking apparatus" being drawn into the election narrative.[22] See [[september-2024-fed-rate-cut|September 2024 Federal Reserve Rate Cut]].

## September 2024 emergency-scale rate cut

On Wednesday, September 18, 2024 the Federal Reserve cut its benchmark rate by 50 basis points, an unusually large move that markets read chaotically: stocks ended red while the dollar and yields ended higher, alongside what commentators described as uber-dovish dot-plot projections that Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] then pushed back against.[23] Powell said "the US economy is in a good place and our decision today is designed to keep it there," and, asked whether political factors entered Fed deliberations, replied simply: "We just don't do that."[23]

Critics noted the oddity of a crisis-scale cut delivered at record highs for stocks and home prices just two months before a presidential election, with the next meeting falling after voters had already cast ballots.[23] [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] called the decision "welcome news for Americans," and Democratic Congresswoman Maxine Waters of California said she was "pleased that the Fed has not only remained independent, but shown the importance of remaining independent as it followed the data and not politics."[23] Republican Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota countered that the Fed "could have waited to move rates until after the election to confirm" its independence, arguing that "25 basis points would've received minor grumblings, but the claim rings hollow with 50 basis points."[23]

Badlands commentary held that both Democratic politicians and the Fed were absurdly citing the cut as proof of political independence, and that an economy "held together by duct tape and popsicle sticks" was being sold as strong.[23] The authors suggested the cut was intended to seed economic disaster for an incoming [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration, noting that exactly 17 years earlier to the day the Fed had made the identical cut ahead of the 2008 financial crisis.[23] The move coincided with the [[business-roundtable|Business Roundtable]] CEO survey showing cooling hiring and sales expectations.[23]


## September 2024 rate cut and the end of an economic era

In September 2024, [[axios|Axios]] framed the Federal Reserve's imminent interest-rate cut as the end of an economic era, with economics journalists set to report the decision at 2 p.m. Wednesday from a windowless ground-floor room in Washington's Foggy Bottom neighborhood.[24] The account held that the defining economic feature of the preceding four years had been the highest inflation in modern times followed by the Fed's late but aggressive efforts to end it, a chapter closing that week.[24] What came next was less clear: the U.S. job market had shown mounting signs of weakness, with the jobless rate rising from a modern low of 3.4% the prior year to 4.2% in August 2024, softening that might prompt an extra-large half-percentage-point cut rather than the usual quarter-point move — a decision described as a coin flip.[24] Beyond the tactical choice, Axios judged that the era of very low mortgage and other rates that prevailed in the 2010s was not coming back.[24]

## December 2023 dot-plot pivot

At its December 13, 2023 meeting, the Federal Reserve signaled through its "dot plot" that it had pivoted from a pause to a rate-cutting trajectory for 2024, dovishly delivering what markets had already priced in and sending the Dow Jones Industrial Average to a record high.[25] Chair Jerome Powell made no attempt to walk back the resulting exuberance and said nothing about the massive easing of financial conditions whose earlier tightening had been the stated rationale for the September pause.[25] Critics noted that the projected easing cycle was set to unfold during a presidential election year, with ZeroHedge asking whether Powell had received a "'Biden-esque' tap on the shoulder"; while the Dow set records, the session was described as a bloodbath for hedge funds.[25]

## Tariff-driven policy bind (April 2025)

Following President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s April 2025 tariff announcements, a rally in U.S. government debt sent the two-year Treasury yield to its lowest level since 2022 as traders priced in a greater chance the Fed would move aggressively to cut interest rates and prevent the economy from stalling.[26] Analysts cautioned that this positioning discounted the inflationary side of the tariff shock: [[kathryn-kaminski|Kathryn Kaminski]] of [[alphasimplex-group|AlphaSimplex Group]] warned that investors fixated on slowing growth were ignoring Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]]'s remarks on tariff-driven inflation at their peril, and that the Fed would likely be pushed to cut on weak growth while inflation complicated that process.[26] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the central bank as caught in a pincer, reluctant to cut rates in a way that would paper over pain inflicted on the globalist system it represents, yet left with no choice.[26]

## Trump Pressure Campaign (April 2025)

In April 2025 the Fed became the central front of a public confrontation with [[donald-trump|President Trump]], who threatened to fire Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] well before the end of his term in May 2026.[28] Critics noted the Fed is an independent agency, but Trump had already dismissed officials at a series of nominally independent bodies, and commentary predicted the Supreme Court would sustain a Powell removal.[28] Markets that had ignored earlier firings responded sharply to Powell speculation: the dollar index fell to a three-year low, gold reached a record and [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] spiked after [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]] confirmed the administration was still exploring removal options.[28] Badlands commentator Burning Bright argued Trump had the Fed trapped in "game theory pincers," claimed the central bank had effectively been folded into a [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury]] now run by [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]], and framed the 2020 COVID-era money printing as a deliberately provoked acceleration of the fiat system's timeline that exposed the link between central banking and economic strife.[28] In this reading the US economy sat in an engineered stagflationary holding pattern in which raising rates would guarantee recession and cutting them would risk an inflationary boom, while Trump's tariffs, tax breaks and onshoring were intended to produce an offsetting deflationary cycle and a shift back toward sound-money valuation in gold, Bitcoin, or both.[28]

Attention turned to the Federal Reserve immediately after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[liberation-day-tariffs|"Liberation Day" tariffs]] triggered the worst one-day US equity declines since 2020 on April 3, 2025, with the March employment report and Chairman [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] both due the following morning.[29] Traders treated the payrolls release as a read on the health of the economy and on the outlook for monetary easing, amid fears that a full-blown trade war could trigger a global recession, fuel inflation, or produce stagflation.[29]

## Gold Standard Restoration Act (April 2023)

In April 2023, three Republican congressmen — [[alex-mooney|Alex Mooney]], [[andy-biggs|Andy Biggs]] and [[paul-gosar|Paul Gosar]] — introduced H.R. 2435, the [[gold-standard-restoration-act|Gold Standard Restoration Act]], which would give the Federal Reserve and the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|U.S. Treasury]] 24 months to publicly disclose all gold holdings and gold transactions, after which the Federal Reserve note "dollar" would be formally repegged to a fixed weight of gold at its then-market price.[30] Sponsors presented the measure as a response to the twin threats of inflation and bank failures, allowing the Federal Reserve note to regain stable footing for the first time in more than half a century.[30] Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that while a gold peg could be a step in the right direction, the deeper problem is a money system controlled by bankers and hidden actors rather than transparently by the people, and predicted that international bankers pursuing [[central-bank-digital-currency|Central Bank Digital Currencies]] would resist such a reversal.[30]

## January 2025 rate decision

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday, January 29, 2025, days after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] publicly called on the central bank to lower them, putting the Fed on a potential collision course with the president even as a longstanding norm of independence typically insulates it from direct political interference.[31] Speaking at a press conference in Washington, Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] declined to comment on Trump's call for lower rates, saying it would be "inappropriate" to respond, and said "the public should be confident that we'll continue to do our work as we always have," adding that the Fed would keep using its tools to achieve its goals.[31] Powell said he had had "no contact" with Trump since the president's call for lower rates the previous week.[31]

## Coordinated Dollar Swap Lines (March 2023)

On Sunday, March 19, 2023, at 5:00 p.m., the Federal Reserve announced "coordinated central bank action to enhance the provision of U.S. dollar liquidity," opening daily dollar swap lines with all major central banks in what ZeroHedge called a carbon-copy repeat of the Fed's panicked post-COVID crisis policy playbook.[32] The move followed the end of the Fed's tightening cycle "in disaster," with [[silicon-valley-bank|SVB]] the first bank to fail and the collapse of [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]] following days later.[32] ZeroHedge argued the Fed had "finally capitulated" as it had repeatedly warned it would, rendering moot the debate over whether the prior week's record surge in use of the Fed's discount window constituted quantitative easing, and predicted that once the swap lines reopened "the rest of the cavalry follows: rate cuts, QE ... etc."[32]

The same commentary warned that if the Fed did not contain the regional bank collapse there would be another great depression, noting that small and medium-sized banks account for 50 percent of U.S. commercial and industrial lending, 60 percent of residential real estate lending, 80 percent of commercial real estate lending, and 45 percent of consumer lending.[32]

## September 2025 rate cut expectations

In late July 2025 analysts placed the probability of a Federal Reserve rate cut at the September FOMC meeting at 64%, citing weakening demand and easing inflation from tariffs.[34] Political pressures — including the influence of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] — and a slowing labor market were seen as potentially accelerating policy easing despite mixed market signals.[34] Treasury markets remained skeptical, with the 30-year yield near 5%, in contrast to futures markets pricing in September cuts; the Fed's decision was described as hinging on real-time data, balancing inflation control against growth support amid economic uncertainty.[34] Badlands contributor [[canncon|CannCon]] used the item to fact-check a Fox News claim about inflation, noting that inflation had not been above 3% since Trump took office.[34]

## April 2024 reporting on Trump allies' plans to erode Fed independence

In April 2024 the Wall Street Journal reported that allies of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] were quietly drafting proposals to erode the Federal Reserve's independence if he won a second term, amid a deepening divide among his advisers over how aggressively to challenge the central bank's authority.[38] A small and highly secretive group produced a roughly ten-page policy document arguing that the president should be consulted on interest-rate decisions, that Fed regulations should be subject to White House review, and that the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] should be used more forcefully as a check on the central bank.[38] The group also contended that a returning Trump would have authority to remove [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] as chair before his four-year term ended in 2026, though Powell would likely remain on the board of governors.[38] Senior Trump advisers [[susie-wiles|Susie Wiles]] and [[chris-lacivita|Chris LaCivita]] cautioned that no staffing or policy announcement should be deemed official unless it came directly from Trump or an authorized campaign member.[38] Badlands Media read the report as establishment panic, arguing that the [[deep-state|Deep State]] fears both a presidential reassertion of authority over money issuance in the [[andrew-jackson|Jacksonian]] tradition and the loss of rate-setting control that lets it time recessions and the boom-bust cycle.[38] See [[trump-allies-plans-to-blunt-federal-reserve-independence]].

## Badlands framing after the Dudley reversal, July 2024

In July 2024 [[bill-dudley|Bill Dudley]], a former Goldman Sachs chief economist and former president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, reversed his earlier hawkish position and demanded an immediate rate cut — a flip-flop [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] treated as confirmation that the Fed is "nothing more than a political tool for a faceless, oligarch elite."[39] The reversal was measured against Dudley's August 2019 Bloomberg op-ed urging the Fed not to cushion [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s trade war and arguing that the 2020 election itself arguably fell within the central bank's purview, which critics cited as proof the institution works to perpetuate the status quo and the wealth imbalance it has cultivated since its founding in 1913.[39] Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued the significance lay in failure: the Fed under [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] ignored Dudley's 2019 advice and bent to Trump's bidding, and was ignoring the same voices again in 2024 — raising the question of who controls the central bank if the old establishment no longer does.[39]

## Money-supply contraction of 2022-2023

Analysis published by the [[mises-institute|Mises Institute]] and highlighted by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] in April 2023 reported that money supply growth had fallen again in February 2023, sinking further into negative territory after turning negative in November 2022 for the first time in twenty-eight years.[40] Growth had slowed rapidly since April 2021 from the unprecedented highs of the preceding two years, and the previous instance of negative year-over-year change had been November 1994, when contraction persisted for fifteen months before turning positive again in January 1996.[40] In February 2023 the year-over-year change stood at -6.6 percent, down from -5.0 percent in January and from +6.9 percent in February 2022; at no point in at least sixty years had the money supply fallen by more than 5.6 percent in any month.[40]

Badlands writer [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] characterized the contraction as "monetary warfare," likening the monetary system to a circulatory system that builds up favored sectors and starves others, and arguing that the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s expansion of currency creation in the mid-1990s had funneled credit into the emerging computer and big-tech industries up to the dot-com break.[40] In his reading, a shrinking money supply excises sectors deprived of new loans — evidenced by falling home prices, soaring credit card debt, rising consumer loan delinquencies, falling job openings and a weakening manufacturing outlook — displacing workers who then become available, and more willing to compromise on principle, for the industries oligarchs intend to launch under [[agenda-2030|Agenda 2030]].[40] His prescribed remedy was a [[parallel-economy|parallel economy]] in which citizens "become our own banks" and rediscover the law and the truth about money.[40]

## December 2023 dovish pivot

On Wednesday, December 13, 2023, the Federal Reserve held interest rates steady and signaled in new economic projections that the historic monetary tightening of the previous two years had ended and that lower borrowing costs were coming in 2024.[41] The message was more dovish than many investors expected: plunging Treasury yields helped the S&P 500 rise nearly 1.4% that day — the index's biggest gain on a Fed statement day since July 2022 — and the benchmark 10-year Treasury yield stood near 3.96% the following morning, its lowest level since late July.[41] "The Fed is done raising rates, and the market could not be more thrilled to have higher conviction in that," said Matthew Miskin, co-chief investment strategist at John Hancock Investment Management, though markets remained more dovish still than the central bank's own outlook.[41]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued the late-year rally defied inflationary and CPI data and that Chairman [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] had abruptly reversed hawkish signaling given just two weeks earlier.[41] The commentary relayed the view — attributed to maverick analysts including [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] — that Powell had received a phone call from the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration as it retreated from its "Bidenomics" messaging entering the 2024 election cycle, prompting 2025 rate cuts to be priced a year early; this was framed as "partisan politicking at best ... and election interference at worst."[41]

## March 2023 banking stress and the pause debate

After the failures of [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]] and [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]], investors looked to the Federal Reserve for relief and for a reversal within months of its policy of raising interest rates, the central bank having already joined the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury]] and the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] in shoring up the sector with special loans and guarantees for uninsured deposits at the failed banks.[42] [[bill-ackman|Bill Ackman]], who in 2022 had urged the Fed to hike "with shock and awe," reversed to call for a hiking pause, while hawks argued a pause would squander the Fed's remaining inflation-fighting credibility and invite the question of what policymakers knew that markets did not.[42]

## The $2.5 billion headquarters renovation controversy

In July 2025 the Federal Reserve's renovation of its Washington, D.C. headquarters became a flashpoint in the Trump administration's feud with the central bank over interest rates.[43] Chair Jerome Powell defended the $2.5 billion budget in a letter responding to a senior administration official who alleged mismanagement and potential legal violations, noting that neither headquarters building — constructed in the 1930s — had seen a comprehensive renovation since it was first built.[43] Powell said the Board had made a small number of design changes to scale back or eliminate certain elements while adding none, in order to simplify construction and limit further delays and cost increases.[43] The project's original 2017 price tag of $1.9 billion had grown to $2.5 billion by 2023.[43]

Badlands commentary treated the episode as emblematic of the institution itself, arguing that a $2.5 billion building was a massive expenditure for "an institution that really shouldn't exist," that Americans had lost their sense of scale for government spending, and that the Fed should not be housed in "a palace" while it "actively exploits and bankrupts the full faith and credit" of the country.[43] The same brief covered the House's passage of anti-central-bank-digital-currency legislation alongside the GENIUS and CLARITY crypto bills.[43]

## July 2023 rate hike to a 22-year high

On Wednesday, July 26, 2023, the Federal Reserve pushed interest rates to a 22-year high, one month after a brief respite in hikes during the central bank's race to bring down historic inflation.[44] The Fed raised its baseline interest rate range by 0.25 percent to a span of 5.25 to 5.5 percent, its 11th hike since March 2022 and a dizzying ascent from the near-zero rates in place at the start of 2022.[44] All 11 voting members of the [[federal-open-market-committee|Federal Open Market Committee]], the panel charged with managing monetary policy, supported the increase.[44]

Interest rates are among the sharpest tools available to the Fed for attacking high [[inflation|inflation]], which had finally begun falling after squeezing consumers for two years.[44] Inflation peaked at 9.1 percent annually in June 2022, a 40-year high that sparked fears of a recession, and price growth had since fallen to 3 percent year over year according to Department of Labor consumer price data.[44] Despite the improvement, Fed officials remained wary of pulling back on hikes before inflation approached the bank's 2 percent annual target.[44]

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] read the hike as an instrument of financial warfare rather than sound policy, arguing that the higher cost of money and credit would normally carry catastrophic consequences for individuals and business owners funding living expenses and working capital, but that insiders enjoy "special accommodation" — "woke and broke entities" that appear indifferent to profit while using well-funded enterprises to propagandize, absorb market share from small businesses, and, in his framing, erode freedom and prosperity.[44] He drew a parallel to the run-up to the 1913 passage of the Federal Reserve Act, when [[jp-morgan|J.P. Morgan]] and his associates were said to have spread rumors of a liquidity crisis that triggered a bank run and thereby produced the very crisis he had warned of, allowing Morgan and his allies to buy up large portions of the economy at rock-bottom prices and consolidate financial power.[44]

Deschamps contended that the modern playbook is identical — make money scarce, force people and businesses to sell assets, and buy the cheap goods — and that falling profits are an acceptable short-term price for insiders securing long-term position.[44] He further argued that control over the cost and availability of money confers the ability to fund businesses that advance a preferred agenda, speculating that recipients of such accommodations would prove to be DEI or [[esg|ESG]] "all-stars" working with globalist oligarchs, and closed by calling for a people-driven financial system and quoting Thomas Jefferson's maxim that "eternal vigilance is the price of liberty."[44]
