# Hilary Cass

Hilary Cass is a British pediatrician who spearheaded the independent review of youth gender medicine in the National Health Service known as the [[cass-review]].[1] The Dispatch described her as a respected clinician whose review explained that youth gender medicine "is an area of remarkably weak evidence," with study results exaggerated or misrepresented by people on all sides of the debate.[1]

Cass and her colleagues arrived at that conclusion after an ambitious yearslong effort to interview clinicians, parents, and patients about their experiences with the NHS youth gender medicine system.[1] She commissioned a sizable bundle of independent systematic reviews evaluating the evidence for puberty blockers and hormones as well as the quality of recommendations issued by influential groups such as the World Professional Association for Transgender Healthcare, with dozens of studies collected and evaluated by a team at the University of York.[1]

In the report Cass clarifies that her goal is not to question whether some young people are "really" transgender, acknowledging that some experience tremendous distress about their gender and that some may benefit from blockers or hormones.[1] Her central argument is that powerful medical treatments should be underpinned by quality evidence, and she stresses the need for proper screening and assessment before medical intervention, particularly for autistic youth and those with mental-health comorbidities.[1] Coverage in the [[badlands-news-brief]] treated her findings as a decisive blow to the gender-affirmative model of care.[1]

## April 2024 findings

Coverage of the review's April 2024 release described Cass as a British pediatrician whose landmark study defied the warning that gender-dysphoric adolescents who do not receive "gender-affirming care" will kill themselves.[2] Her research was reported as confirming that children presenting with sudden-onset gender dysphoria are actually suffering from other mental health diagnoses, and that they disproportionately have mental-health issues stemming from a difficult family situation or domestic abuse and are more likely to be neurodiverse or to have a co-morbid autism spectrum disorder.[2] Commentators treated the findings as a rebuttal to the claim that imposing medical gender-affirming care on mentally ill children is necessary and life-saving.[2]
