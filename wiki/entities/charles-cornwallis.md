# Charles Cornwallis

Lord Charles Cornwallis was the British general who assumed overall command of the [[british-empire|British]] southern campaign in the [[american-revolution|American Revolution]] following the fall of Charleston in 1780, and who became the principal strategic victim of [[francis-marion|Francis Marion]]'s guerrilla campaign in the South Carolina lowcountry.[1]

## Command in South Carolina

With Marion frustrating all British logistics throughout South Carolina, Cornwallis summoned [[banastre-tarleton|Banastre Tarleton]] and directed him against the partisan leader, an assignment Tarleton accepted confidently after six months of thrashing every senior American commander in his path.[1] Cornwallis simultaneously issued orders sharply increasing the severity of treatment of suspected patriot sympathizers: anyone found with firearms on their property was to have their home burned.[1] He was openly frustrated that he remained tied down in South Carolina when he had expected by then to be campaigning in North Carolina — a delay the article attributes entirely to Francis Marion.[1]

## Effects of the partisan war

Cornwallis's punitive policy contributed to the collapse of South Carolina into what the article calls the first American civil war, an "uncivilized hellscape of war and savagery" in which more than two hundred battles were fought — more than in any other colony, most of them after the British invasion of 1780.[1] Marion's dispersal of his small force across an enormous area caused Cornwallis to vastly overestimate the size and resources of the partisan bands, making the British commander markedly more conservative in his movements, particularly after Marion captured a wagon containing Cornwallis's personal writings and effects.[1]

Cornwallis also employed the conventions of parlay against Marion, on one occasion sending a young officer under white flag into the swamps to negotiate; the officer was blindfolded, escorted to the patriot camp, dined with Marion, and — according to the legend recorded in the sources — resigned his commission upon return.[1] By containing Cornwallis in South Carolina long enough for the French fleet to reinforce [[george-washington|George Washington]] in Virginia, Marion is credited in the article with shaping the campaign that ended at Yorktown.[1] When Cornwallis departed for Yorktown, he left [[francis-rawdon|Lord Francis Rawdon]] in charge of the British field army in the South.[1]
