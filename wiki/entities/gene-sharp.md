# Gene Sharp

Gene Sharp is described by Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] as the man who "wrote the playbook for 'nonviolent' regime change" — a figure few people recognize by name even though nearly everyone has witnessed the results of his life's work.[1]

## Reputation and theoretical work

Sharp is conventionally regarded as the preeminent scholar and practitioner of [[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]], earning him the nicknames "the Clausewitz of nonviolent warfare" and "the Machiavelli of nonviolence" for his detailed theories of civilian-based resistance and their strategic implementation.[1] He wrote extensively on the subject and developed the catalogue known as the 198 Methods of Nonviolent Action, a taxonomy employed by organizations including [[usaid|USAID]] and the [[united-states-institute-of-peace|United States Institute of Peace]].[1]

His how-to pamphlet on nonviolent revolution, *[[from-dictatorship-to-democracy|From Dictatorship to Democracy]]*, has been translated into more than thirty languages and is cited by protest movements worldwide.[1] Scholars of nonviolence and American activist training programs promoted his ideas extensively, and DeLarme states they have been implemented by nearly every significant protest movement of the past four decades.[1] His larger theoretical work, *The Politics of Nonviolent Action*, was later translated and repackaged abroad as an operational manual.[1]

## Institutional affiliations

Although Sharp is often billed as a people's champion, the article argues he is more accurately understood as one of the most significant American defense intellectuals of the Cold War.[1] It documents his links to the [[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]], the [[pentagon|Pentagon]], and Republican-aligned institutions such as the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]], the [[rand-corporation|RAND Corporation]], and the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]].[1]

In the mid-1960s the Nobel Prize–winning nuclear theorist [[thomas-schelling|Thomas Schelling]] recruited the 29-year-old Sharp to the [[weatherhead-center-for-international-affairs|Center for International Affairs]] at Harvard — an institution whose initials the article notes are also "CIA," and which was widely nicknamed "The CIA at Harvard" for its role as a bastion of the Cold War defense, intelligence and security establishment.[1] That center nurtured figures including [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]], future National Security Advisor McGeorge Bundy, and future CIA chief Robert Bowie.[1]

## The Albert Einstein Institution

Sharp founded the [[albert-einstein-institution|Albert Einstein Institution]] (AEI), an organization the article says has virtually no connection to Albert Einstein — a known pacifist — beyond Sharp's brief correspondence with Einstein in the final years of the physicist's life.[1] DeLarme highlights that Sharp launched AEI in 1984, the same year the National Endowment for Democracy was created to carry out operations formerly associated with the CIA, and asks whether the timing was coincidental.[1]

AEI, per the article, played a key role in training and deploying youth movements around the world to prepare conditions for coups: fostering the impression that targeted governments were deeply unpopular and destabilizing them through demonstrations.[1]

## NATO and Cold War strategy

In 1985 Sharp published *Making Europe Unconquerable: The Potential of Civilian-based Deterrence and Defense*, urging [[nato|NATO]] to adopt his methods of nonviolent action within its defense posture toward the Soviet Union and its satellites.[1] His strategy involved exploiting existing social fissures, promoting news and views hostile to the Soviet regime, translating guides on nonviolent struggle and distributing them among target populations, and providing financial support to opposition groups.[1] DeLarme presents the [[orange-revolution|Orange Revolution]], the 2014 Ukraine coup, and [[otpor|Otpor!]] in Yugoslavia as downstream products of this design.[1]

The article offers a historical analogy for readers of deep political history, casting Sharp's approach as a modernization of the strategy employed in the early 1800s by Giuseppe Mazzini, who organized the Young Italy movement that spawned imitators across the Western world — Young France, Young Germany, Young Poland, Young Switzerland, Young America, and, decades later, the original Young Turks.[1]

## The Arab Spring

The [[arab-spring|Arab Spring]] is characterized as one of Sharp's "greatest hits," with Sharp at the center of a wave of antigovernmental protests, uprisings and armed rebellions across the Arab world in the early 2010s, frequently cited as the quintessential demonstration of nonviolent action's usefulness.[1]

## Invocation in analysis of the 2025 campus protests

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] cited the "Gene Sharp color revolution playbook" — with the Arab Spring given as the reference case — in arguing that the pro-Palestine campus protests of 2024 and 2025 were not organic but a domestic application of established regime-change methodology.[2] He wrote that NGOs including [[jewish-voice-for-peace|Jewish Voice for Peace]], funded through tax-exempt vehicles such as [[george-soros|George Soros]]' [[open-society-foundations|Open Society Foundations]], the [[tides-foundation|Tides Foundation]] and the [[rockefeller-brothers-fund|Rockefeller Brothers Fund]], were building a "rent-a-riot mob" for future use rather than seeking to halt events in Palestine.[2]
