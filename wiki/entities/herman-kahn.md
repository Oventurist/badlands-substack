# Herman Kahn

Herman Kahn was an American military strategist, futurist and think-tank intellectual described by Badlands Media as one of the three men chiefly responsible for helping Klaus Schwab create the World Economic Forum.[1] Characterized in the article as "an atheist Jew from New Jersey," Kahn was associated with the [[hudson-institute|Hudson Institute]] and the [[rand-corporation|RAND Corporation]], where he authored reports on the practicality of nuclear deterrence that were eventually adopted as military policy.[1]

## Nuclear strategy

Kahn's seminal work, *On Thermonuclear War*, was published by Princeton and had a long-lasting impact on global politics, driving American establishment politicians to construct a foreign policy explicitly designed around worst-case thermonuclear scenarios.[1] Upon its release, the Israeli-American sociologist [[amitai-etzioni|Amitai Etzioni]] remarked that "Kahn does for nuclear arms what free-love advocates did for sex: he speaks candidly of acts about which others whisper behind closed doors."[1]

Kahn was popularly described as "the real-life Dr. Strangelove." Asked about the comparison, he told *Newsweek*, "Kubrick is a friend of mine. He told me Dr. Strangelove wasn't supposed to be me," though later students and researchers of Stanley Kubrick's work continued to note the similarities between the character and Kahn.[1]

## Europe, technocracy and the WEF

In a July 1966 essay for the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]] titled "Our Alternatives in Europe," Kahn argued that existing U.S. policy aimed at the political, economic and military integration of Western Europe, and that some form of federation of Europe — and of Europe with America — had come to be regarded as an intrinsically desirable goal indispensable to future world stability.[1] Badlands Media reads this statement as hinting at a unified American and European superstate.[1]

Kahn also promoted the marriage of long-term planning with technological forecasting, first exemplified in *The Year 2000: A Framework for Speculation on the Next Thirty-Three Years*, in which he attempted to predict the technological state of the world by century's end.[1] A companion document written simultaneously, *Ancillary Pilot Study for the Educational Policy Research Program: Final Report*, laid out how the future society envisaged in *The Year 2000* might be achieved.[1] Under a section titled "Special Educational Needs of Decision-Makers," the paper argued for explicitly educated decision-makers "better able, in effect, to plan the destiny of the nation," through "the creation of a shared set of concepts, shared language, shared analogies, shared references," and suggested that "universal re-teaching in the spirit of the humanistic tradition of Europe—at least for its comprehensive leadership group—might be useful in many ways."[1] Badlands Media interprets these passages as advocating the systematic subversion of democracy in favor of a small, similarly groomed leadership class deciding society's shared values — precisely, in the article's view, what the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] does today.[1]

Asked what he did at the Hudson Institute, Kahn replied: "We take God's view. The President's view. Big. Aerial. Global. Galactic. Ethereal. Spatial. Overall. Megalomania is the standard occupational hazard."[1]

By 1970 Kahn was traveling to Europe with [[john-kenneth-galbraith|John Kenneth Galbraith]] in support of Klaus Schwab's recruitment drive for the first European Management Symposium, and in 1971 he sat center stage for Galbraith's keynote speech at the historic first session of the organization that would become the World Economic Forum.[1]
