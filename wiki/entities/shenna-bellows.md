# Shenna Bellows

Shenna Bellows (rendered "Sheena Bellows" in some coverage) is the Secretary of State of [[maine|Maine]], and became nationally known at the end of 2023 as the official who unilaterally removed [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from Maine's 2024 presidential primary ballot under the disqualification clause of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]].[1]

## Removal of Trump from the Maine ballot

Bellows acted in her administrative capacity as Maine's chief election officer rather than pursuant to a criminal conviction or a contested judicial finding in her own state, a distinction that drove much of the criticism of her decision.[1] Commentary published by [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] and reproduced in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] characterized the removal as part of a broader pattern in which states such as [[colorado|Colorado]] and Maine could "change the political landscape of America forever if allowed to go unchallenged," on the theory that removing presidential candidates "based on unilateral opinion rather than any kind of legally arbitrated decision or criminal conviction is the most slippery of slopes."[1]

Critics argued that the most dangerous implication of the precedent was that "a handful of officials can decide for the entire population of their states (or the entire population of the country) what leaders they are allowed to vote for based on a 'guilty until proven innocent' ideology" — that officials need only make accusations of criminal behavior or criminal intent and then remove a candidate on the strength of those accusations alone.[1] The same commentary held that while the two-party system already filters candidates, state ballot removal was "a decidedly leftist/Democrat affair clearly engineered to benefit the progressive power structure," and warned that the mechanism was "not only about Donald Trump" because bureaucrats could later deny ballot access to any conservative candidate deemed a "threat to Democracy."[1]

## "Better leaders" remarks

Following her decision, Bellows publicly argued that "better leaders" must be placed in positions of power to prevent "election sabotage" and the end of democracy.[1] Commentators found this framing self-refuting, asking whether it is not "the job of the American public to decide what leaders are 'better leaders.'"[1]

## Appeal

Trump's campaign said it planned to appeal Bellows' decision immediately to the Maine Superior Court, the state's top trial court. Maine statute mandates that the court act quickly, with a decision expected by January 17, 2024 either affirming or overturning the decision — a date singled out for attention by Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]].[1]

## Relationship to the Colorado case

Badlands commentary argued that Bellows' action was made possible by the underlying findings in the Colorado litigation: the trial court's finding of fact that January 6 was an insurrection, upheld by the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]], "gave this Secretary of State in Maine all she needed to act unilaterally in barring 45 from her state's primary ballot."[1] On that reading, the Colorado case mattered not for ballot access — Trump remained on the Colorado primary ballot — but for the precedent-setting insurrection finding that officials elsewhere could then invoke.[1]

## Removal of Trump from the 2024 Maine primary ballot

On Thursday, December 28, 2023, Bellows, Maine's Democratic Secretary of State, removed former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the state's presidential primary ballot under the Constitution's insurrection clause, becoming the first election official to take such action unilaterally.[2] Her decision followed the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] ruling earlier that month in [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]], which barred Trump under Section 3 of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] and was stayed pending review by the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]].[2]

Bellows found that Trump's role in the January 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol violated Section 3, which bars from office those who "engaged in insurrection."[2] She acted after challenges from state residents, including a bipartisan group of former lawmakers.[2] The Trump campaign said it would appeal to Maine's state courts, and Bellows suspended her ruling pending that review; commentators expected the nation's highest court to have the final say.[2] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] contrasted Bellows's unilateral removal with Colorado Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]]'s same-day decision to keep Trump on the ballot, calling the Maine action "a great development" because it placed additional pressure on the Supreme Court to resolve the insurrection question.[2]

## Failed appeal and the collapse of the Maine case (January 2024)

In January 2024 Bellows lost a second attempt to force review of her disqualification decision before the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|United States Supreme Court]] heard argument on the issue on February 8.[3] Maine Superior Court Justice [[michaela-murphy|Michaela Murphy]] had deferred judgment in the case pending the Colorado argument; Bellows appealed, but the Maine Supreme Court declined to review the matter, noting that Murphy had only deferred and had not ruled, and holding that "because the appeal is not from a final judgment, we dismiss the appeal as interlocutory and not justiciable."[3]

Legal scholar [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]], who titled his commentary "The Maine Event That Wasn't," wrote that with other states such as Massachusetts also ruling against disqualification that week, Colorado would remain the outlier as the only state supreme court willing to embrace what he called a dangerous and anti-democratic theory.[3] He noted that advocates had been rejected in a dozen states while continuing to seek judges receptive to the novel argument, and that they "had Bellows at hello" given her prior public statements — including her declaration that "the Jan. 6 insurrection was an unlawful attempt to overthrow the results of a free and fair election… The insurrectionists failed, and democracy prevailed," and her continued denunciations of the "violent insurrection" a year after the riot.[3] Turley judged her decision "poorly written and conclusory in virtually every respect."[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the writing was on the wall for Colorado's Fourteenth Amendment theory, that the entire endeavor was about legitimizing the [[january-6-committee|J6 Committee]]'s insurrection narrative, and that the central question before the Supreme Court was whether January 6 was an insurrection at all.[3]

## Stay of the Maine ballot decision (January 2024)

After Bellows removed [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from Maine's presidential primary ballot, Trump's lawyers appealed in state court and then asked the court to pause proceedings pending the U.S. Supreme Court's ruling in the Colorado disqualification case.[4] On January 17, 2024, Superior Court Justice [[michaela-murphy|Michaela Murphy]] concluded she lacked authority to stay the judicial proceedings but remanded the matter to Bellows with instructions to await the Supreme Court's outcome before withdrawing, modifying or upholding her original decision.[4] The practical effect was that Trump remained on the Maine primary ballot ahead of the state's March 5 Super Tuesday vote and its ballot-printing deadline for uniformed and overseas voters.[4]
