# Thule Society

The Thule Society was a German occult order led by [[dietrich-eckart|Dietrich Eckart]] which, in Matthew Ehret's account, functioned as the religious core shared by the Nazi high command.[1] Ehret argues that the Nazis shut down Freemasonic lodges not out of hostility to occultism but because the SS-Thule-New Templar high command "only had room for one Satanic secret society managing Germany."[1]

## Doctrine

Thule was held to have been the magic centre of a vanished civilisation. Eckart and his associates believed that beings intermediate between man and other intelligent beings "from Beyond" would place at the disposal of the initiates a reservoir of forces enabling Germany to dominate the world, guided by "the Great Ones of the Ancient World" — a cosmology described by Louis Pauwels and Jacques Bergier in *The Morning of the Magicians* (1960).[1] Ehret characterises this doctrine of "The Secret Doctrine" and supernatural helpers as pure Theosophy adapted to the German psyche.[1]

Among the society's practices was belief in a mystical fluid called "Vril," a name drawn from *The Coming Race*, a novel by the Rosicrucian [[edward-bulwer-lytton|Edward Bulwer-Lytton]]; the [[vril-society|Vril Society]] of the early 1920s took its name directly from Bulwer-Lytton's "Vril-ya."[1] Members strove to practice the magical workings developed by Bulwer-Lytton's Rosicrucian followers and organized a network of witches said to channel demonic messages for their "Aryan Warlock handlers" amid sexual rites, orgies, sacrifices and drugs.[1]

## Membership and influence

Eckart's Thule Society was a major influence on the leading stratum of the Nazi high command that took power in 1933, including Alfred Rosenberg, Hermann Göring, Hitler's personal physician Dr. Theodor Morel, and [[heinrich-himmler|Heinrich Himmler]].[1] [[adolf-hitler|Adolf Hitler]] himself was an occultist initiated into "The Secret Doctrine" by Eckart.[1] [[wewelsburg-castle|Wewelsburg Castle]] was later described as "the magical centre of the new Thule."[1] Ehret notes that the Thule Society still exists, maintaining its own website and promotional videos.[1]

## Origins in the High Armanen Order

In Matthew Ehret's account of the Nazi religious program, the Thule Society grew out of the High Armanen Order, the elite occult body founded by the [[ariosophy|Ariosophist]] [[guido-von-list|Guido von List]], who served as its Grand Master.[2] Its leading members included Rudolf Hess, Hans Frank, Hermann Göring, Karl Haushofer, and Hitler's coach [[dietrich-eckart|Dietrich Eckart]].[2]

[[lanz-von-liebenfels|Lanz von Liebenfels]] and the Theosophical Aryan supremacist Franz Hartmann, who promoted a German branch of the New Templars, both associated closely with the society, which sought — like the covens of earlier times — to use long-haired priestesses in sacred orgies, séances, and soothsaying.[2] The thrust of the philosophy promoted by the Ariosophists and the Thule priesthood held that Christianity was a Jewish plot to shackle the pure spiritual culture of the Wotan/Viking spirit of the Teutonic warriors who inhabited the Black Forest under the Roman Empire, a culture supposedly managed by a druidic high priesthood using runes to interface with pagan deities.[2] The occult secret society shaping the Nazi high command organized a central headquarters at [[wewelsburg-castle|Wewelsburg Castle]].[2]

## Formation from the pre-war occult networks

Part 5 of the series traces the society's emergence to the dense period of occult organizing before World War I, when [[aleister-crowley|Aleister Crowley]] made contact with the leading Ariosophists led by Otto von List and Lanz von Liebenfels and with the German Theosophist leader Dr. Franz Hartmann.[3] The New Templar Order established by Liebenfels in 1907 worked closely with the Rosicrucian Pansophists led by Heinrich Traenker, and by 1918 the collaboration of these various occult networks gave rise to the Thule Society in 1919.[3] The society formed a central pagan religious hub and was soon led by [[dietrich-eckart|Dietrich Eckart]] and other German racists who would make up the vanguard of the Nazi high command.[3]

Shelley Klein, in The Most Evil Secret Societies in History, writes that the Thule-Gesellschaft met in secret and played a significant part in [[adolf-hitler|Hitler]]'s rise to power, fomenting support in the beer halls of Munich where Hitler first practised his rabble-rousing speeches, and represented an early expression of the Nazi fetish for "Aryan" tribes and northern lands, eliding an idea of natural purity with a belief in racial superiority.[3]
