# ActBlue

**ActBlue** is the dominant online small-dollar fundraising platform for Democratic candidates and progressive causes in the United States. It launched in 2004 as an effort to harness the power of grassroots small donors, and in the years since has processed the great majority of online Democratic contributions.[1]

## Congressional scrutiny

Congressional scrutiny of ActBlue and its Republican counterpart [[winred|WinRed]] intensified in June 2026 as House Republicans held a hearing examining allegations of fraud, foreign contributions, and weaknesses in donor-verification systems.[1] During the hearing, ActBlue chief executive [[regina-wallace-jones|Regina Wallace-Jones]] repeatedly invoked her Fifth Amendment rights when questioned by lawmakers.[1] Democrats on the panel argued that Republicans were using the proceeding to target Democratic fundraising operations, noting that similar concerns had been raised about WinRed, while Republicans said questions remained about suspicious donation patterns, identity verification, and the potential use of straw donors or foreign funds.[1]

The hearing did not produce new evidence of criminal wrongdoing by either platform, but it underscored that congressional investigations into online political fundraising were continuing and that donor-verification concerns would remain a subject of debate heading into the [[2026-midterm-elections|2026 midterm elections]].[1]

## The 2025 House Republican report

The June 2026 hearing followed an April 2025 report by House Republicans which stated that ActBlue had changed its fraud policy twice during the 2024 campaign, implementing "more lenient" standards that let foreign and other suspicious donor money flow into US campaigns.[1] The report explicitly described itself as an interim product, saying investigators were also seeking transcribed interviews with current ActBlue employees — including those responsible for the platform's fraud-prevention operation — and reviewing suspicious activity reports (SARs) of fraud on ActBlue.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing in the [[badlands-media|Badlands]] Brief, argued that the House Republicans had been telling the truth and that ActBlue was now being prosecuted, which in her reading explains why Wallace-Jones pleaded the Fifth.[1] She characterised Democratic use of "grassroots donors" as a meeting of the minds to engage in what she called a highly coordinated "portfolio of crime."[1]

Crediting investigator Peter Bernegger, the Brief listed federal statutes potentially implicated by the alleged conduct: 18 U.S.C. § 1956 (money laundering); § 1957 (monetary transactions in property derived from unlawful activity); § 1001 (false statements to federal agencies); 52 U.S.C. § 30122 (contributions in the name of another, under FECA); 18 U.S.C. § 1343 (wire fraud); § 1341 (mail fraud); § 371 (conspiracy to defraud the United States); § 1028 (identity theft and document fraud); § 1030 (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act); 15 U.S.C. §§ 6801–6809 (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act); and 31 U.S.C. § 5311 et seq. (Bank Secrecy Act, with implementing regulations at 12 CFR 21.11 and 12 CFR 21.21), plus state analogues applicable in the states, ostensibly for each transaction.[1]

The commentary also placed the practice in a bipartisan frame: Republicans "got in on the operation" in 2012 with the Mitt Romney campaign, while Barack Obama's PAC received what the Brief called the largest campaign-finance-violation fine in history for "primitive smurfing" in 2008, a penalty that did not come down until 2013.[1] Ashe in America rejected the defences that "this is just how campaign finance works now" and "both sides do it," concluding: "Prosecute every one of them."[1]

## April 2026 developments

On 20 April 2026 the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] issued a new report stating: "New reporting has confirmed our findings: ActBlue accepts illegal foreign donations en masse. ActBlue tried to cover this up by misleading and withholding documents from Congress. But it didn't work."[2] Badlands reported that the ActBlue employees the committee had sought to interview — including those responsible for the platform's fraud-prevention operation — invoked the Fifth Amendment to avoid self-incrimination 146 times.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] linked this to a longer history of [[smurfing|smurfing]] in Democratic small-donor fundraising, recalling her earlier comparison of relaxed fraud-prevention measures before the general election to "a Hunter Biden suspicious activity report – alarming and illegal but buried and forgotten."[2] She noted that ActBlue launched in 2004 as an endeavor to harness the power of grassroots small donors, and argued that if the campaign finance apparatus was set up to launder money "in the name of democracy," counsel were wise to advise silence.[2] She tied the matter to the "Grand RICO" theory encompassing the 2020 election, concluding that newly appointed prosecutor [[joseph-digenova|Joseph DiGenova]] "should be busy."[2]

## Possible convergence with the SPLC probe

In May 2026 Ashe in America speculated that Florida's civil probe into the Southern Poverty Law Center's fundraising and informant payments could crash into the ActBlue (and WinRed) investigations, framing both as parts of a single money-driven scheme domain.[3]


## Contempt threat over subpoena compliance (2026)

In June 2026, House Judiciary Chairman [[jim-jordan|Jim Jordan]], House Administration Chairman [[bryan-steil|Bryan Steil]], and House Oversight Chairman [[james-comer|James Comer]] sent a letter to ActBlue CEO [[regina-wallace-jones|Regina Wallace-Jones]] warning that the organization could face [[contempt-of-congress|contempt of Congress]] for failure to comply with subpoenas.[4] The subpoenas, issued July 22, 2025, requested documents on alleged misconduct at ActBlue, whistleblower retaliation, and mass legal team departures after the company ended voluntary cooperation; ActBlue said in October 2025 that it had produced all non-privileged responsive records.[4]

The committees pointed to an April 2, 2026 [[new-york-times|New York Times]] report citing documents they said were not produced, including former Interim General Counsel Aaron Ting's resignation letter and an internal message from former Legal Counsel Zain Ahmad alleging retaliation after reporting misconduct.[4] Lawmakers argued these fell within the subpoenas' scope and were not protected by attorney-client privilege, and alleged that the materials indicate potential foreign donations, misleading statements to Congress, and retaliation against a whistleblower — with ActBlue withholding them to conceal the extent and duration of the misconduct.[4] After a follow-up demand on April 14, 2026, lawmakers said ActBlue still did not comply.[4]

On June 5, 2026, ActBlue submitted a privilege log but continued withholding those records along with at least 420 additional documents, citing attorney-client privilege.[4] The committees said they had repeatedly sought justification for the claims without adequate explanation, arguing the lack of compliance was hindering Congress's ability to investigate possible foreign interference in political donations and to consider election security legislation.[4] Earlier that month, Wallace-Jones declined to answer questions before the [[committee-on-house-administration|House Administration Committee]] about allegations that the organization facilitated foreign campaign donations to Democratic candidates, invoking the Fifth Amendment.[4]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] went to prison for contempt of Congress despite ongoing litigation, arguing that enforcement "can be done," and wrote that the corruption of ActBlue and [[winred|WinRed]] money laundering has been documented for years.[4] She suggested the deliberate pace reflected a justice phase "dotting every 'i'," that Wallace-Jones's rights had been protected throughout and that she was set up to take the fall, and urged her to flip.[4]

## May 2025 refusal to testify

In May 2025, the chairmen of three House committees threatened to issue subpoenas after several current and former top officials of the Democratic online fundraising platform declined to testify in a probe into possible foreign and fraudulent political donations.[5] House Judiciary Chairman Jim Jordan of Ohio, House Oversight Chairman James Comer of Kentucky, and House Administration Chairman Bryan Steil of Wisconsin disclosed in letters that several witnesses had initially agreed to voluntary transcribed interviews, then pulled back through their lawyer earlier that month after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed an order instructing the [[justice-department|Justice Department]] to investigate the platform.[5]

## Public opinion on foreign and straw donors (July 2025)

A [[rasmussen-reports|Rasmussen Reports]] national telephone and online survey released in July 2025 found that 81% of likely U.S. voters suspect campaigns have used illegal foreign donors, and that voters overwhelmingly favor cracking down on illegal campaign contributions from foreigners.[6] The survey found 86% of likely voters believe it is important to enforce the federal law prohibiting foreigners from making campaign contributions to U.S. politicians, including 69% who called it very important, while 85% considered enforcement of the law against "straw donors" important, including 65% who said very important.[6]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the numbers refuted the claim that America is a bitterly divided 50/50 country in need of a "national divorce," since 81% of likely voters had come to terms with the proposition that campaign finance equals money laundering.[6] She predicted the figure would only increase as the ActBlue story developed, and raised the question of whether money laundering in service of a grand conspiracy constitutes an overt act within that conspiracy.[6]

## Ossoff smurfing allegations (February 2025)

In February 2025 election investigator [[peter-bernegger|Peter Bernegger]] posted on X that Senator Jon Ossoff had been "caught [[smurfing]]," saying a quick data run on his campaign "doesn't show all of the laundering."[7] According to Bernegger's records, 20 people made 7,380 contributions totalling $197,223 — figures drawn only from the top 20 donors and only via ActBlue.[7] Badlands writer Ashe in America argued each such contribution would constitute a campaign finance violation, falsifying business records, forgery, identity theft, and wire fraud, and connected the pattern to reporting that Democratic donations had dried up, noting that ActBlue launched in 2004 as an effort to harness grassroots small donors.[7]

## June 2025 congressional subpoenas

On June 25, 2025, ActBlue officials were summoned to testify before House Republicans about potential "widespread" fraud on the Democratic fundraising platform, according to subpoena letters obtained by the New York Post.[8] A current ActBlue senior workflow specialist, unidentified in the letters, and former vice president of customer service Alyssa Twomey received summonses from the GOP chairmen of the House Oversight, Judiciary, and Administration committees.[8] Both officials had been prepared to sit for voluntary interviews before President Donald Trump authorized Attorney General Pam Bondi to investigate "the unlawful use of online fundraising platforms to make 'straw' or 'dummy' contributions," opening a parallel Justice Department probe.[8] Danny Onorato, a lawyer for the ActBlue employees, responded by asking the committees to withdraw their requests for transcribed interviews until the Justice Department completes its investigation or clarifies its position regarding his clients.[8] Badlands commentary urged that the same scrutiny be applied to the Republican platform WinRed, citing investigator Peter Bernegger's claim that Senator Ted Cruz is the top national recipient of smurfed donations.[8]

## April 2025 straw donor memorandum

In April 2025 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] issued the [[2025-straw-donor-and-foreign-contributions-investigation-memorandum|Investigation into Unlawful "Straw Donor" and Foreign Contributions in American Elections]], directing the Attorney General, in consultation with the Secretary of the Treasury, to investigate the use of online fundraising platforms to make straw, dummy or foreign contributions and to report back through the Counsel to the President within 180 days.[9] The memorandum cited 52 U.S.C. 30121 and 30122 and pointed to press reports and congressional committee investigations suggesting platforms had been willing participants in laundering excessive and prohibited contributions.[9] Badlands contributor [[canncon|CannCon]] described the directive as "the ActBlue memorandum" and predicted that many additional fundraising platforms on both sides of the aisle — [[winred|WinRed]] among them — would be drawn into the same inquiry.[9]

## 2025 foreign money laundering subpoena

In July 2025 it was announced that ActBlue had been subpoenaed over ostensible foreign money laundering.[10] Reporting the development in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] highlighted that [[perkins-coie|Perkins Coie]] and later the [[elias-law-group|Elias Law Group]] had advised ActBlue on legal compliance, tying the platform's exposure to Democratic election attorney [[marc-elias|Marc Elias]], who the same week lost a federal challenge to [[wyoming-house-bill-156|Wyoming House Bill 156]].[10]
