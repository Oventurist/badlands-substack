# Justice Department

The Justice Department is the principal federal law-enforcement and prosecutorial arm of the United States government, headed by the Attorney General. In 2026 reporting it appears simultaneously as the driver of criminal investigations into former intelligence officials and as the target of congressional pressure over document disclosure.[1] It is also catalogued in this wiki as [[department-of-justice|the Department of Justice]].

## Florida probe of the 2016 Russia assessment

The Justice Department issued additional subpoenas in a criminal investigation in Florida examining the U.S. intelligence community's response to Russian election interference in 2016.[1] Persons familiar with the matter said the latest subpoenas sought documents from the years following the release of the January 2017 assessment that concluded Russia interfered in the presidential race in an effort to help [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] defeat [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]; those demands followed an earlier round of subpoenas in November that focused on the preparation of the assessment itself.[1] Former CIA director [[john-brennan|John Brennan]], a central figure in producing the report, was told he is a target of the investigation, and his attorneys wrote to the chief judge of the Southern District of Florida in December describing the probe as "manufactured" and complaining that the potential crimes under investigation remained unclear.[1] The department declined to comment on the case, one of several criminal inquiries opened into former officials viewed as Trump adversaries, and no charges had been announced.[1] The matter is treated at length at [[florida-criminal-probe-of-2016-russia-assessment|the Florida criminal probe of the 2016 Russia assessment]].

Commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the expansion of the subpoenas beyond the 2017 window indicated prosecutors were examining acts in furtherance of an ongoing conspiracy rather than merely the original assessment, and that officials who continued to advance a narrative they knew lacked grounding in fact or law could be criminally liable for sedition or, if foreign-incentivized, treason.[1]

## Epstein files disclosure fight

The department also faced sustained pressure to fully unredact and disclose [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] documents. Representative [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] confronted Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] during a tense [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] hearing, highlighting suspicious redactions in the roughly three million pages released under legislation he co-sponsored; the department quickly corrected those redactions after his objections.[1] [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] separately accused the Trump administration of slow-walking the files and attempting a cover-up, calling in a BBC interview from Berlin for complete public disclosure and open congressional testimony.[1] Commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that public anger over the files had been "masterfully redirected" away from the networks the documents implicate and toward the Trump administration and Trump DOJ, describing the maneuver as [[narrative-shielding|narrative shielding]].[1]

## Reported closure of the "weaponization" fund

In June 2026 the Trump administration was reported to be preparing to eliminate a Justice Department initiative known internally as the [[weaponization-fund|"weaponization" fund]], a program established to support investigations and legal actions tied to alleged political abuse by federal agencies during prior administrations.[2] According to the reporting, administration officials believed the effort had become politically burdensome and legally difficult to sustain as attention shifted toward broader institutional reforms and budget priorities.[2] The fund had been associated with inquiries into alleged misconduct involving federal law enforcement, intelligence agencies, censorship coordination, and politically motivated prosecutions, and critics inside and outside the administration reportedly argued that it created expectations of sweeping criminal accountability that proved difficult to deliver in court.[2] Supporters viewed the program as a symbolic commitment to confronting entrenched bureaucratic and prosecutorial abuse.[2] The reported move came amid wider restructuring across the department and renewed debate in Trump-aligned circles over whether systemic reform or high-profile prosecutorial action should be the administration's primary strategy.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] rejected the account, noting that it rested on two anonymous sources and calling it "statist wishful thinking," and predicted that pressure from figures such as [[michael-bennet|Michael Bennet]] and [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]] — who were pushing to tax weaponization disbursements at 100 percent — would instead prompt [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to double the fund.[2]

## First filing before the Alien Terrorist Removal Court (2026)

On July 15, 2026 the Department filed the first-ever application with the [[alien-terrorist-removal-court|Alien Terrorist Removal Court]], a five-judge panel created by [[congress|Congress]] in 1996 but never previously used, activating an expedited process to deport foreign nationals designated as alien terrorists or their immediate family members.[3] The filing was sealed and its cover sheet indicated the contents were classified.[3] Chief Judge Joan Ericksen held a sealed hearing that Thursday but ordered the Department to submit a more carefully considered application after raising questions about its target and how the alleged conduct connected to the laws authorizing removal, with an updated response due the following Wednesday.[3]

## April 2026 actions

In April 2026 the Justice Department received criminal referrals from the [[office-of-the-director-of-national-intelligence|Office of the Director of National Intelligence]] against both the whistleblower behind the complaint tied to President [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s 2019 impeachment and former Inspector General [[michael-atkinson|Michael Atkinson]], citing possible federal law violations tied to 2019 briefings before the [[house-permanent-select-committee-on-intelligence|House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence]].[4]

In the same week the department asked a federal appeals court to vacate seditious conspiracy convictions against leaders of the [[proud-boys|Proud Boys]] and [[oath-keepers|Oath Keepers]] tied to the January 6 Capitol riot and to dismiss the cases entirely.[4] The filings asked the US Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit to erase the convictions and permit prosecutors to permanently drop the indictments, including against Oath Keepers founder [[stewart-rhodes|Stewart Rhodes]].[4] The motion, signed by US Attorney [[jeanine-pirro|Jeanine Pirro]], stated that the move follows standard practice when the government determines dismissal is in the interest of justice.[4]

Separately, federal prosecutors obtained a superseding indictment against [[brian-cole-jr|Brian Cole Jr.]], the man accused of planting pipe bombs outside the [[republican-national-committee|RNC]] and [[democratic-national-committee|DNC]] headquarters on the eve of January 6, 2021, adding counts of attempting to use weapons of mass destruction and committing an act of terrorism while armed.[4]

## 2023 Google antitrust trial

The Department of Justice's antitrust case against [[google|Google]] over search engine dominance went to trial in September 2023 before U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta, the largest such action since the government's suit against [[microsoft|Microsoft]].[5] Commentary at [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] emphasised that the investigation was opened under [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s DOJ and carried forward under the Biden administration, a continuity treated as notable.[5] See [[united-states-v-google-antitrust-trial]].[5]


## Colorado actions of December 2025

In December 2025 the Justice Department opened a second front against Colorado's state government. It sued Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] on 11 December to compel production of detailed voter information without redactions.[6] Earlier that week the department announced an investigation into complaints about conditions in the [[colorado-department-of-corrections|Colorado Department of Corrections]], the agency holding former clerk [[tina-peters|Tina Peters]].[6] Badlands commentary linked both actions to the broader confrontation triggered by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s pardon of Peters and suggested they might open a lawful path to move her out of state custody.[6]

## Visa antitrust action and the Adams subpoenas (2024)

In September 2024 the department's antitrust division prepared to sue [[visa-inc|Visa Inc.]] in federal court, alleging that the operator of the largest US payments network had illegally monopolized the debit card market through exclusive agreements that hindered competing networks and efforts to block technology companies from entering the market.[7]

In the same period, the US Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York expanded its investigation into Turkish campaign donations to New York City Mayor [[eric-adams|Eric Adams]], with a July slate of subpoenas demanding all communications between the Adams administration and Israel, China, Qatar, South Korea and Uzbekistan.[7]

## Voter roll lawsuits (December 2025)

By mid-December 2025 the department's Civil Rights Division had filed 18 federal lawsuits against states and local governments that failed to produce voter registration lists when requested, part of the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s effort to enforce federal election laws.[8] New suits announced that month targeted Colorado, Hawaii, Massachusetts and Nevada, joining twelve states sued in September and on December 2, plus one locality — Fulton County, Georgia — for records related to the 2020 election.[8] Assistant Attorney General [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet K. Dhillon]] framed the campaign as a defense against vote dilution.[8] Details are covered at [[doj-voter-registration-list-lawsuits|DOJ voter registration list lawsuits]].[8]

## Strzok and Page Privacy Act settlements (2024)

In July 2024 the Justice Department agreed to pay a total of $2 million to settle lawsuits brought by former [[fbi|FBI]] officials [[peter-strzok|Peter Strzok]] and [[lisa-page|Lisa Page]] over the department's 2018 release to the media of text messages in which the pair criticized [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] during the 2016 campaign.[9] Strzok received $1.2 million and Page $800,000 under agreements obtained by [[cnn|CNN]] and finalized in court filings on Friday, July 26, 2024; the plaintiffs had alleged the disclosure violated the Privacy Act.[9] The department declined to comment, and Strzok retained separate pending claims over his termination during the Trump administration.[9]

## Gertler prosecution

Badlands commentary notes that the Biden-era Justice Department successfully prosecuted the case against Israeli oligarch [[dan-gertler|Dan Gertler]], stripping him of his African mining rights in 2022 after Treasury Secretary [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] reimposed sanctions that had been quietly reversed at the [[state-department|State Department]] in January 2021.[9]


## First RICO Indictments Against Tren de Aragua (2025)

The Justice Department announced on April 21, 2025 that it had obtained two New York indictments charging 27 people connected to [[tren-de-aragua|Tren de Aragua]] and the splinter group Anti-Tren with offenses ranging from robbery and drug trafficking to sex trafficking and murder.[10] The department said these were its first indictments against TdA under the [[racketeer-influenced-and-corrupt-organizations-act|RICO]] statute.[10]

## Refusal to share investigative material with House Judiciary (January 2023)

On Friday, January 20, 2023, the department signaled that it was unlikely to share information about ongoing criminal investigations with the new GOP-controlled House, a stance certain to frustrate Republicans in the chamber.[11] In a letter to House Judiciary Chairman [[jim-jordan|Jim Jordan]] responding to a flurry of document requests, the department said that "any oversight requests must be weighed against the department's interests in protecting the integrity of its work," and added that its mission to independently and impartially uphold the rule of law required it to maintain the integrity of its investigations, prosecutions and civil actions and to avoid even a perception that its efforts were influenced by anything but the law and the facts.[11] Badlands contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] characterised the refusal as shielding records concerning President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s retention of classified documents and as evidence of a two-tiered justice system compared with the treatment of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[11]

## Classified-documents prosecution of Trump (June 2023)

In June 2023, the Justice Department notified [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] that he was a criminal target in the classified-documents investigation and declined to delay the planned indictment in order to examine allegations of witness tampering submitted by his legal team.[12] The allegation held that a senior prosecutor had discussed a federal judgeship with a key witness's lawyer, a matter then pending in a sealed case before Chief Judge [[james-boasberg|James E. Boasberg]].[12] [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary argued that indicting a former head of state would set legal and psychological templates providing narrative shielding for future prosecutions.[12] Separate commentary in the same brief criticized the department for devoting less attention and funding to child exploitation networks than to January 6 prosecutions.[12]

## Comey and James dismissals (November 2025)

In November 2025 the cases the department had brought against former FBI director [[james-comey|James Comey]] and New York Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] were dismissed after U.S. District Judge [[cameron-mcgowan-currie|Cameron McGowan Currie]] ruled that the federal prosecutor handling them, [[lindsey-halligan|Lindsey Halligan]], had been illegally appointed by the department.[13] Currie's orders did not address the substance of the allegations, leaving open the possibility of re-indictment or appeal, and the administration announced on November 25, 2025 that the department would appeal.[13]
