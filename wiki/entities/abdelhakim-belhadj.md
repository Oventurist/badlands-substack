# Abdelhakim Belhadj

Abdelhakim Belhadj is named in the article as an example of the Al Qaeda elements that controlled Libya's newly formed government after Muammar Gaddafi was overthrown.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

The article states that with Gaddafi gone, Libya descended into chaos and [[benghazi|Benghazi]] became a safe haven for Al Qaeda training camps and a hub for funding and supplying jihadists fighting Americans in Iraq and Afghanistan.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty) It argues Gaddafi had in fact been fighting Al Qaeda within his own country, contrary to his portrayal by the U.S. government as a dictator killing his own people, and that his removal gave Al Qaeda free rein.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

Ambassador [[christopher-stevens|Christopher Stevens]] is described as the liaison between weapons dealers and this Al Qaeda-connected Libyan government associated with Belhadj — a relationship the article uses to argue that the same government whose approval [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] cited as a precondition for any rescue mission was one Washington itself had installed.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-dereliction-of-duty)

## Weapons transfers to Syria

According to "What Difference Does Benghazi Make?," Belhadj was more than the head of [[al-qaeda|Al Qaeda]]-aligned forces in Libya; after Gaddafi's fall he effectively controlled the new government.[2] In March 2011 [[christopher-stevens|Christopher Stevens]] became the official U.S. liaison to the al-Qaeda-linked Libyan opposition, working directly with Belhadj of the [[libyan-islamic-fighting-group|Libyan Islamic Fighting Group]], a group that had joined Al Qaeda in 2007 and some of whose fighters reportedly took part in the attack that killed Stevens.[2] Belhadj also led the February 17th Martyrs Brigade, named for the day the anti-Gaddafi uprisings began, which was later tasked with helping secure the Benghazi compound.[2]

In November 2011 The Telegraph reported that Belhadj, acting as head of the Tripoli Military Council, met Free Syrian Army leaders in Istanbul and on the [[turkey|Turkish]] border in an effort by the new Libyan government to supply money and weapons to the Syrian insurgency.[2] Joe Lange writes that Belhadj owned an airline used to move jihadists trained in Benghazi to Syria via Turkey, and that his wealth followed the release of $30 billion in Gaddafi-era assets after the [[national-transitional-council|National Transitional Council]] was recognized.[2] A Libyan ship carrying roughly 400 tons of weapons, including SA-7 surface-to-air missiles, docked in Turkey under a captain from Benghazi, leaving only Belhadj between Stevens and that shipment.[2]
