# Simon Wiesenthal Center

The Simon Wiesenthal Center (SWC) is a Jewish human rights organization named for the famous Nazi hunter Simon Wiesenthal.[1] In March 2020 the Center approached Senator [[sheldon-whitehouse|Sheldon Whitehouse]] and put him up to the task of opening a congressional investigation into whether [[credit-suisse|Credit Suisse]] had provided discreet banking services that helped Nazi SS officers flee Germany for [[argentina|Argentina]] after World War II.[1]

That request set in motion the inquiry that eventually produced the appointment of independent ombudsman [[neil-barofsky|Neil Barofsky]], his firing by the bank, his reinstatement by [[ubs|UBS]], and the disclosure of 890 previously undisclosed Nazi-linked accounts announced by Senator [[chuck-grassley|Chuck Grassley]].[1] Badlands commentary highlights the convergence of a Jewish human rights organization's initiative with unrelated cartel-finance investigations as evidence that the global banking system is being exposed along multiple tracks simultaneously.[1]

## Argentine ratline files (February 2025)

In February 2025, Argentine President [[javier-milei|Javier Milei]] promised officials of the Simon Wiesenthal Center his full cooperation in granting access to documents relating to the financing of the [[ratlines|"ratlines"]] that helped Nazis escape Europe after the Holocaust, a commitment made at the Casa Rosada presidential palace in Buenos Aires during a meeting with Milei and activists.[2] For decades the Center had sought records on the unofficial escape routes taken by up to 10,000 Nazis and other fascist war criminals who fled to Argentina and other countries after the Second World War.[2]

Rabbi Abraham Cooper, the Center's associate dean, told The Times of Israel that although some previous Argentine leaders had promised full cooperation, "Milei is the first to act with lightning speed to enable the SWC to uncover important pieces of the historic puzzle, especially as it related to involvement with Nazis before, during and after the Holocaust."[2]
