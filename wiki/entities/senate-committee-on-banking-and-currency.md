# Senate Committee on Banking and Currency

The U.S. Committee on Banking and Currency was the Senate body established in 1932 under the leadership of Senators [[peter-norbeck|Peter Norbeck]] of South Dakota and [[george-norris|George Norris]] of Nebraska to investigate the causes of the financial collapse.[1] It was created at a moment when the American economy was on life support: unemployment had reached 25 percent, over 40 percent of banks had gone bankrupt, a quarter of the population had lost their savings, over half of America's industrial capacity had shut down, thousands of farms had been foreclosed, and tent cities known as "Hoovervilles" had spread across the country.[1]

The committee was "a relatively impotent body when it began," but in April 1932 Norbeck brought in the New York lawyer [[ferdinand-pecora|Ferdinand Pecora]] as chief counsel.[1] Granted broad subpoena powers to audit banks and compel testimony, Pecora transformed the committee into the investigation remembered as the [[pecora-commission|Pecora Commission]], which extracted admissions of fraud from the heads of [[national-city-bank|National City Bank]], Kuhn Loeb, Chase Bank, [[brown-brothers-harriman|Brown Brothers Harriman]] and the House of Morgan.[1]

The committee's revelations supplied the political groundwork for [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]]'s first-hundred-days banking reforms, including [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]] separation and the creation of the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]].[1]
