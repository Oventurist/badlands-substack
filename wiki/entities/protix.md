# Protix

Protix is a Netherlands-based insect-protein manufacturer that produces so-called "bug food" at industrial scale.[1] The company manufactures roughly 14,000 metric tons of insect-derived food product per year, an output that its promotional framing ties to the goal of "fighting climate change."[1]

## World Economic Forum association

In 2015 Protix was selected as one of the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]]'s Technology Pioneers, a designation Badlands Media coverage treats as evidence that the company's growth is embedded in a broader globalist food-transition agenda rather than in ordinary consumer demand.[1] Badlands commentary notes that the WEF has publicly anticipated a future in which "we can all buy a bag of edible insects at our local grocery store," and that insect consumption is "heavily pushed by globalist governments."[1]

## Tyson Foods partnership

In October 2023, [[tyson-foods|Tyson Foods]] announced a partnership with Protix consisting of a cash investment in the Dutch firm together with an agreement to build a large insect-protein facility in the United States.[1] According to Tyson's statement, the planned facility would house an enclosed system supporting all aspects of insect protein production, including the breeding, incubating and hatching of insect larvae, with processed larvae destined for aquaculture and pet food ingredients and potentially for livestock and plant feed as well.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor Ashe in America framed the Tyson–Protix deal as a concrete instance of a phenomenon that mainstream outlets have dismissed as a conspiracy theory, pointing out that [[npr|NPR]] promoted insect eating in 2016, 2017 and 2018 before publishing a 2023 podcast episode characterizing right-wing criticism of bug consumption as racist and antisemitic.[1] The commentary argued that the existence of a collaborative network of governments, corporations, pseudo-governmental entities, academics, NGOs and charitable organizations pursuing a shift away from animal protein is documented rather than speculative.[1] Related regulatory movement cited in the same coverage includes European Union approval of cricket powder in major processed foods and the addition of cricket-based snacks to canteens at 1,000 Australian schools.[1]
