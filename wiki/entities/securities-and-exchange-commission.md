# Securities and Exchange Commission

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is the federal securities regulator, cited by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] as evidence that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] is a target rather than a beneficiary of entrenched power.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

## Role in the article

Carlson asks why the SEC took what he calls "the unprecedented step" of holding up the merger between DWAC and [[trump-media-and-technology-group|Trump Media]], keeping Trump from growing his media business and from injecting money into [[truth-social|Truth Social]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

The action is listed alongside the decline in Trump's net worth after becoming president and the mainstream media's uniform demonization of him, as part of an argument that if Trump were corrupt and part of the [[deep-state|Deep State]], he does not appear to benefit from that membership in any way whatsoever.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

## Proposed corporate governance rules (2025)

In November 2025 the SEC signaled it would propose corporate governance rules and clarifications aimed at what its leadership called the "weaponization of shareholder proposals."[2] Chair [[paul-atkins|Paul Atkins]] told Fox Business on November 14 that the commission would issue proposed rules and clarifications for proxy advisers, referring back to steps he had outlined in Delaware about a month earlier concerning corporate governance, shareholder proposals, and "the abuse of the corporate governance system... by politicized shareholder activists."[2] Atkins said proxy advisory companies "play a role" and that "the charges of conflicts of interest are really legion."[2]

His remarks, flagged by Bloomberg, came amid heightened scrutiny of proxy advisers, including a reported [[federal-trade-commission|Federal Trade Commission]] investigation into whether two such firms violated federal antitrust laws by advising shareholders how to vote on politically charged topics for ideological reasons.[2]

## JPMorgan Chase email deletion case (2023)

In June 2023 the SEC fined [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]] $4 million after about 47 million emails belonging to the bank's retail banking group were mistakenly and permanently deleted.[3] The records covered January 1 to April 23, 2018, were erased in June 2019 from roughly 8,700 mailboxes, and included business records the bank was required under SEC rules to preserve for three years.[3] A cease-and-desist order attributed the loss to an outside vendor that failed to apply the three-year retention setting after JPMorgan's compliance technology department sought help deleting much older communications.[3] The SEC stated that JPMorgan had been unable in at least twelve civil securities-related regulatory probes to comply with subpoenas and document requests for the deleted communications.[3]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the penalty a token sanction against a bank managing more than $3 trillion in assets and argued that JPMorgan chief executive [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] effectively shapes the investigative priorities of the SEC and the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]].[3]

## Congressional repeal of SAB 121 (2024)

In May 2024 both houses of Congress passed a resolution condemning the SEC's [[staff-accounting-bulletin-121|Staff Accounting Bulletin 121]], the rule governing how cryptocurrency assets are accounted for and which makes it very expensive for financial institutions to participate in the crypto custody business.[4] The [[us-senate|Senate]] vote was 60-38, following earlier passage in the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]]; the [[white-house|White House]] indicated it would veto any such legislation.[4]

The episode marked the crypto industry's successful extension of its fight against chairman [[gary-gensler|Gary Gensler]]'s SEC from the courts onto the legislature.[4] The agency had been feeling more confident after a procedural win on most points in its case against [[coinbase|Coinbase]], the country's largest cryptocurrency exchange, and on key points in its case against Terraform Labs — results that followed a string of major losses, including on the characterization of the cryptocurrency XRP and the approval of a spot bitcoin ETF.[4] Asked for comment on the vote, SEC staff sent Axios a description of SAB 121 without commenting on the legislative action.[4]

## Biden veto threat over SAB 121 (May 2024)

In May 2024 the [[white-house|White House]] announced that President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] would veto H.J.Res. 109, a joint resolution that would have overturned the commission's [[staff-accounting-bulletin-121|Staff Accounting Bulletin No. 121]], which requires banks to keep customers' digital assets on their balance sheets with capital maintained against them.[5] The administration said it "strongly opposes" the effort, arguing that the resolution would disrupt the SEC's work "to protect investors in crypto-asset markets and to safeguard the broader financial system."[5] It further contended that SAB 121 had been issued in response to demonstrated technological, legal and regulatory risks causing substantial consumer losses, and that invoking the Congressional Review Act could inappropriately constrain the agency's ability to establish guardrails and address future crypto-asset issues including financial stability.[5]

## Musk consent decree litigation (2024)

The SEC's consent decree with [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] arose from his 2018 statement on Twitter that he had "funding secured" to take [[tesla-inc|Tesla]] private, which the agency said defrauded investors.[6] The settlement required Musk and Tesla each to pay $20 million in fines, required Musk to relinquish the Tesla chairmanship, and obliged him to obtain approval from a Tesla lawyer for certain posts.[6] After a lower court upheld the decree, Musk petitioned the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] in December 2023, and in March 2024 the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration urged the justices to decline the case.[6] Musk described the decree as a "muzzle" on his free speech rights; critics argued the pretext of guarding markets from his speech was undermined by the conduct of Congress, which writes the agency's governing statutes.[6]


## Quarterly reporting proposal (September 2025)

On 15 September 2025 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] proposed that U.S.-based companies report financial results every six months rather than every three, a change that would require approval from the Securities and Exchange Commission.[7] The agency first required [[quarterly-earnings-reporting|quarterly reporting]] for U.S. public companies in 1970.[7] Trump argued on [[truth-social|Truth Social]] that the shift "will save money, and allow managers to focus on properly running their companies," and ending the mandate would bring the United States closer to the United Kingdom and the [[european-union|European Union]], where semiannual reporting is required and quarterly reporting optional.[7]

## June 2024 Supreme Court jury-trial ruling

On Thursday, June 27, 2024, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruled in a decision described by Axios as potentially groundbreaking that defendants accused of fraud by the SEC have the right to a jury trial, curtailing the agency's use of in-house administrative proceedings.[8] The ruling came a day before the Court overturned [[chevron-deference|Chevron deference]], and Badlands contributor Burning Bright cast the pair of decisions as the administrative state being "neutered to close out the week."[8]

## Trump's 2024 pledge on SEC leadership

At the [[bitcoin-2024-nashville-conference|Bitcoin 2024 conference]] in Nashville on July 27, 2024, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] pledged to remove SEC chair [[gary-gensler|Gary Gensler]] if elected and to implement a crypto advisory council, telling attendees that under his administration "we will have regulations, but from now on the rules will be written by people who love your industry, not hate your industry."[9] The remarks were widely read as a repudiation of the commission's enforcement-driven posture toward digital assets.[9]

## March 2024 Coinbase ruling

On 27 March 2024 the SEC scored a major win in its lawsuit against the cryptocurrency exchange [[coinbase|Coinbase]] when U.S. District Judge [[katherine-polk-failla|Katherine Polk Failla]] ruled that its claim that the exchange engaged in unregistered sales of securities could be heard by a jury at trial.[10] The agency had filed suit in June 2023, alleging Coinbase acted as an unregistered broker and exchange and asking that it be "permanently restrained and enjoined" from doing so; the court found the SEC had adequately alleged that Coinbase's Staking Program involved the unregistered offer and sale of securities, while dismissing the unregistered-broker claim concerning the Wallet application.[10] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that although the SEC is rightly seen by much of the alternative media as part of the Deep Administrative State, more was going on than met the eye, noting that the agency treats bitcoin as a free-market commodity rather than a security — implying the US government considers all other digital currencies to be securities — and asking whether, alongside [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s pledges to block CBDCs, the banks might eventually be undone by their own securities laws.[10]
