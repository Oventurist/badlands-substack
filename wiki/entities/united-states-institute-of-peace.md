# United States Institute of Peace

The **United States Institute of Peace** (USIP) is a federally funded U.S. institution working on conflict prevention and resolution abroad.[1]

In early 2025 the institute became one of the targets of the [[doge|Department of Government Efficiency]] under [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]; DOGE attempted to dismantle the agency as part of a broader campaign of budget cuts and federal workforce reductions.[1] Protesters in the [[tesla-takedown|#TeslaTakedown]] movement cited the attempt to dismantle USIP — alongside a 20% staffing cut at the [[internal-revenue-service|Internal Revenue Service]] — as evidence of DOGE's damage to public health, scientific research, and foreign aid.[1]

## Commentary on the Taliban opium ban

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] characterises USIP as one of the controlled institutions of "NGO world" and criticises an article published by the institute, authored by William Byrd, which argued that the opium ban imposed by the [[taliban|Taliban]] after it reclaimed power in [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] was harmful.[2] The original article now leads to a dead page and must be retrieved via the Wayback Machine, though it remains preserved on other websites.[2]

DeLarme notes that the institute receives roughly $56 million in taxpayer funding annually and sits directly across from the [[state-department|State Department]] in Washington, D.C., and invites readers to draw their own conclusions about who really runs it given the CIA/[[pentagon|Pentagon]]/State Department nexus that he argues produces cut-out NGOs.[2] He observes that Afghan poppy fields account for some 90 percent of the world's opium supply, so an NGO of this kind warning that the ban would have "negative economic consequences" is telling — while conceding there is some truth to the assessment, since narcotics are, depending on the source, the third biggest global commodity behind crude oil and natural gas.[2]

## Mandate creep and the nonviolent action portfolio

USIP was created by the United States Institute of Peace Act of 1984 with a narrow mandate: to conduct research on peace and to educate diplomats, soldiers, and others in best practices for avoiding or resolving violent conflict.[3] Badlands Media argues that its remit has broadened so far that the institute's current portfolio scarcely reflects its lawful purposes, growing from a modest $4 million research and education institute into a sprawling $55 million conglomerate with programs in almost half the countries in the world.[3] Its budget rose from $39 million in fiscal year 2017 to $55 million in FY 2024, and the article notes that how that money is spent is not clear — the 2024 Congressional Budget Justification contains only vague language about "priorities," with no breakdown by purpose or activity.[3]

Unlike nonprofits that must publish annual reports and file a public Form 990, or formal agencies that must publish detailed budgets, USIP's budget is described as opaque to both Congress and the taxpayers funding it.[3] The article cites a Heritage Foundation finding that 98% of individual USIP employee political donations from 2019 to 2022 went to Democratic candidates and causes, arguing this bias is recent: prior to establishment Republicans shifting toward establishment Democrats in the Trump era, USIP was equally cozy with the Republican establishment.[3]

Like [[usaid|USAID]], USIP maintains networks of people on the ground in every country where it has established relations; its emissaries liaise with tribal leaders, judges, politicians, and journalists as part of "capacity building" local networks that can serve foreign-policy establishment objectives.[3] Beyond electioneering and media control, the institute is said to specialize in engineering civil unrest under the label "[[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]]," a term appearing frequently in USIP white papers — a compendium of 678 of which is archived on JSTOR.[3] [[maria-stephan|Maria Stephan]] directed the institute's Nonviolent Action Program from 2015 to 2021.[3]

## Governance and the Howell ruling

USIP is governed by a bipartisan board of fifteen directors that must include the Secretary of Defense, the Secretary of State, and the president of the [[national-defense-university|National Defense University]]; the remaining twelve are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate.[3] Badlands calls the notion that USIP is separate from government — and thus exempt from executive control, the main thrust of Judge [[beryl-howell|Beryl Howell]]'s May 2025 ruling blocking the Trump takeover — preposterous, since all funding for its programs and salaries must come from the federal government.[3] The article situates USIP within the wider [[plausible-deniability-network|plausible deniability network]] operating between the State Department, the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]], and the Pentagon.[3]

## Role in nonviolent-action operations

The 2025 article "How to Rent a Riot - The History of 'Nonviolence'" lists USIP alongside [[usaid|USAID]] as an organization that employs [[gene-sharp|Gene Sharp]]'s 198 Methods of [[nonviolent-action|Nonviolent Action]].[4] Its link to the Serbian movement [[otpor|Otpor!]] ran through [[daniel-serwer|Daniel Serwer]], director of the institute's Balkans Initiative, who came, in the article's phrase, "right out of the CIA/DoD civil society pipeline."[4] Contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] counts USIP among the major nodes of the NATO/CIA/Pentagon NGO proxy network whose fingerprints are on the campaign against [[slobodan-milosevic|Slobodan Milosevic]], and closes by urging the public to demand transparency from USIP and the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]].[4]

## Origins in the Reagan-era NGO layer

The August 2025 Badlands article "Killing the Peace" describes USIP as one of a litany of 501(c)(3) "trojan horses" employed by the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and the Department of Defense to conduct plausibly deniable operations in conflict zones, and notes that its stated purpose is "preventing, mitigating, and resolving violent conflict abroad."[5] The article argues that, in practice, "peace" for USIP means furthering the objectives of the international ruling class regardless of who suffers, and that the institute generates more conflict globally than it resolves.[5] It places USIP inside a network that also includes [[usaid|USAID]], the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]], the [[global-engagement-center|Global Engagement Center]], the [[national-democratic-institute|National Democratic Institute]], the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]], the [[solidarity-center|Solidarity Center]] and the [[center-for-international-private-enterprise|Center for International Private Enterprise]].[5]

DeLarme frames the lineage as a set of Matryoshka dolls: the OSS/CIA served as a plausible-deniability layer for the [[state-department|State Department]], USAID became a deniability layer for the CIA after the exposures and reforms of the 1960s and 1970s, the NED became one for USAID in the 1980s, and USIP in turn became one for the NED.[5] USIP was created in 1984 with what the article gives as a $55 million annual budget, as part of the Reagan reforms that sought to restore CIA capabilities after the [[church-committee|Church Committee]] revelations of [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]] and MKUltra and Jimmy Carter's 1977 restructuring that curtailed covert operations; CIA director [[william-casey|William Casey]] and the administration won bipartisan support to carry on that work at the NGO layer.[5] The article stresses that these "non-governmental" organizations were created by an act of Congress, require an act of Congress to dissolve, are staffed by government officials and are funded by the government.[5]

## Afghanistan, opium and the drug-funded covert economy

"Killing the Peace" highlights USIP's work in [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]], where it conducted "conflict resolution" activity with [[taliban|Taliban]] tribes and Afghan Special Forces while publishing analysis urging that the flow of some 95% of the world's heroin supply continue — described by DeLarme as the closest thing to a written confession that U.S. intelligence runs the global drug trade.[5] The Taliban had also attempted to halt opium production in 2000, when Afghanistan produced roughly 70% of global opium output; after the U.S. occupation began, output rose to about 90%.[5] The article traces the funding of the Mujahedeen-era proxy force to the drug trade and links the resulting black-market economy to the collapse of the CIA-linked [[bank-of-credit-and-commerce-international|Bank of Credit and Commerce International]], arguing that USIP's messaging function supplied a palatable public rationale for continued opium production once the Cold War ended.[5]

## Color revolutions and domestic unrest

The article identifies the coordination of "non-violent resistance" as USIP's primary function, calling it a key player in orchestrating [[color-revolution|color revolutions]] — the deliberate promotion of civil unrest and destabilization abroad to advance U.S. geopolitical interests.[5] It claims USIP's fingerprints appear in politically motivated mass unrest inside the United States as well, from the [[2020-george-floyd-riots|George Floyd riots]] of 2020 to the [[no-kings-protests|No Kings protests]].[5] Other described functions include "justice reform initiatives" used to exert influence over foreign judicial systems under the cover of "strengthening legal institutions," and propaganda, messaging and damage control.[5]

## The 2025 takeover

On February 19, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] issued [[executive-order-14217|Executive Order 14217]], which targeted USIP and other entities for reduction to their "statutory minimums" as "unnecessary" components of the federal government.[5] The administration fired 11 of USIP's 15 board members without stated legal or factual justification, in apparent violation of the USIP Act's for-cause removal provisions, and dismissed acting president and CEO [[george-moose|George Moose]]; the post passed to a [[doge|DOGE]] staffer before [[darren-beattie|Darren Beattie]] assumed the role.[5] On March 17, 2025, DOGE personnel backed by [[fbi|FBI]] agents, D.C. Metropolitan Police and USIP's own private security — whose contract had been canceled — forcibly entered the institute's Washington headquarters after unsuccessful earlier attempts, amid reports of vandalism including removal of the institute's signage.[5] Leadership and staff called it an illegal takeover, asserting that USIP is a private nonprofit outside executive-branch control.[5]

Judge [[beryl-howell|Beryl Howell]] ruled in May 2025 that the board firings, leadership replacements, staff terminations and asset transfers were illegal and "null and void," reinstating the board and Moose; in late June the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit stayed her ruling, holding that USIP exercises "substantial executive power" and that the president likely has at-will removal authority over its board.[5] The shake-up left most staff fired, programs halted and the headquarters repeatedly changing hands, with the institute's future in limbo pending appeal.[5] DeLarme calls the fight one of the most consequential flashpoints between populism and globalism in Trump's second term, comparable to the dissolution of the Global Engagement Center and the termination of contractors tied to the [[counter-foreign-information-manipulation-and-interference-hub|R/FIMI hub]], and argues a successful outcome would supply a template for seizing other nodes of the network.[5] He further calls for a "USIP Files" data dump — complete declassification of internal documents, chats and emails in the mold of the [[twitter-files|Twitter Files]] — noting the precedent of the JFK, RFK and MLK files and the [[durham-annex|Durham Annex]], and the appointment of Rep. [[anna-paulina-luna|Anna Paulina Luna]] to head the [[task-force-on-the-declassification-of-federal-secrets|Task Force on the Declassification of Federal Secrets]].[5]
