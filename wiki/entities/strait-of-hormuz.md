# Strait of Hormuz

The **Strait of Hormuz** is the narrow waterway linking the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the open sea, and a chokepoint for a large share of global seaborne energy traffic.[1]

## 2026 conflict and blockade

During the US–[[iran|Iran]] confrontation of mid-2026 the strait was a principal theatre of clashes.[1] US forces carried out strikes on Iranian targets and enforced a naval blockade on Iranian shipping, while Iran launched retaliatory missile and drone attacks and threatened broader disruption of regional energy flows.[1] Both sides accused the other of repeatedly violating ceasefire arrangements.[1]

## Role in the proposed accord

Under the draft memorandum of understanding reported in June 2026 and known as the [[islamabad-agreement|Islamabad agreement]], the Strait of Hormuz would be reopened without tolls, alongside sanctions relief tied to Iranian compliance and a 60-day extension of the ceasefire.[1]

## Shutdown and market effects (April 2026)

[[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] declared that the United States had "total control" over Hormuz and that it would remain "sealed up tight" until a deal was struck.[2] A US assessment estimated that it would take six months to clear Iranian mines from the strait if that became necessary.[2] The shutdown disrupted one-fifth of global oil and LNG flows and pushed Brent crude to $103 per barrel, with analysts warning of potential spikes above $120 if the stalemate persisted beyond the following week or two.[2] Tehran claimed it had begun collecting tolls from passing vessels, with the first revenue transferred to its Central Bank, and refused to resume negotiations while the US blockade remained in place.[2]

Badlands commentary argued that the disruptions rippled outward to hammer importers across Europe, Asia, and Australia while the United States, buoyed by domestic energy abundance, absorbed the shock and redirected capital inward — an inversion in which the chokepoint squeezed the [[invisible-enemy|Invisible Enemy]] rather than Iran.[2]

## Energy and currency resets (March 2026)

US Energy Information Administration data cited by Badlands put the strait's throughput at roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption, making it one of the world's most critical energy transit routes.[3] Supply-disruption concerns tied to Hormuz drove [[india|India's]] refiners to book approximately 60 million barrels of [[russia|Russian]] crude for the following month, more than double February levels.[3] Those refiners increasingly settled trades in non-dollar currencies — UAE dirhams and Chinese yuan — converting rupees as a workaround, and analysts said the crisis could accelerate a shift away from dollar-denominated oil trade toward alternative settlement systems.[3]

## March 2026 disruption and the NATO ultimatum

Following U.S. strikes on [[kharg-island|Kharg Island]], [[donald-trump|President Trump]] warned that broader strikes could follow if [[iran|Iran]] interfered with shipping in the strait, and the escalating conflict disrupted maritime traffic through the waterway, which carries roughly one-fifth of the world's oil supply.[4] In an interview with the Financial Times, Trump said [[nato|NATO]] faced a "very bad" future if allies did not assist the United States in reopening the strait, calling on Gulf-oil-dependent countries including China, the United Kingdom, France, Japan and South Korea to contribute minesweepers and air defenses.[4]

Badlands analysis read the demand as a pincer: Europe must either send ships and risk entanglement with Iran and the loss of Persian Gulf LNG, or refuse the demands of its only remaining reliable energy supplier.[4] The episode is treated as a central front of the wider [[global-energy-war|global energy war]].[4]

## 2026 closure threat

In a February 2026 RT interview, former [[islamic-revolutionary-guard-corps|IRGC]] commander [[hussein-kanani|Hussein Kanani]] warned that [[iran|Iran]] could respond to U.S. aggression by closing the Strait of Hormuz as a pressure tactic, potentially disrupting global oil flows including shipments to China and Russia.[5] The warning accompanied his claim that [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] possesses a nuclear bomb, and came amid U.S. deployments led by the USS Abraham Lincoln carrier group.[5]

## 2026 tanker guarantees

During the [[iran-war|U.S.–Iran war]] of March 2026, Iranian attacks and threats to close the strait triggered war-risk insurance policies that froze tanker traffic, sent supertanker rates to all-time highs, and spiked oil volatility.[6] [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] responded by announcing that the United States would immediately provide political risk insurance and financial guarantees at "very reasonable" prices for all maritime trade — especially energy — transiting the Persian Gulf, delivered through the [[development-finance-corporation|Development Finance Corporation]], and added that the U.S. Navy would escort tankers through the strait "if necessary."[6] Oil prices tumbled on the announcement as fears of major supply disruption eased.[6]

## Project Freedom (May 2026)

In May 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced a military operation dubbed [[project-freedom|Project Freedom]] aimed at reopening the strait and escorting commercial vessels through the shipping lane as clashes with Iranian forces escalated.[7] US naval and air assets engaged Iranian drones and small boats near the strait, destroying multiple vessels while attempting to secure safe passage for commercial shipping through a waterway that handles a significant share of global oil traffic.[7] Iranian state-linked sources claimed their forces struck a US warship, an assertion the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] rejected, stating no American vessels were hit and that US forces repelled the attacks.[7] Some commercial ships were reportedly damaged or targeted during the exchange while others transited under US protection.[7]


## 2026 ceasefire strains
Renewed military exchanges in the Strait of Hormuz challenged the recently established US-[[iran|Iran]] ceasefire in June 2026, forming the backdrop to the contradictory announcements about talks in Doha, [[qatar|Qatar]].[8] Both sides continued diplomatic efforts aimed at preventing further escalation despite the exchanges.[8]

## Shipping easement in the May 2026 draft deal

In late May 2026 the United States and [[iran|Iran]] were reported to have reached a tentative agreement to extend the existing ceasefire by another 60 days and ease shipping restrictions through the Strait of Hormuz.[9] Under the reported terms the United States would lift its blockade of Iranian ports and some sanctions on Iranian oil sales in exchange for unrestricted shipping through the strategic waterway.[9] The agreement remained pending approval from President [[donald-trump|Trump]] and Iranian leadership, and Iranian state media reported that the text had not been finalized or confirmed.[9] Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] said the sides were "not there yet, but we're very close," while a U.S. official described concurrent American strikes as defensive measures to preserve the ceasefire.[9]

## Allied refusal of a security coalition (March 2026)

In March 2026 [[nato|NATO]] allies flatly refused President Trump's calls for an international coalition to secure the Strait of Hormuz, with multiple European nations declaring the conflict "not our war."[10] French President [[emmanuel-macron|Emmanuel Macron]] stated that France is "not party to the conflict" and "will never take part in operations" to reopen the strait, while Germany's Defense Minister [[boris-pistorius|Boris Pistorius]] questioned the point of sending European frigates when the powerful U.S. Navy was already involved; Spain, Italy and [[greece|Greece]] also declined.[10] Trump criticized the reluctance as a "one-way street" but insisted the United States no longer needed or desired NATO assistance, claiming recent military successes had set Iran back by up to a decade.[10]

Parts of the alternative media described the situation as a [[kobayashi-maru|Kobayashi Maru]] no-win scenario for the administration - retreat risking a Suez Crisis-style geopolitical defeat, continuation risking prolonged energy-market chaos and higher global inflation - a framing Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] inverted, arguing the trap was engineered by Trump against the globalist enemy rather than sprung upon him.[10]

Iranian officials meanwhile continued to assert control over the waterway, warning that its security situation had permanently changed, as Iran escalated into what it called a "new phase of oil war" with drone swarms and attacks on energy infrastructure in the UAE, Iraq and Saudi Arabia.[10]

## The Easter 2026 ultimatum

At Easter 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said the United States would begin targeting Iranian power plants and bridges starting Tuesday evening if [[iran|Iran]] did not reopen the strait, setting a deadline of 8:00 p.m. Eastern Time after extending a prior ultimatum.[11] A proposal assembled by [[pakistan|Pakistan]] and shared with Washington and Tehran outlined a two-phase plan: an immediate ceasefire and reopening of the strait, formalized through a memorandum of understanding, followed by negotiations on a broader agreement covering longer-term issues.[11] Iran had not agreed to the proposal as the deadline approached.[11]

The strait also sat at the center of the [[nato|NATO]] rift, with Trump criticizing European allies for declining to deploy naval forces there and signaling the United States would retake Hormuz on its own timeline, leaving allies to secure their own energy lifelines or buy American exports at premium rates.[11]

## March 2026 supply shock

By March 2026 shipping through the strait — a corridor carrying roughly 20% of the world's oil supply — had been severely curtailed, creating fears of a large-scale supply shock, with analysts estimating that sharply reduced transits had removed a significant share of crude exports from the market and pushed prices higher.[12] Brent crude briefly climbed above $119 per barrel, while an [[islamic-revolutionary-guard-corps|IRGC]] spokesman warned that continued US and Israeli action against [[iran|Iran]] could trigger retaliation affecting oil production and economic infrastructure across the region, with Iranian officials warning of $200-per-barrel crude.[12]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the near-collapse of tanker traffic through Hormuz as part of a deliberate weaponization of energy, observing that China-linked carriers continued to transit the narrowest stretches without incident while European and allied economies bore the brunt — evidence, in his reading, of a [[nuclear-renaissance|Nuclear Renaissance]] being accelerated by the crisis.[12]

## Shared control proposal and price effects (March 2026)

In March 2026 the war significantly disrupted global oil flows through the strait, driving the US [[2026-gasoline-price-surge|gasoline price surge]] toward the $4-per-gallon threshold.[13] Asked about reopening the strait, President [[donald-trump|Trump]] suggested that he and the Ayatollah might control it together, a remark the Iranian government answered by posting a picture of a child's toy steering wheel alongside his quote and emojis.[13]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the dynamics being amplified in the strait are designed not to trap the United States in further foreign dependence but to break it, with the calibrated crisis creating conditions for a clean disentanglement from Middle Eastern energy leverage on both Actual and Narrative grounds.[13] On his reading the situation is forcing a permanent sovereign disentanglement of foreign energy flows toward a multipolar realignment in which America is no longer vulnerable to single chokepoints — a pattern he expected adversaries and allies to mirror, eventually squeezing oil conglomerate margins globally.[13]

## 2026 disruption amid the Iran conflict

Escalating hostilities involving [[iran|Iran]] in early 2026 disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a key global oil transit route connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea, through which approximately 20% of the world's oil supply passes.[14] Recent attacks and electronic interference in the region led shipping companies to suspend or reduce transits through the waterway.[14]

Saudi oil facilities were drawn into the broader regional conflict following conventional and drone attacks on oil infrastructure in [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]].[14] Oil prices and tanker costs rose in response to the disruption risks, and energy analysts said prolonged interruption of shipping through the strait could significantly affect global energy markets.[14] US naval forces operated in the region to safeguard maritime traffic and global commerce.[14]

## "Guardian of the Hormuz Strait" (July 2026)

In July 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced on [[truth-social|Truth Social]] that the United States would be known as the "[[guardian-of-the-hormuz-strait|Guardian of the Hormuz Strait]]," providing security for the waterway while allowing all countries except [[iran|Iran]] and its customers under the "Iranian Blockade" to continue using it.[15] He said the United States would be reimbursed through a proposed 20% fee on all cargo shipped through the Strait to cover the costs of that security, with the process beginning immediately.[15]

## Reopening under the June 2026 Iran deal

President [[donald-trump|Trump]] announced in June 2026 that a peace agreement between the United States and [[iran|Iran]] had been completed, describing it as a deal that would bring "Peace and Security" to the region.[16] According to Trump the agreement included the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, removal of the US naval blockade, and mine-removal operations to restore the free flow of commercial shipping and oil transit.[16] In a Monday morning Truth Social post he said ships loaded with oil had already begun moving through the Strait, adding that the southern shipping route was "totally safe, secure, and pristine."[16]

## April 2026 narrative escalation

In April 2026 news of Iranian forces firing on a vessel in the Strait of Hormuz flooded the info-war battlespace, arriving alongside the [[mv-touska-seizure|M/V Touska seizure]] in the north Arabian Sea.[17] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the analytically important question is not what is or is not occurring within the Strait, but which actors the escalatory narratives emanate from and which they conspicuously do not — his standing thesis on the [[fake-iran-war|fake Iran war]].[17]

The Strait was also cited in a Badlands discussion of "GeoSpatial Politics" as a live example of geography shaping geopolitics through its connection to worldwide energy prices.[17]

## Reopening under the June 2026 agreement

The urgent reopening of the Strait of Hormuz was one of the 14 points codified in the [[us-iran-memorandum-of-understanding|US–Iran memorandum of understanding]] signed in June 2026.[18] [[centcom|CENTCOM]] subsequently announced that U.S. forces had lifted the blockade on all maritime traffic entering and exiting Iranian ports and coastal areas in accordance with the President's direction, stating that American forces were not impeding vessel transit and that all blockade enforcement efforts had ceased, while naval ships would remain in the general area to ensure the agreement was adhered to.[18] Badlands reported that [[iran|Iran]] had moved over five million barrels of oil in the days after the electronic signing.[18]

In parallel, Badlands cited reporting affirmed by journalist [[barak-ravid|Barak Ravid]] that [[israel|Israel]] had likely invaded southern Lebanon in an attempt to seize control of the Strait, framing the Israeli operation around Nabatieh as a challenge to the newly reopened corridor.[18]

## Blockade and tiered transit control (April 2026)

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced on April 7, 2026 a two-week suspension of U.S. bombing operations against [[iran|Iran]], contingent on Tehran reopening the Strait of Hormuz, as part of a provisional ceasefire mediated by [[pakistan|Pakistan]] and joined by [[israel|Israel]].[19] After talks in Islamabad to extend the truce collapsed over Iran's nuclear program and related conditions, Trump ordered a U.S. naval blockade targeting Iranian ports and vessels while allowing non-Iranian shipping to continue transiting the strait, and warned of possible strikes on Iranian infrastructure.[19] The [[islamic-revolutionary-guard-corps|Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps]] said any military vessel approaching the strait would be considered a ceasefire violation and could prompt retaliation, and oil prices rose amid fears of disrupted traffic through the route.[19]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that Iran was asserting direct and tiered control over the strait — limiting daily transits, vetting affiliations, and floating toll structures leaning on DeFi rails for reconstruction funding — and that this had triggered complaints about "dangerous precedents" from the institutions that once enforced the rules-based order.[19] He characterized the blockade as one the United States is largely insulated from but that the globalist hegemon represented by [[nato|NATO]] and the [[european-union|European Union]] cannot afford.[19]

## June 2026 closure threat
After [[iran|Iran]] suspended indirect talks with the United States, [[tasnim-news-agency|Tasnim News Agency]] reported that Tehran and allied resistance groups were weighing efforts to close the Strait of Hormuz and to activate pressure points along other strategic waterways, including the [[bab-el-mandeb-strait|Bab el-Mandeb Strait]], unless Israeli operations in Gaza and [[lebanon|Lebanon]] ended.[20]

## Iraq-Syria crude oil pipeline revival (2026)

US officials said in July 2026 that the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] is supporting efforts to revive a long-idled crude oil pipeline connecting [[iraq|Iraq]] to [[syria|Syria]]'s Mediterranean coast, giving Iraq an alternative export route and reducing its dependence on the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] and the Persian Gulf.[21] Officials described the proposal as part of a broader strategy to strengthen regional energy security and limit [[iran|Iran]]'s leverage over global oil shipments, though significant infrastructure repairs, security challenges, and political agreements between Iraq and Syria would still be required.[21] Iran-backed Iraqi militias opposed the plan, with one prominent group threatening to disrupt any future pipeline project.[21] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] noted the announcement came less than a week after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] revealed his intention to remove Syria from the state sponsors of terrorism list, opening the country to international investors, and predicted Saudi involvement.[21] See [[iraq-syria-crude-oil-pipeline|the pipeline]] for fuller coverage.[21]


## June 2026 shipping disruption

The June 2026 Iran–Israel escalation, including the reported downing of a U.S. Apache helicopter and President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s claim of an imminent Middle East peace deal, affected regional shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and contributed to volatility in global oil prices.[22]

## Post-agreement normalization dispute (June 2026)

After the early signing of the [[us-iran-memorandum-of-understanding|US–Iran memorandum of understanding]], major media outlets and energy analysts reported that any easing in oil and gas markets was unlikely to bring an immediate return to pre-crisis conditions, with analysts cited by OilPrice.com saying sanctions relief, infrastructure constraints and shipping disruptions could delay significant increases in Iranian exports and further reports noting that uncertainty surrounding the Strait and regional security continued to weigh on markets.[23] Businesses and governments prepared for prolonged supply-chain disruptions and elevated energy costs, with concerns focused on shipping through the Persian Gulf and the potential for renewed instability.[23]

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] struck a more optimistic tone, writing on Truth Social that oil prices should come down and expressing confidence that the agreement and regional developments would stabilize markets.[23] He claimed traffic through the chokepoint would normalize rapidly — and possibly already had — asserting that hundreds of ships and millions of barrels of oil continued to move through the Strait for the entirety of the conflict, often under conditions the surface narrative was never made privy to.[23] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated that claim as evidence that the visible war functioned as narrative theater and as an accelerant for the [[nuclear-energy-pivot|Nuclear Energy Pivot]].[23]

### June 2026 MOU collapse and the tanker strikes

A renewed round of hostilities in late June 2026 was resolved when the United States and [[iran|Iran]] agreed to halt further military strikes and to meet in Doha, [[qatar|Qatar]], to address disputes surrounding the Strait, according to senior U.S. officials cited by [[axios|Axios]].[24] Both sides agreed to suspend "kinetic activity" and continue technical discussions.[24] The Guardian reported that differing interpretations of the earlier memorandum of understanding, particularly regarding the Strait, appear to have contributed to the escalation, and that a residential building in Muharraq, [[bahrain|Bahrain]], was struck by an Iranian drone.[24] [[reuters|Reuters]] reported that oil prices rose on June 29 after reciprocal U.S. and Iranian strikes slowed shipping through the chokepoint, and that attacks on vessels resumed, including an incident involving a Qatar-linked oil tanker.[24]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued the collapse was engineered.[24] He noted that the [[international-maritime-organization|International Maritime Organization]] had worked with the United States, Iran, and the Gulf States to establish a two-lane traffic pattern that all parties agreed would be the sole routes used during the MOU's 60-day window, during which Iran paused toll collection.[24] The tanker Ever Lovely instead charted a course along the Omani coast with five other tankers; when Iranian authorities ordered them back to the designated lanes, the other five complied while the Ever Lovely's AIS tracker stopped transmitting, after which "an object," presumed to be a drone, struck the vessel.[24] A second ship, the Kiku — owned by [[evalend-shipping|Evalend Shipping]] of [[kriton-lentoudis|Kriton Lentoudis]] — was reportedly struck by Iran on the following Saturday after attempting to bypass the agreed lanes.[24]

The author tied both vessels to the sanctioned oil-laundering network of [[salim-ahmed-said|Salim Ahmed Said]] via [[vs-tankers-fze|VS Tankers FZE]], and drew a further link to [[evergreen-group|Evergreen Group]], owner of the Ever Given that blocked the Suez Canal in 2021.[24] He also observed that Iran's strikes fell on [[kuwait|Kuwait]] and Bahrain — the states that had attended [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]]'s UAE meeting convened, in his reading, by the [[deep-state|Deep State]] to undermine the [[steve-witkoff|Witkoff]]–[[jared-kushner|Kushner]] peace deal — concluding that Iran was effectively acting as an enforcer for [[donald-trump|Trump]], a callback to the [[doha-agreement|Doha Agreement]] signed with the [[taliban|Taliban]] in 2020.[24]

## Permanent reopening and Chinese cooperation (April 2026)

In April 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said that [[china|China]] was "very happy" that he was permanently opening the Strait of Hormuz, framing the operation as undertaken for China and the world and asserting that the situation "will never happen again."[25] The claim accompanied continued aggressive US military and economic action in the Middle East, including a naval blockade of Iranian ports and operations to reopen the strait, described as a critical global energy chokepoint on which China depends heavily for energy imports.[25] Concurrently, [[iraq|Iraq]] moved to revive the [[iraq-saudi-pipeline|Iraq–Saudi Pipeline (IPSA)]] to the Red Sea specifically so that Iraqi crude could bypass Hormuz, part of a wider trend of Middle Eastern producers seeking redundant export routes amid conflict with [[iran|Iran]].[25]
