# Howard Scott

Howard Scott was the founder and leader of [[technocracy-inc|Technocracy Inc.]], the North American movement that campaigned in the 1930s for the replacement of price-based capitalism with an engineer-run "Technate."[1] Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes him as "a snake oil salesman who lied about being an engineer for his entire adult life."[1]

## From the Technical Alliance to Technocracy Inc.

Scott joined the [[technical-alliance|Technical Alliance]] in 1919, an organization created by the economist [[thorstein-veblen|Thorstein Veblen]] around a study group at the [[new-school-for-social-research|New School for Social Research]] in Greenwich Village.[1] Through the Alliance, Scott received contracts from the Industrial Workers of the World and the Railroad Brotherhood to compile voluminous data on American agriculture, industry, raw materials and workers — including worker behaviour and psychological profiles — an early exercise in mass data collection intended to make "scientific" social control possible.[1]

In 1932 Scott founded Technocracy Inc. in New York, promising to reorganize both human society and human nature around a top-down Technate of experts insulated from democratic institutions and profit motives.[1] The historian William E. Akin, writing in *Technocracy and the American Dream*, concluded that "Scott absorbed many of Veblen's fundamental themes," adopting Veblen's scientific positivism and technological determinism and seeing society as a mechanical operation — an influence Scott himself later denied.[1]

## Doctrine

Scott and his fellow technocrats held that automation, computers and robots would soon render most jobs worthless, so that the majority of humanity would neither work nor participate in government, both being privileges reserved for initiated technicians.[1] He summarized his view of labour with the line: "One of the lowest social diseases is the belief in the morality of work."[1] The proposed remedy was a form of guaranteed income — "energy credits" distributed to all people regardless of employment — a proposal Ehret links directly to modern advocacy of [[universal-basic-income|Universal Basic Income]].[1]

Scott directed the Energy Survey of North America, an attempt to measure all energy associated with the production of consumable and capital goods on the continent, on the theory that total production energy could then be divided among all citizens over the age of 25.[1] Under his scheme no one could accumulate more energy certificates than anyone else, invest, start a business for profit, or save credits, since any of these would produce inequality; in exchange, members were promised a 16-hour work week and retirement at 45.[1]

Asked to situate his movement politically, Scott said: "As far as Technocracy's ideas are concerned, we're so far left that we make communism look bourgeois."[1] Ehret argues that the abundance rhetoric of Scott, [[joshua-haldeman|Joshua Haldeman]] and other technocrats was empty, because the movement's underlying creed was absolute submission to entropy and universal decay.[1] He further alleges that Scott, like the Social Credit theorist C. H. Douglas, was closely affiliated with the esoteric and oligarchical networks whose existence their public conspiracy narratives obscured.[1]
