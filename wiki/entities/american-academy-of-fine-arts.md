# American Academy of Fine Arts

The American Academy of Fine Arts was an early nineteenth-century New York institution founded by the Revolutionary War painter [[john-trumbull|John Trumbull]] and a group of Federalist financiers, and the earliest institutional attempt to shape something that could be called "an American school of painting."[1] In Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]], the Academy is characterised as an Anglophile instrument that suppressed rather than nurtured a republican aesthetic in the young United States.[1]

## Structure and patrons

Its primary sponsor was [[robert-livingston|Robert Livingston]], described as the dominant force behind the Federalist Party after [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] was removed from the picture in 1804.[1] Other members of the circle included James Pinchot, Thomas Clark and Ogden Haggerty, leading speculators and political intelligence operatives among the Federalists whom the article implicates in the [[aaron-burr|Aaron Burr]] conspiracy to dissolve the union.[1] Trumbull was the only practising artist among the founding group.[1]

[[william-dunlap|William Dunlap]] described the institution as a "joint stock company," composed of persons of every trade and profession who thought the privilege of visiting the exhibition an equivalent for their subscription; stockholders elected the directors, while artists could share those privileges only by purchasing stock and could "be controlled in everything respecting their profession by those who were ignorant of the arts."[1]

## Aesthetic consequences

Because portraiture was the most lucrative field of painting before the advent of photography, the Academy's financier patrons built a market almost exclusively around it, so that anyone wishing to be an artist in America and also make ends meet had to become a portrait painter.[1] Some latitude was permitted for undemanding landscape work, but the article argues that essentially no inspiring painting of the period approached the achievements of European masters such as [[leonardo-da-vinci|Leonardo da Vinci]], Raphael Sanzio, Rembrandt van Rijn or Vermeer.[1] Ehret holds that the patrons enjoyed the field mainly for money laundering, speculation, entertainment and commercialism, and worked to stifle the promethean spirit that had given rise to the revolution.[1]

## Collapse

The Academy's monopoly was broken when [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]] and Dunlap founded the [[national-academy-of-design|National Academy of the Arts of Design]], drawing away the best young talent.[1] After a failed compromise over artist representation on the board and a series of public debates in which Morse eviscerated Trumbull's defence of aristocratic patronage, Trumbull was forced to resign by popular consent and the Academy disintegrated within months, around 1828.[1]
