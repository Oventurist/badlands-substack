# Timothy Pickering

Timothy Pickering was a former United States Secretary of State under President John Adams and, in the account given by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] for Badlands Media, the guiding hand behind the New England cabal known as the [[essex-junto|Essex Junto]].[1] Ehret lists him among the "aristocratically minded traitors" — alongside [[aaron-burr|Aaron Burr]], Col. [[james-wilkinson|James Wilkinson]], [[george-cabot|George Cabot]] and Albert Gallatin — who worked within the eastern establishment on behalf of the British Empire to break the American republic into two separate confederacies under the argument that slave states and free states could not co-exist.[1]

## The 1803 letter to Richard Peters

Writing to his co-conspiratorial senator Richard Peters on December 24, 1803, Pickering described the design in explicit terms, declaring that although "the end of all our Revolutionary labors and expectations is disappointment," he would "rather anticipate a new confederacy, exempt from the corrupt and corrupting influence and oppression of the aristocratic Democrats of the South," adding that "the white and black population will mark the boundary" and that "the British Provinces, even with the assent of Britain, will become members of the Northern confederacy."[1]

## Correspondence with George Cabot

In a letter to the opium-trading Junto leader George Cabot, Pickering elaborated that the British provinces in Canada and Nova Scotia might, "at no remote period, perhaps without delay, and with the assent of Great Britain," become members of the Northern League, and that a liberal treaty of amity and commerce would "form a bond of union between Great Britain and the Northern confederacy highly useful to both."[1] He reasoned that London would be rid of the cost of maintaining its provinces while still receiving all the commercial returns its merchants then enjoyed.[1]

## Promotion of Burr in New York

In a further letter of March 4, 1804, Pickering wrote that the Federalists "in general anxiously desire the election of Mr. Burr to the chair of New York," believing that "Mr. Burr alone... can break your Democratic phalanx."[1] Detaching New York from Virginia influence, he argued, would benefit the whole Union and force [[thomas-jefferson|Jefferson]] into caution; and "if a separation should be deemed proper, the five New England States, New York, and New Jersey would naturally be united," with "New York the centre."[1] The strategy hinged on bringing New York into the northern secession plot as the economic powerhouse capable of fusing the free states into British Canada, and it collapsed when [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] engineered Burr's defeat in the 1804 New York gubernatorial race.[1]
