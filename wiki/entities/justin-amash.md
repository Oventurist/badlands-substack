# Justin Amash

**Justin Amash** is a former Michigan congressman known for his strict constitutionalist and libertarian reading of federal power, particularly on questions of war and executive authority.[1] In January 2024 he published a widely circulated thread on X analyzing the legality of [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s airstrikes on [[yemen|Yemen]], which [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] quoted at length in the Badlands Media essay "War Pigs."[1]

## Analysis of the War Powers Resolution

Amash argued that of the three scenarios listed in Section 1541(c) of the [[war-powers-act|War Powers Resolution]], the first two permit the president to take offensive military action only with Congress's express approval, since Article I of the Constitution grants [[congress|Congress]] the exclusive power to declare war.[1] The third authority, he wrote, allows defensive action without congressional approval only in the event of a sudden, unforeseen attack on the United States occurring too quickly for Congress to convene.[1]

It is for that last situation — or for cases in which the president introduces forces into hostilities unlawfully — that the resolution provides the 48-hour report to Congress under § 1543 and the 60-day, extendable-to-90-day withdrawal timeline under § 1544.[1] Amash emphasized that the response contemplated is to an attack "currently happening," after which the president is to be held to account.[1]

He further noted that the Framers, at the federal convention of 1787, agreed that the president should have the "power to repel sudden attacks" but not the power to otherwise introduce forces into hostilities without congressional approval.[1] His conclusion was that the War Powers Resolution "does not confer any new authority on the president to take offensive military action without congressional approval — nor could it under our Constitution," functioning instead as a check on the president.[1]

## Use in Badlands commentary

DeLarme presented Amash's breakdown as the decisive rebuttal to defenders of the Yemen strikes, pairing it with his own argument that reliance on the [[authorization-for-use-of-military-force-of-2001|2001 AUMF]] reflected a [[neoconservatives|neoconservative]] theory of unlimited executive war-making that took hold during the Bush–Cheney era.[1]
