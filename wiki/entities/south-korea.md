# South Korea

South Korea is a U.S. treaty ally in East Asia that hosts roughly 28,000 American troops and advanced air defense systems under a decades-long alliance aimed at countering nuclear-armed [[north-korea|North Korea]].[1]

## 2026 dispute over air defense transfers

In March 2026, South Korea objected to the redeployment of U.S. air defense systems from its territory to the Middle East but was unable to block the move.[1] [[us-forces-korea|US Forces Korea]] transferred Patriot interceptors and other assets from Osan Air Base to bases in [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] and the [[united-arab-emirates|United Arab Emirates]] to replenish stocks depleted by the [[iran-war|US-Israeli campaign against Iran]], then in its twelfth day.[1]

President [[lee-jae-myung|Lee Jae-myung]] said Seoul had voiced its opposition to USFK acting "according to its own military needs" while conceding that "it is also the reality that we cannot fully enforce our position," and maintained that the transfer would not cause a "serious setback" to South Korean deterrence against the North.[1] Badlands commentary treated the episode as evidence that the Iran war was forcing the [[military-industrial-complex|Military Industrial Complex]] to draw down the Pacific theater.[1]

## Response to North Korean deployments to Russia, 2024
In October 2024, South Korea signalled it was considering directly supplying weapons to Ukraine as evidence mounted that North Korean soldiers were preparing to assist Russia's war effort.[2] The country's spy agency, the National Intelligence Service, reported that North Korea had shipped 1,500 special forces personnel to Russia's Far East for training and acclimatisation, and local media citing the NIS said Pyongyang planned to dispatch 12,000 troops in four brigades.[2]

A senior official in the office of President Yoon Suk Yeol said on Tuesday, October 22: "We would consider supplying weapons for defensive purposes as part of the step-by-step scenarios, and if it seems they are going too far, we might also consider offensive use."[2] The statement marked Seoul's most proactive position toward arming Ukraine to that date and underscored the potential for a divided Korean peninsula to become entangled in the war.[2]

## December 2024 martial law crisis

On the night of December 3, 2024, President [[yoon-suk-yeol|Yoon Suk Yeol]] imposed martial law, sending troops to surround parliament before the [[national-assembly-of-south-korea|National Assembly]] voted to reject military rule and the measure was lifted around 4:30 a.m. after roughly six hours.[3] The [[2024-south-korea-martial-law-declaration|declaration]] blindsided the nation, Yoon's own [[people-power-party|People Power Party]] and global allies including the United States, and the main opposition [[democratic-party-of-korea|Democratic Party]] moved to impeach Yoon on treason charges along with the defense and safety ministers.[3] The chaos whipsawed investors and prompted reassurances from monetary authorities that they would support markets if needed, with analysts predicting a presidential election early in the second quarter of 2025.[3]

## April 2025 tariff call with Trump

On the morning of Tuesday, April 8, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced on Truth Social that he had spoken with South Korea's acting president, amid US tariffs targeting Seoul and many other countries.[4] Trump described "a great call," listing topics including South Korea's "tremendous and unsustainable Surplus," tariffs, shipbuilding, large-scale purchases of US LNG, a joint venture in an Alaska pipeline, and payment for the "big time Military Protection" the United States provides.[4] He claimed those military payments had begun during his first term and totaled billions of dollars before [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] terminated the deal "for reasons unknown."[4] The post suggested a deal with South Korea was possible as part of multifaceted negotiations with foreign nations, and the stock market, which had fallen since the tariff announcement the previous week, spiked higher that morning.[4] Reporting noted South Korea has long supported the cost of the US military presence in the country, dating back to before Trump's first term.[4]

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that South Korea, like Ukraine, Taiwan, and Guyana, "is not a real country" but a regime proxy state and US military protectorate, predicting that when the Korean War is officially declared over it will simply be "southern Korea."[4]

## July 2025 US tariffs

On Monday, July 7, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] set a 25 percent tax on goods imported from South Korea and [[japan|Japan]], along with new tariff rates on a dozen other nations, effective August 1.[5] He provided notice by posting letters on Truth Social addressed to the leaders of the countries involved — including South Korean President [[lee-jae-myung|Lee Jae-myung]] — warning that any retaliatory increase would be added on top of the 25 percent rate.[5]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] called the South Korea tariff even more telling than the Japanese one, citing Colonel [[douglas-macgregor|Douglas Macgregor]]'s explanation that South Korea has been a CIA puppet state since the end of the Korean War.[5] Under this reading, the South Korean government has long engaged in a "slap-tickle fight" with North Korea that helped sustain a constant existential threat from the ruling Kim dynasty, which the military and defense sector exploited in order to extract the wealth of the US taxpayer; the commentary concluded that the American people should never again be beholden to a foreign power that thinks it can dictate terms to the United States.[5]

## Warning over North Korean balloons (September 2024)

On Monday, September 23, 2024, South Korea's military pledged a "stern" response if [[north-korea|North Korea]] "crosses the line" with its campaign of sending trash-filled balloons across the border, after a barrage launched between Sunday evening and early Monday morning forced flight suspensions at the country's main international airport.[6] Col. Lee Sung-jun, spokesman for Seoul's Joint Chiefs of Staff, told a press briefing that the military viewed such incidents as "having a higher level of risk," and said that "if serious danger is caused to the safety of our citizens or if the North is assessed to have crossed the line, our military will take stern military measures."[6] He declined to specify where that line lay, adding that the military does not consider the balloons a serious threat to public safety "as long as there are no fatalities."[6]

The balloon exchange began in April 2024, when South Korea sent 20 large balloons carrying more than 300,000 leaflets criticizing [[kim-jong-un|Kim Jong Un]] along with 2,000 USB sticks of K-pop content into the North; Pyongyang's trash-balloon reply followed in May.[6]
