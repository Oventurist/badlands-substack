# Alexander Hamilton

Alexander Hamilton was an American founder and Federalist, best known in the September 2, 2024 Badlands News Brief for his authorship of [[federalist-papers|Federalist Paper]] No. 67, "The Executive Department."[1]

## Federalist No. 67 and the administrative state

In that paper Hamilton argued that the federal government would not degenerate into a monarchy or dictatorship because power would be decentralized within the Executive Branch through the appointment of unlimited bureaucrats, each individually possessing only a portion of power, so that no single person or agency alone wielded absolute control.[1] The Badlands commentary treated this as a catastrophic misjudgment, siding instead with [[patrick-henry|Patrick Henry]], who called the paper an abomination that would produce a new form of tyranny.[1]

## Place in the constitutional debate

Hamilton's Federalist position stands opposite the Anti-Federalist demand that produced the [[bill-of-rights|Bill of Rights]], drafted by [[james-madison|James Madison]] in 1791 as a concession to critics who found the [[united-states-constitution|Constitution]] preoccupied with the powers of government rather than the rights of the people.[1] The brief argued that the modern bureaucratic apparatus vindicated Henry and discredited Hamilton, and called for reopening the Federalist/Anti-Federalist argument.[1]

## Treasury Secretary and the American System

Hamilton served as George Washington's Aide de Camp during the Revolutionary War and later as the first Treasury Secretary, in which capacity he authored four major reports to Congress in 1791–92.[2] In the period 1783–1791 the newly established American republic was an agrarian economy in financial ruins, with no means to pay off its debts or even the soldiers who had fought for years in the revolutionary war, and it appeared only a matter of time before the fragile new nation would be re-absorbed into the British Empire.[2] Hamilton, who had studied the great dirigiste economists such as France's Finance Minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert, introduced a four-fold solution.[2]

First, he consolidated all unpayable state debts into a single federal debt secured by the issuance of new bonds, through his *1790 Report on Public Credit*.[2] Second, he tied those bonds to internal improvements — roads, canals, academies and industrial growth — creating a qualitatively new form of debt that would let the nation produce its way out of poverty and achieve "the augmentation of the active or productive capital of a country," a thrust developed in his [[report-on-the-subject-of-manufactures|Report on the Subject of Manufactures]] of 1791.[2] Third, he proposed to guide the new national power over finance through the [[first-bank-of-the-united-states|First Bank of the United States]] and a system of national banks subservient to the Constitution and the General Welfare, as set out in his *1790 Report on a National Bank* and his 1791 *On the Constitutionality of a National Bank*.[2] Fourth, he called for protective measures to block foreign dumping of cheap goods, along with federal incentives and bounties encouraging private enterprises to build in alignment with the national interest.[2]

Hamilton insisted that value lies not in land, gold, money, or any arbitrary measure favored by followers of the British School such as [[adam-smith|Adam Smith]], but in the physical and intellectual development of the nation.[2] His writings praised Colbert in "The Continentalist" of 1782 for having "laid the foundation of the French commerce."[2] Nancy Spannaus, author of *Hamilton vs Wall Street*, summarizes that for Hamilton the wealth of the nation depends upon its physical economic development, "including, most emphatically, the intellectual capabilities of its population."[2]

## Contested reputation

Contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] opens the Badlands Media "Founders & Foundations" series by observing that Hamilton's name invokes radically different things to different people: forces on the political left promote a version of Hamilton conforming to a desire to justify centralized planning and empire, while figures on the right have also embraced him — Romania's conservative leader [[calin-georgescu|Calin Georgescu]] described himself on [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]]'s program on April 10, 2025 as "The Alexander Hamilton of Romania."[2] [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] endorsed Hamilton at a 2017 press conference, saying "I think Hamilton is tough to beat," and [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] later encouraged restoration of the Hamilton–Clay American System.[2] Among alternative-media networks a contrary consensus formed around the thesis that Hamilton was a Rothschild stooge whose national bank was the forerunner to the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and whose Federalist Papers writings justified the modern deep state.[2] Ehret, who studied Hamilton's writings from 2006 to 2012 and led workshops on the subject through the [[rising-tide-foundation|Rising Tide Foundation]], concludes that most of what people think about Hamilton — admirers and detractors alike — is mistaken.[2]

## Death and aftermath

Hamilton was killed in 1804 by [[aaron-burr|Aaron Burr]], described by Ehret as a City of London-affiliated traitor who established the speculative Bank of Manhattan and thereby started Wall Street, derailing many of Hamilton's grand designs for the young republic.[2] The system nonetheless survived decades of attempts to destroy it.[2] Ehret argues that quite literally every American president who died in office by poison or bullet was re-activating the Hamiltonian system of constitutional banking shaped by Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution, protective tariffs, and internal improvements — a group including [[john-quincy-adams|John Quincy Adams]], Senator [[henry-clay|Henry Clay]], [[william-henry-harrison|William Harrison]], [[zachary-taylor|Zachary Taylor]], [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]], Secretary of State James Blaine, Garfield, [[william-mckinley|William McKinley]], [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]], and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]].[2] Outside the United States, leaders who broke their nations free of British imperial control were frequently students of the Hamiltonian system, including Russia's [[sergei-witte|Sergey Witte]], French President Sadi Carnot, Germany's [[friedrich-list|Friedrich List]], China's Sun Yat-sen, and Argentina's Luis Marie Drago.[2]
