# Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)

OPEC is the multinational oil producers' cartel whose quota system has, for decades, governed global crude supply and defended price floors. In Badlands coverage dated May 1, 2026, the organization was described as facing a "major strategic fracture" following the formal exit of the [[united-arab-emirates|United Arab Emirates]], an event framed as weakening the cartel's ability to control supply and defend prices.[1]

## The UAE exit

The UAE was reported to be leaving OPEC on May 1, 2026, after nearly six decades of membership.[1] Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the departure not as an impulsive wartime defection but as a deliberate sovereign policy decision, noting that Abu Dhabi had already invested heavily to expand production capacity from roughly 3.4 million barrels per day toward 5 million by 2027.[1] By stepping outside the cartel's quota "straitjacket," the Emirates were said to be signaling the end of the enforced scarcity model they had once helped perpetuate.[1]

Russia publicly downplayed the risk of an immediate price war, arguing that disruptions from the Iran war and constraints at the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] still dominated market pricing.[1] Analysts cited in the coverage held that the real impact would arrive after the war: once Gulf exports normalized, the UAE could ramp production outside quota limits, potentially forcing [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] and other producers into a market-share fight.[1]

## Framing as a cartel apparatus

Burning Bright described OPEC as "the crown jewel" of a broader "Global Cartel" apparatus — a centralized, cross-border collective that weaponized financial and energy levers against sovereign nations, using war, regime change and color revolutions as enforcement mechanisms whenever a player threatened the scarcity model.[1] Under this reading, OPEC functioned as the throttle ensuring artificial price floors, engineered dependence, and compliance through economic pain.[1]

## Relationship to Epic Fury

The Badlands analysis tied the unwinding of OPEC to [[operation-epic-fury|Epic Fury]], arguing that the "end of OPEC" was not merely an energy story but a geopolitical objective: break Iran's leverage, weaken cartel coordination, and drive postwar oil prices lower.[1] Burning Bright predicted a cascade in which other Gulf producers long constrained by artificial caps would follow the UAE, with Saudi spare capacity — previously held in reserve for cartel discipline — exposed to the logic of competition, producing a free-market race to the top in capacity and innovation and a race to the bottom in price.[1]

## Announcement of the UAE withdrawal (April 2026)

On Tuesday, April 28, 2026, the [[united-arab-emirates|UAE]] announced its withdrawal from both OPEC and OPEC+, describing the step as a unilateral policy decision taken after a review of its energy strategies and production policies.[2] UAE Energy Minister [[suhail-al-mazrouei|Suhail Mohamed al-Mazrouei]] cited frustration over insufficient [[gulf-cooperation-council|Gulf Cooperation Council]] support against Iranian attacks and threats during the conflict, calling the exit a blow to OPEC unity amid the historic energy shock caused by [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] disruptions.[2]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the pending exit, effective May 1, as "the first domino to fall" against a cartel that had spent decades keeping global energy prices artificially inflated — scarcity by design that he argued greased the rails for a green globalist agenda then being dismantled in favor of national interests and sovereign production.[2]

## August 2025 output decision

In July 2025 an OPEC+ meeting to decide August production levels — expected to be a short routine video call announcing another output hike of 411,000 barrels per day — instead produced a larger-than-expected increase of 548,000 bpd.[3] The eight OPEC+ members unwinding earlier cuts were expected to make another supersized increase in September, at which point the full 2.2 million bpd of cuts would nominally be back on the market.[3] Analysts cautioned that no one is certain how much OPEC+ is actually raising production, since some producers are pumping below quota to compensate for previous overproduction, and concurred that the supersized hikes are not as large as the headline figures suggest.[3]
