# SAVAK

SAVAK — a Farsi acronym for the Organization of Intelligence and Security of the Nation — was the domestic intelligence and secret police service of Imperial Iran, formed in 1957. It was created with the help of the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]] and Israel's [[mossad]], and initially targeted communists and leftists in the aftermath of the [[1953-iranian-coup|1953 CIA-backed coup]] that removed elected Prime Minister [[mohammad-mosaddegh|Mohammed Mosaddegh]].[1]

## Function and methods

Over time SAVAK's scope widened drastically, and torture became widespread.[1] The organization operated as an intelligence agency with effectively unlimited police powers, serving as a deterrent to any opposition to [[mohammad-reza-pahlavi|the Shah]]. Its officers could spy on or arrest almost anyone at will and frequently used torture to extract information or simply to intimidate the population.[1] SAVAK's presence deepened through the 1960s and 1970s, when it arrested, tortured and killed untold thousands of Iranians — anyone perceived as a threat to the Shah's one-party rule.[1] In the eyes of the Iranian public, and especially of those who engineered the 1979 revolution that toppled the Shah, SAVAK was inseparable from Western interference in Iran's affairs and from the Tehran government's repressive control.[1]

## CIA training and Schwarzkopf

A U.S. Army colonel working for the CIA was sent to Persia in September 1953 to work with General Teymur Bakhtiar, appointed military governor of Tehran that December, who immediately began assembling the nucleus of a new intelligence organization.[1] In March 1955 the colonel was replaced with a more permanent team of five career CIA officers — specialists in covert operations, intelligence analysis and counterintelligence — including Major General [[norman-schwarzkopf-sr|Herbert Norman Schwarzkopf]], who "trained virtually all of the first generation of SAVAK personnel."[1]

## Interpretation in the article

Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presents SAVAK as one instance of a recurring CIA pattern: remove an elected leader who threatens cabal-controlled corporate control of national resources — in Mosaddegh's case, the nationalization of Iran's oil industry — install a puppet dictator, then train a security service to terrorize the population into compliance.[1] Lange compares SAVAK's methods to the [[phoenix-program|Phoenix Program]] in Vietnam and to the instruction given to Latin American officers at the [[school-of-the-americas|School of the Americas]].[1] In this reading, the [[iranian-revolution|Iranian Revolution]] removed a CIA client just as the Sandinistas had ousted the CIA-backed Somoza regime in Nicaragua, and that fact forms the forgotten backdrop of the [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] scandal.[1]
