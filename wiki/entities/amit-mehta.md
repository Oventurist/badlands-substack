# Amit Mehta

Amit Mehta is a United States district judge who has presided over prosecutions arising from the January 6, 2021 breach of the U.S. Capitol.[1] In late January 2024, after the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] agreed to review the government's use of the obstruction-of-an-official-proceeding charge in [[fischer-v-united-states|Fischer v. United States]], Mehta and other judges began granting early release to January 6 defendants pending the Court's decision, describing the step as "a real precautionary move to make sure nobody ends up serving more time than they should."[1]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized this shift as "feigning temperance" — arguing that it took a grant of certiorari to produce judicial restraint after years of aggressive application of a [[sarbanes-oxley-act|Sarbanes-Oxley]] financial-records statute to protest defendants — and framed the reversal as "narrative whiplash" with "no black pills in sight."[1]

## Google antitrust ruling (2024)

On August 5, 2024, Mehta ruled that [[google|Google]] had illegally monopolized the online search and search text advertising markets.[2] Google announced plans to appeal, but Mehta directed both parties to begin preparing for the next phase of the case, which would involve the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]]'s proposals for restoring competition — potentially including a breakup of the company.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] criticized his involvement, describing him as "an authoritarian who imagines the Judiciary as an extension of the Executive" and saying she remained firmly opposed to a breakup so long as Mehta and [[merrick-garland|Garland]] were directing it.[2] See [[united-states-v-google-antitrust-trial|United States v. Google]].[2]

## Sentencing of Peter Navarro (January 2024)

On Thursday, January 25, 2024, Mehta sentenced former Trump White House adviser [[peter-navarro|Peter Navarro]] to four months in prison for criminal contempt of Congress, telling him he was "not a victim" or a target of political persecution, as much as he may have proclaimed otherwise.[3] Navarro had been convicted in September 2023 on two counts for refusing to testify and produce documents to the [[january-6-committee|House Select Committee to Investigate the January 6th Attack]].[3] Mehta also imposed a $9,500 fine; prosecutors had sought six months against a one-month mandatory minimum.[3]

From the bench Mehta said what he found disappointing was that "in all of this, even today, there's little acknowledgment of what your obligation is as an American — to cooperate with Congress, to provide them with information that they're seeking," adding that even if Navarro believed the committee was "a political hatchet job," its members "had a job to do and you made it harder."[3] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] responded that "Judge Mehta has lost the plot," arguing that Americans' primary obligation is to the Constitution rather than to Congress.[3]

## Navarro contempt trial

Mehta presided over the September 2023 contempt of Congress trial of former Trump trade adviser [[peter-navarro|Peter Navarro]], for which a jury was seated on September 5, 2023.[4] In pre-trial hearings he characterized Navarro's legal arguments as "pretty weak sauce" and rejected the executive privilege defense, stating that "there was no formal invocation of executive privilege by [Trump] after personal consideration nor authorization to Mr. Navarro to invoke privilege on his behalf."[4] Badlands Media commentary held that Mehta had "already demonstrated bias in the case," making a conviction and subsequent appeal the likely outcome.[4]

## Oath Keepers convictions

In January 2023, Mehta oversaw the proceedings in which four members of the [[oath-keepers|Oath Keepers]] were convicted of seditious conspiracy in connection with January 6, 2021.[5] [[the-gateway-pundit|The Gateway Pundit]] described him as a "Wicked Obama Judge" presiding over what it called a "DC Kangaroo Court," and Badlands contributor [[sean-morgan|Sean Morgan]] cited the convictions as evidence of a two-tiered system of justice intended to chill protest over disputed elections.[5]
