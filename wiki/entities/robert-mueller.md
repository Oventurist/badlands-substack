# Robert Mueller

Robert S. Mueller III was an American prosecutor and law-enforcement official who served as Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation from 2001 to 2013 and later as the special counsel appointed to investigate alleged Russian interference in the 2016 United States presidential election.[1] He died on March 20, 2026, in Charlottesville, Virginia, according to WilmerHale, his former law firm, which said he was survived by his wife, Ann, two daughters and five grandchildren; Reuters and the Associated Press reported he was 81.[1]

## Early life, military service and legal career

Mueller attended Princeton University as an undergraduate and earned his law degree from the University of Virginia School of Law.[1] He served as a Marine officer during the Vietnam War and was awarded the Bronze Star and the Purple Heart.[1] He subsequently became a federal prosecutor, working in the United States Attorney's offices in San Francisco and Boston, where he built a reputation for pursuing violent crime, organized crime and public corruption cases.[1] By the late 1990s he had risen to serve as United States Attorney for the Northern District of California, and in 2001 he served as Acting Deputy Attorney General.[1]

## FBI directorship

Mueller was appointed to lead the [[fbi|FBI]] just days before the September 11, 2001 attacks, and he oversaw the bureau's transformation into a counterterrorism- and intelligence-focused agency in the wake of those attacks.[1] [[congress|Congress]] extended his term beyond the traditional ten-year limit on the grounds that the bureau required strong and consistent leadership during that period.[1]

## Special counsel investigation

Mueller was later appointed special counsel to investigate Russian interference in the 2016 election, including possible links or coordination between the Russian government and individuals associated with [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s campaign, according to the Justice Department's archived statement of his May 29, 2019 remarks.[1] In its final report, the special counsel's office said it did not establish a criminal conspiracy between Trump campaign members and Russia, and in Volume II wrote that while it did not conclude that Trump had committed a crime, it also did not exonerate him.[1] Reuters reported that the investigation produced charges against 34 individuals and entities, including Trump associates and Russian operatives.[1]

In Badlands commentary, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the inquiry as a "fabricated witch hunt investigation into 'Russian Collusion'" and noted that Mueller had been diagnosed with Parkinson's disease in 2021 although the public was not notified until August 2025 — roughly a month after [[tulsi-gabbard|Tulsi Gabbard]] announced that the government was reviewing those actions as a "treasonous conspiracy."[1]

## Death and reaction

After news of Mueller's death, accessible reports said Trump posted on [[truth-social|Truth Social]], "Good, I'm glad he's dead."[1] Ashe in America argued that there were "a thousand different reasons and a six-decade long record" about which Trump could have said what he said, and suggested that when the full truth is told all Americans may share the sentiment.[1]

## Comparison with the Durham investigation

During [[john-durham|John Durham]]'s June 21, 2023 testimony before the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]], Democratic members repeatedly contrasted Durham's inquiry with Mueller's, asking why Durham's investigation had cost more than $6 million while producing far fewer indictments than the Mueller probe.[2] [[adam-schiff|Adam Schiff]] continued to defend the legitimacy of the Mueller report during the hearing, a position [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued was untenable given that the [[durham-report|Durham Report]] had undermined the credibility of the [[steele-dossier|Steele dossier]].[2]
