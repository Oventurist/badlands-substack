# Harvard Management Company

The **Harvard Management Company (HMC)** is the investment arm that manages [[harvard-university|Harvard University]]'s endowment. In the Badlands Media essay "The Ivy Dominion," HMC is presented as the financial command node of the alleged [[ivy-dominion|Ivy Dominion]], with more than $50 billion under management and functioning, in the author's words, "more like a sovereign wealth fund than a university endowment."[1]

## Alleged network

The article describes HMC as a "black-box capital node" interlocked with the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]], the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]], the [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller Foundation]] and legacy intelligence partnerships.[1]

Board and executive figures named in the piece include:[1]

- [[michael-chae|Michael Chae]], CFO of [[blackstone|Blackstone]], a senior CFR member and [[asia-society|Asia Society]] board member alongside Saudi intelligence chief [[turki-al-faisal|Prince Turki al-Faisal]].[1]
- [[timothy-barakett|Timothy Barakett]], HMC chair and co-founder of [[atticus-capital|Atticus Capital]] with [[nathaniel-rothschild|Nathaniel Rothschild]].[1]
- [[amy-falls|Amy Falls]], former CIO of [[rockefeller-university|Rockefeller University]], mentored by [[barton-biggs|Barton Biggs]] and connected to [[morgan-stanley|Morgan Stanley]].[1]
- [[rick-slocum|Rick Slocum]], HMC's chief investment officer, who also steers capital for the Rockefeller Foundation and holds membership at [[chatham-house|Chatham House]].[1]
- [[drew-gilpin-faust|Drew Gilpin Faust]], former Harvard president and a [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] board member.[1]
- [[paul-finnegan|Paul Finnegan]], co-CEO of [[madison-dearborn-partners|Madison Dearborn]].[1]

The article characterizes this roster as "not a list of academics" but "a cartel — a financial politburo with global reach."[1]

## Liquidation

HMC's sale of over $1 billion in illiquid private equity assets, reported as portfolio rebalancing, is characterized by the article as panic and as the trigger of a wider "liquidation cascade" across elite finance.[1] The author links the exposure to Executive Orders [[executive-order-13818|13818]] and [[executive-order-13848|13848]], arguing that endowment ties to sanctioned foreign actors, concealed Chinese funding and NGO pipelines leave HMC legally vulnerable.[1]

## The Ivy Web account (2025)

"The Ivy Web," published May 29, 2025, describes HMC as "the engine of globalist liquidity," noting that Harvard's $53 billion endowment "isn't managed by faculty" but by "financial engineers embedded deep in the global asset system."[2] As of 2024, the article states, former and current HMC board members held ties to [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] and [[morgan-stanley|Morgan Stanley]], Blackstone and [[kkr|KKR]], the Council on Foreign Relations, the Trilateral Commission, and the [[ford-foundation|Ford]] and Rockefeller Foundations — a roster it says is documented in board minutes, tax filings and public records of affiliated institutions.[2] These individuals are characterized as shuttling between Davos panels and Pentagon advisory boards.[2]

The article reports that HMC's disposal of more than $1 billion in illiquid holdings involved stakes in vehicles managed by Blackstone, KKR and [[bain-capital|Bain Capital]], and cites January 2024 Business Insider reporting that the liquidation was mirrored by institutional partners including Goldman Sachs and [[blackrock|BlackRock]], which it interprets as a coordinated exit.[2] It also asserts that from 2018 HMC quietly increased exposure to international infrastructure funds, ESG compliance products and sovereign sustainability bonds promoted by the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] as mechanisms of "public-private governance."[2]

## "Wilted Ivy" account

Badlands Media's May 2025 essay "Wilted Ivy" calls the Harvard Management Company, overseer of the university's $53 billion endowment, "not just a collection of skilled investors" but "a conduit of influence, populated by individuals deeply embedded in Wall Street, globalist think tanks, elite policy circles, and philanthropic networks tied to historical centers of power."[3]

The article states that the people entrusted with Harvard's finances are also board members of [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]], [[blackstone|Blackstone]] and [[t-rowe-price|T. Rowe Price]]; partners with the [[rothschild-family|Rothschilds]] and Rockefellers; members of the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]] and [[trilateral-commission|Trilateral Commission]]; regulars at [[world-economic-forum|Davos]] and in some cases [[bilderberg-group|Bilderberg]]; and funders or advisors to the [[ford-foundation|Ford]] and [[rockefeller-foundation|Rockefeller]] foundations, which it describes as historically tied to intelligence soft power.[3] This convergence, the essay argues, creates "what intelligence analysts call a single point of failure," so that destabilizing Harvard ripples pressure through the financial, political and cultural layers of the old order.[3] The liquidation of more than $1 billion in illiquid private equity stakes is read as a distress signal rather than rebalancing.[3]
