# Texas Attorney General's Office

The Office of the Attorney General (OAG) of Texas is the state's chief legal office, led during the period covered here by Attorney General [[ken-paxton|Ken Paxton]].[1] Among its enforcement powers are actions under the Texas Business Organizations Code and the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act, which it can use against nonprofit organizations that commit fraudulent acts in or affecting the state.[1]

## Investigation of Media Matters (November 2023)

On November 20, 2023, coinciding with the filing of [[x-corp|X Corp.]]'s lawsuit against [[media-matters|Media Matters]] in federal court in Fort Worth, the OAG announced it was opening an investigation into the organization for potential fraudulent activity.[1] The office stated that "under the Texas Business Organizations Code and the Deceptive Trade Practices Act, the OAG will vigorously enforce against nonprofits who commit fraudulent acts in or affecting the state of Texas."[1]

Paxton said he was "extremely troubled by the allegations that Media Matters, a radical anti-free speech organization, fraudulently manipulated data on X.com (formerly known as Twitter)," adding: "We are examining the issue closely to ensure that the public has not been deceived by the schemes of radical left-wing organizations who would like nothing more than to limit freedom by reducing participation in the public square."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the OAG investigation and the parallel [[x-corp-v-media-matters|X Corp. v. Media Matters]] suit as part of a "lawsuit cascade" in the [[information-war|Info War]], arguing that such actions represented a template-setting campaign against organizations accused of assaulting the constitutional right to free speech ahead of the 2024 election.[1]

## "Spear of Justice" Portrait of the Office (2025)

An August 2025 Badlands Media essay portrayed the Texas Attorney General's Office under [[ken-paxton|Ken Paxton]] as running a coordinated, multi-domain legal campaign rather than a docket of unrelated cases, striking at pharmaceuticals, technology and AI, coordinated capital, sovereignty, and the [[administrative-state|administrative state]].[2] Beyond those principal fronts, the essay described the office cutting "ligaments" that connect them: prosecuting voter fraud and defending voter ID laws while resisting federal interference in voter roll maintenance; suing over censorship collusion between federal agencies and technology platforms, which the author characterized as intelligence gathering under cover of civil litigation; exposing NGO funding flows at the border, in energy policy, and in corporate governance campaigns; and pursuing financial-interface cases over consumer lending, predatory investment schemes, and insurance practices.[2] It added that coalition suits and amicus briefs extend the office's reach into jurisdictions where it does not directly file, while "legislative locking" converts judicial wins into state statute to make reversal difficult, and identified the AI–bio-data crossover involving health data, wearables, and genomic information as the next emerging target.[2] The essay's author noted that filings and rulings were landing so quickly that the piece had to be revised twice during writing.[2]
