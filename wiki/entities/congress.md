# Congress

The United States Congress appears in "A Glaring Double Standard" as the institution through which post–October 7 campus grievances were converted into legislative action on speech.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

Congress convened hearings at which the presidents of the [[university-of-pennsylvania|University of Pennsylvania]], Harvard, and MIT testified on campus antisemitism; a House press conference facilitated by Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]], at which [[eyal-yakoby|Eyal Yakoby]] spoke, preceded that testimony.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

In May 2024 the U.S. House passed the [[antisemitism-awareness-act|Antisemitism Awareness Act]] by a vote of 320–91, legislation that would codify the [[ihra-definition-of-antisemitism|IHRA definition of antisemitism]] into federal civil rights law and direct the [[department-of-education|Department of Education]] to apply it in campus discrimination investigations.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] notes that among the bill's most prominent congressional supporters were figures he associates with the [[deep-state|interagency blob]] — Elissa Slotkin, Seth Moulton, and Robert Menendez.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

DeLarme's broader argument is that Congress has repeatedly served as the vehicle by which tragedy is converted into restrictions on liberty, citing the Patriot Act after 9/11 as the template.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Appropriations and foreign lobbying

"A Conflict of Interest" treats Congress as the appropriating body through which taxpayer money reaches designated terrorist organisations by way of [[lobbying|lobbying]] groups.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) Its central charge is that Congress was giving taxpayer money to [[isis|ISIS]] through the [[syrian-emergency-task-force|Syrian Emergency Task Force]], the Washington organisation run by [[mouaz-moustafa|Mouaz Moustafa]] that the article identifies as an ISIS lobbying operation, following the same pattern set earlier by the [[libyan-emergency-task-force|Libyan Emergency Task Force]].[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) Such groups, it argues, are established by the enemy specifically to fund the organisations the United States is at war with, and [[john-mccain|McCain]] and many other officials knew whom they represented.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest)

Congress also features as the arena in which recognition of Libya's [[transitional-national-council|Transitional National Council]] was pursued: McCain, ranking Republican on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee and the most senior Republican on the Senate Armed Services Committee, pressed for the rebels to be armed, trained and funded.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) The article notes that [[barack-obama|Obama]] acted in Libya with limited congressional consultation and that McCain defended him for not waiting even a few days for debate.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest) Its verdict on the institution is that politics is one big scam and that both parties are simply stealing taxpayers' money.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-conflict-of-interest)

## Taxing power and career politicians

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] writes that the [[sixteenth-amendment|Sixteenth Amendment]], ratified on February 3, 1913, granted Congress the authority to issue an income tax without apportioning it by population — which he interprets as removing the question from any popular vote.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) Politicians knew, in his account, that Americans would never approve a [[federal-income-tax|federal income tax]] or a [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] System run by bankers, and so voted both in against their constituents' wishes, accepting the loss of re-election because they were "paid handsomely for their treasonous actions."[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

Carlson adds that federal office was never meant to be a full-time job and that the concept of career politicians was appalling to the founders; the federal government was intended to resemble local government today, with a handful of full-time officials and the remainder returning to work as farmers, lawyers, or small business owners.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) In his view federal politicians still work only part time for the government, spending the balance of their time lobbying and cutting deals with whoever will pay most to advance their agendas.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) Changing the majority in Congress or the Speaker of the House accomplishes nothing, he argues, until members actually work for the people rather than lobbyists and foreign enemies.[[3]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

## Overruled on foreign aid and Iraq oversight

"Bad Actors" presents Congress twice as a body sidelined on the movement of taxpayer money abroad. In January 2017 the outgoing [[barack-obama|Obama]] administration formally notified Congress of a $221 million transfer to the [[palestinian-authority|Palestinian Authority]] just hours before [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s inauguration; The Independent reported that Obama had overruled Congress to make the decision, and Secretary of State [[john-kerry|John Kerry]] informed the House only at the last moment.[4]

Earlier, congressional investigation of the missing Iraq reconstruction funds is described as the pressure that prompted [[george-w-bush|George W. Bush]] to appoint [[stuart-w-bowen-jr|Stuart W. Bowen Jr.]] as special inspector general — an appointment Joe Lange interprets as an attempt to make those investigations go away.[4] House Government Reform Committee investigators charged in 2005 that U.S. officials "used virtually no financial controls to account for these enormous cash withdrawals once they arrived in Iraq, and there is evidence of substantial waste, fraud and abuse in the actual spending and disbursement of the Iraqi funds."[4]

## Section 702 renewal fight (2026)

In 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] urged Congress to pass an extension of [[section-702|Section 702]] of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, calling on Republican lawmakers to unify behind the authority ahead of its looming expiration deadline and backing a "clean" extension of roughly eighteen months.[5] The push collided with bipartisan resistance, as civil liberties advocates and lawmakers from both parties demanded reforms such as requiring warrants before searching Americans' data and tightening oversight of intelligence agencies.[5] The debate unfolded amid ongoing concerns about past misuse of the authority, including improper surveillance of Americans and political figures.[5]

Congress also figured in the [[john-brennan|John Brennan]] investigation: the criminal probe into the former [[cia|CIA]] director centered in part on his 2023 congressional testimony about the creation of the 2017 [[intelligence-community-assessment|Intelligence Community Assessment]].[5]

## Removal power, confirmations, and oversight (2026)

Badlands contributor Ashe in America argued in January 2026 that Congress may create offices and prescribe duties but may not transfer executive authority away from the president by insulating officers from removal, a claim advanced in connection with the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] case over [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] Governor [[lisa-cook|Lisa Cook]].[6] Federal judges meanwhile repeatedly rejected the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s efforts to install US attorneys without Senate confirmation, with Judge [[lorna-schofield|Lorna Schofield]] writing that when the executive branch skirts restraints put in place by Congress to investigate political adversaries, it acts without lawful authority.[6]

On the confirmation side, Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] pledged swift action on a nominee for the new fraud-focused assistant attorney general post announced by Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]].[6] Commenting on House oversight of the [[minnesota-somali-fraud-hearings|Minnesota Somali fraud hearings]], Ashe in America criticized the five-minute questioning format as an unserious amount of time for the People's representatives' pursuit of truth, suited only to campaign clips.[6]

## 2026: removal power and election legislation
The [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]]'s 6-3 ruling in June 2026 overturned a 91-year-old precedent that had allowed Congress to limit a president's power to fire Senate-confirmed agency leaders to cases of "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office," substantially narrowing the legislature's ability to insulate executive officers.[8] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that Congress, including what he called the Uniparty GOP, has revealed itself as opposed to the implementation of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s agenda rather than merely slowing it.[8]

Separately, after the Court declined to rewrite absentee ballot deadlines from the bench, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that pressure should be placed on Congress to pass [[clay-higgins|Clay Higgins]]' mail ballot act and the [[save-america-act|SAVE America Act]].[8]

## Staff vetting and conduct rules (2026)

In July 2026 Representative [[kat-cammack|Kat Cammack]] called for an immediate House vote on [[h-res-500|H.Res. 500]], which would require criminal background checks for all House employees and public disclosure of foreign payments, contracts, or nationality in the three years prior to employment.[9] Separately, Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] endorsed expanding the House Code of Official Conduct to bar lawmakers from sexual relationships with any congressional staff rather than only subordinates, broadening a rule adopted in 2018.[9]

Badlands treated both initiatives as evidence against the institution rather than for it. ## Saudi nuclear agreement review

Congress also faced a review of the U.S.–Saudi [[123-agreement|123 Agreement]] on civil nuclear cooperation, which the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration prepared to submit in July 2026.[9] Under the [[atomic-energy-act-of-1954|Atomic Energy Act of 1954]], Congress would have roughly 90 days of continuous session to review, with a joint resolution and a two-thirds veto override required to block it.[9]

## Nuclear agreement review and Canada sanctions (2026)

Congress's statutory role in reviewing civilian nuclear cooperation agreements figured in July 2026 reporting on the [[us-saudi-civilian-nuclear-cooperation-agreement|US–Saudi civilian nuclear cooperation agreement]], which CNN said had not been submitted for the review required after presidential signature; a source suggested that anticipated bipartisan congressional opposition may itself have delayed the president's signature.[10] Democratic lawmakers criticized the framework for omitting the [[international-atomic-energy-agency|IAEA]] Additional Protocol and the U.S. "gold standard" against enrichment.[10]

Separately, Republican Senator [[bernie-moreno|Bernie Moreno]] said he would introduce legislation imposing sanctions, asset freezes, visa restrictions and tariffs on Canada over the [[canadian-wildfire-smoke-crisis-2026|wildfire smoke crisis]], while four Republican House members sent a letter criticizing Canadian forest management policy.[10]

## Iran briefings and Epstein hearings (2026)

In March 2026 Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] delivered a closed-door briefing to Congress on the joint US–Israeli military operation against [[iran|Iran]], telling reporters beforehand that the strikes were preemptive.[13] House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] and other Republican lawmakers said in congressional briefings that [[israel|Israel]] was prepared to attack Iran with or without American support and that US involvement was intended to protect American forces from anticipated retaliation.[13]

In parallel, congressional committees released testimony from [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] as part of inquiries tied to [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]], with Hillary Clinton storming out of her hearing after a congressman posted a photo of her on social media during testimony.[13] The releases coincided with broader congressional debate over executive privilege, compliance with subpoenas, and the authority of the House of Representatives to compel testimony, including in matters involving [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[13]

## SAVE Act passage (February 2026)

In February 2026 the House of Representatives passed the [[save-act|SAVE Act]], requiring documentary proof of US citizenship such as a passport or birth certificate to register to vote in federal elections, with Republicans supporting it as an election-integrity measure and Democrats opposing it as a barrier for eligible voters lacking documentation.[14] The bill then faced significant opposition in the [[united-states-senate|Senate]], where bipartisan support would be needed to overcome a filibuster.[14] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that congressional Republicans had promised for years to save elections with the bill despite holding full control of government for over a year, and argued that legislation from lawmakers who will not acknowledge their own legitimacy problem cannot fix elections absent accountability for past fraud.[14]

## Review of the Iran MOU (June 2026)

President Trump said he would send the interim [[us-iran-memorandum-of-understanding|U.S.–Iran memorandum of understanding]] to Congress for review after its formal signing, but lawmakers of both parties expressed frustration at the lack of information shared with them, noting that no classified briefings or text had been provided despite the agreement's national security implications.[16] Senate Democratic Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] and several Republican senators called for immediate details and briefings, citing the unusual absence of transparency.[16] Commentators including [[mark-levin|Mark Levin]] and Senator [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]] signaled their intent to scrutinize the document under congressional review and, in the view of Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]], to use the powers of Congress to nullify it — a step he argued would expose those legislators as controlled by a foreign power, and one Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] suggested was constitutionally unnecessary.[16]

## Surveillance of Members and Ukraine funding disputes (July 2026)

The [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]]'s July 2026 criminal referral of former Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] disclosed that during the [[arctic-frost|Arctic Frost]] probe Biden-Harris DOJ investigators unconstitutionally subpoenaed the phone records of more than a dozen Republican Members of Congress and used gag orders to conceal that the records had been obtained.[19] The referral highlighted potential violations of the Speech or Debate Clause, which protects congressional communications, as well as 18 U.S.C. § 1001.[19] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that legislators became authentically outraged only because their own messages were read, noting that the institution's members had created, funded and defended the surveillance state on the assumption they were exempt from it.[19]

Separately, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] withheld $400 million in funding that Congress had promised to Ukrainian President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]], saying it would not be available "until the next president is in office."[19] Badlands commentary described this as exposing what it called brazen congressional attempts to launder and steal US tax dollars through [[ukraine|Ukraine]], and urged Inspector General [[robert-storch|Robert Storch]] to release a report documenting the practice.[19]

## Paralysis on artificial intelligence (March 2026)

By March 2026 Congress had held hearings and introduced several bills relating to artificial intelligence and employment, but had enacted no legislation addressing how the technology could affect workers.[20] Bipartisan efforts existed — Senators [[josh-hawley|Josh Hawley]] and [[mark-warner|Mark Warner]] introduced legislation in November 2025 to collect data on AI-related layoffs and hiring slowdowns — but nothing had passed even as the [[us-department-of-labor|Labor Department]] reported 92,000 jobs shed in February 2026 and 63 percent of Americans told YouGov and The Economist they expected AI to reduce employment.[20]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] attributed the paralysis to the composition of the institution, writing that "Congress is comprised of the stupidest among us … the best products by district — not thinkers, but actors," and arguing that only real elections could produce lawmakers capable of solving the AI dilemma.[20] She described doing nothing as "only an option for the stupidest among us who have no problem that digital chains and centralized exploitation turn the American people into real human slaves."[20]

## Trump's legislative ultimatum (March 2026)

On March 8, 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced he would not sign any other legislation until Congress passed the [[save-america-act|SAVE America Act]], writing on [[truth-social|Truth Social]] that the bill "must go to the front of the line."[20] A version of the measure had passed the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]] on February 11, 2026 by 218–213 and awaited Senate consideration.[20]

## January 6, 2025 certification security designation

In September 2024 the federal government for the first time declared that the congressional certification of the presidential vote — scheduled for January 6, 2025 — would be treated as a [[national-special-security-event|national special security event]], an acknowledgment that a once-routine part of the democratic process now carried special risk.[21] The designation by Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] placed the session on the same security footing as the Super Bowl or the U.N. General Assembly and authorized measures aimed at preventing a reprise of the 2021 Capitol riot by supporters of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[21]

## Ukraine and Israel supplemental funding (November 2023)

By November 2023 Congress had been dragging its feet on President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s latest supplemental funding requests for [[ukraine-aid-funding-controversy|Ukraine aid]], with wrangling over how much would be sent to Israel versus how much would be set aside for Ukraine.[22] The stalemate coincided with unannounced emergency trips to Kiev by Defense Secretary [[lloyd-austin|Lloyd Austin]] and [[cia|CIA]] Director [[william-burns|William Burns]], and with the [[pentagon|Pentagon]]'s rhetorical shift toward Ukraine's "long-term defense requirements" as its counteroffensive faltered.[22]

## "Duma" comparison and second-term deference (2025)

In October 2025 former White House chief strategist [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] told The Wall Street Journal that the Republican-controlled Congress had become the equivalent of Russia's largely ceremonial Duma, unable or unwilling to check President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in his second term.[23] Bannon attributed the shift to Trump's four years "in the wilderness" between 2021 and 2025, which he said hardened the president's resolve — "he's jackhammering away on levels you haven't seen before."[23] The same reporting described senior administration advisers joking about ruling Capitol Hill with an "iron fist," and cited House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] attending the 40th birthday party of Deputy White House Chief of Staff [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] in Washington on August 23 as emblematic of congressional Republicans bending to the president's will.[23]

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] rejected even the ceremonial framing, arguing that Congress and the Senate are "totally illegitimate" and comparing them to puppet legislatures the regime props up abroad to oppose anti-regime leaders, adding that "Congress is so pathetic that the White House jokes about it in private."[23]

## October 2023 paralysis

Three weeks of Republican infighting following the removal of Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] brought Congress to a halt in October 2023, with the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]] unable to conduct legislative business under Speaker Pro Tempore [[patrick-mchenry|Patrick McHenry]] and unable to elect a successor after the withdrawal of [[jim-jordan|Jim Jordan]].[24] A bipartisan group proposed temporarily expanding McHenry's authority through January so the chamber could vote on funding measures, but the plan collapsed after Republican objections.[24] The paralysis coincided with the Biden administration's $105 billion national security supplemental request for Ukraine, Israel, Taiwan and border security, which OMB Director [[shalanda-young|Shalanda Young]] urged Congress to address "as part of a comprehensive, bipartisan agreement."[24] Badlands commentary argued that the episode exposed [[uniparty|Uniparty]] war hawks and demonstrated to the electorate the positives of an inept and inert Congress, with bipartisan agreement on one thing only: "NO NEW WARS."[24]

## September 2024 stopgap spending agreement

Congressional leaders announced an agreement on Sunday, September 22, 2024, on a short-term spending bill funding federal agencies for about three months, averting a possible partial government shutdown at the start of the new budget year on October 1 and deferring final decisions until after the November election.[25] The measure generally funded agencies at existing levels but added $231 million to bolster the [[us-secret-service|Secret Service]] following two assassination attempts against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], along with additional money for the presidential transition.[25] House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] had linked temporary funding to a mandate requiring states to demand proof of citizenship for voter registration, at the urging of his conference's most conservative members, but abandoned that approach to reach agreement despite Trump's insistence that there be no stop-gap without the voting requirement.[25]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the "adults in the room" occupying the halls of Congress spent more time capitulating to the systemic status quo than acting on the cascading crises facing the country — from Russia–Ukraine and the Middle East to the Taiwan Strait — and suggested that this paralysis was itself the point, setting the stage for Trump's return.[25]

## Appropriations and the November 2023 shutdown deadline

Congress must pass twelve appropriation bills to fund the government through its fiscal year; when it fails to do so it relies on stopgap [[continuing-resolution|continuing resolutions]].[26] In early November 2023, with funding due to expire on November 17, Congress faced another shutdown deadline while Republicans controlling the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]] showed no sign of agreement on a funding plan.[26] Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]], the top Republican in Congress, said members wanted to avoid a shutdown because "It's a dangerous time around the world right now."[26] House Republicans simultaneously worried about being forced to accept a competing measure from the Democratic-led [[us-senate|Senate]].[26]

Congress also featured in the same period's fight over Israel policy, including a 2021 bill to replenish Israel's [[iron-dome|Iron Dome]] missile defense system and an October 2023 resolution to stand with Israel — both of which Rep. [[rashida-tlaib|Rashida Tlaib]] voted against, drawing a six-figure ad campaign from [[democratic-majority-for-israel|Democratic Majority for Israel]].[26]

## The 2024 Border-Ukraine Supplemental Fight

In January 2024 Congress was the arena for a bipartisan Senate effort to pair border enforcement measures with Ukraine aid inside President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s $110 billion emergency funding request covering Ukraine, Israel, immigration enforcement and other national security needs.[27] Senate Republicans had initially demanded that border policy changes be attached to the request, but the emerging deal came under fire from Republicans including [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], who called it a political "gift" to Democrats, and House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] indicated it could be "dead on arrival" in the House.[27] Reporting at the time noted that the diminishing prospects for passage carried far-reaching consequences for U.S. allies abroad, especially Ukraine, and that Biden's embrace of the deal amid Republican resistance could mark an election-year shift in the politics of immigration.[27] Badlands commentary from [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that Congress needed to pass no legislation whatsoever, since the executive already possessed full authority to secure the border, and characterized both parties' pursuit of a deal as [[uniparty|Uniparty]] behavior.[27]

## July 2025: Powell investigation demand and the Big, Beautiful Bill

In July 2025, [[federal-housing-finance-agency|FHFA]] Director [[bill-pulte|Bill Pulte]] publicly asked Congress to investigate [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] Chair [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] over what he called deceptive Senate testimony about the central bank's headquarters renovation, saying it was enough to warrant removal "for cause."[28] _The Hill_ described the demand as an unprecedented campaign by the head of one independent financial regulatory agency to oust the head of another.[28]

At the same time both chambers were consumed by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s domestic policy package, the [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill]], which cleared the [[us-senate|Senate]] on July 1 and faced a difficult procedural vote in the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]] on the night of July 2 ahead of a self-imposed July 4 deadline.[28] Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that no member of Congress has a mandate to govern comparable to Trump's, that most are unknown even in their own districts, and that all 435 House members should support passage.[28]

## Certification of the 2024 election

On Monday, January 6, 2025, Congress certified [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s election victory, completing a process that four years earlier had been violently interrupted when rioters stormed the Capitol in an attempt to halt certification of his defeat to [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[29] The proceeding was uneventful; according to the Washington Times, "it was the first time congressional Democrats did not raise a challenge to a Republican winner of the presidential election since certifying George H.W. Bush's victory in 1989."[29] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the session as political and procedural theater, arguing that Democrat drama is the norm and the quiet certification the exception; see [[2024-election-certification|the 2024 election certification]].[29]

Congressional action was also identified as potentially the only route to reverse [[biden-offshore-drilling-ban|Biden's offshore drilling ban]], which used a law with no clear mechanism for a future president to undo it.[29]

## Republican anxiety over the MAHA report (May 2025)

An expected May 2025 report from Health and Human Services Secretary [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]] on the causes of chronic disease in children was framed as a test of whether congressional Republicans could get along with a health secretary keen on regulating farm and food companies.[30] Republican lawmakers representing agriculture and food-manufacturing districts had warned Kennedy to lay off and worried the [[make-america-healthy-again-commission|MAHA Commission]] report would target pesticides and food dyes.[30] GOP Rep. Mark Alford of Missouri stressed that his state's 88,000 farms needed "the tools they need to have the most effective crops."[30] Most Republicans in Congress had endorsed [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s decision to let Kennedy "go wild" on health care.[30]

In the same period, the [[house-rules-committee|House Rules Committee]] narrowly advanced the [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill]] amid hard-line budget hawks' objections over the $36 trillion national debt, and the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] opened an inquiry into [[pfizer|Pfizer]]'s COVID-19 vaccine trial timing.[30]

## Notification of armed conflict with drug cartels (October 2025)

In early October 2025 the Trump administration sent a confidential notice to several congressional committees stating that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had determined the United States was engaged in a formal "armed conflict" with drug cartels labeled terrorist organizations, and that suspected smugglers for those groups were "unlawful combatants."[31] The notice, obtained by *The New York Times*, supplied additional legal rationale for three military strikes on boats in the Caribbean Sea that killed all 17 people aboard.[31] Legal specialists said the [[armed-conflict-designation-against-drug-cartels|designation]] cemented the president's claim to extraordinary wartime powers exercised largely outside congressional authorization.[31]

Congress's failure to agree on a new budget before the midnight deadline on October 1, 2025 triggered a government shutdown, during which funding for the [[special-supplemental-nutrition-program-for-women-infants-and-children|WIC]] program was placed on hold and legislators continued to be paid — a contrast Badlands commentary described as "gross."[31]


## Fiscal warnings and the 2025 tariff dispute

In April 2025 former New York mayor [[michael-bloomberg|Michael Bloomberg]] argued that Congress should take urgent measures to restore fiscal control rather than contemplate further borrowing, warning that the United States was heading for a "fiscal breakdown."[32] Citing [[congressional-budget-office|Congressional Budget Office]] projections that the deficit and federal debt would keep growing, he wrote that the federal government was spending roughly $7 trillion annually while collecting only $5 trillion in taxes — a deficit a little over 6 percent of GDP at close to full employment — and that unless Congress changed course "there'll be a reckoning, and it will be grim."[32]

Congress also figured in the historical background of the third-term debate revived by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]: after [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin D. Roosevelt]]'s four elections, Congress concluded that his long presidency should remain a one-off exception and in 1947 approved the [[twenty-second-amendment-third-term-loophole|Twenty-Second Amendment]], sending it to the state legislatures, which completed ratification by 1951.[32] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] treated congressional activity of the period — a taskforce on government secrets, a congressional chest-feeding room, and a Senate vote defying Trump's stance on Canada that would itself require his signature — as distractions from the administration's transformation agenda.[32]

## June 2023 items

In June 2023, the [[house-oversight-committee|House Oversight Committee]] introduced the [[ace-act|ACE Act]], election-integrity legislation for the District of Columbia grounded in Congress's constitutional oversight responsibilities for the federal district, presented at a joint hearing with the House Administration Committee on June 7.[33] Separately, a congressional staffer worked with [[united-states-capitol-police|Capitol Police]] to stop the [[rushingbrook-childrens-choir|Rushingbrook Children's Choir]] from singing the national anthem inside the Capitol Building, an episode [[human-events|Human Events]] called "a stain on our national character and the people's House."[33] Badlands commentary also faulted Congress for devoting less funding and attention to pedophile networks than to January 6 prosecutions.[33]

## Shutdown impasse, September 2025

Congressional leaders failed to reach a funding agreement with President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] at a private [[white-house|White House]] meeting on 29 September 2025, leaving the [[october-2025-government-shutdown|October 2025 shutdown]] likely to begin at 12:01 a.m. on 1 October.[34] Republicans pushed a stopgap extending current funding levels to 21 November, while Democrats sought changes mostly related to health care access and subsidies; House Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] reported that "significant and meaningful differences remain."[34] Pentagon contingency guidance noted that without a continuing resolution approved by Congress and signed by the president, the government would shut down.[34] [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] dismissed the entire episode as political theatre, writing that "All of congress sucks. They're all illegitimate."[34]

## Letter threatening UN funding (October 2024)

In late October 2024, more than one hundred bipartisan members of Congress wrote to United Nations Secretary-General [[antonio-guterres|António Guterres]] warning that any downgrade in Israel's status or standing at the UN General Assembly would result in a corresponding downgrade of U.S. financial, material and political support for the [[united-nations|United Nations]].[35] Representatives [[mike-lawler|Mike Lawler]] and [[jared-moskowitz|Jared Moskowitz]] spearheaded the letter, which said Congress had taken note of numerous UN actions "aimed to delegitimize Israel's right to self-defense" and concluded that "the UN is not a neutral party but one that has definitively taken sides against Israel," reminding Guterres that U.S. contributions account for one-third of the body's collective budget.[35]

Badlands commentary treated the letter as evidence of a deeper structural problem, arguing that Congress has been beholden to the Zionist lobby since the era of the [[un-partition-plan-for-palestine|UN Partition Plan for Palestine]] and is prepared to bankrupt the nation to advance that agenda.[35] While welcoming any defunding of the UN as a possible silver lining, the writer asked how long it would be before Congress went so far as to declare war on the UN in Israel's defense — a possibility he judged more likely under firm Republican control, especially with compromised "Deep State Republicans" seeking to undermine a Trump presidency.[35] He also raised the question of dual citizenship among senior officials, citing [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]]'s remark to [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] that he came to Israel after October 7 "not as Secretary of State, but as a Jew."[35]

Separately, Congress featured in the period's news through the release of [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] after four months' imprisonment for [[contempt-of-congress|contempt of Congress]] arising from his refusal to comply with subpoenas from the [[january-6-committee|House select committee]] investigating the Capitol breach.[35]

## FY2026 NDAA and troop-level mandates (December 2025)

In December 2025, House Republican leaders scheduled a vote on the 3,086-page fiscal year 2026 [[national-defense-authorization-act|National Defense Authorization Act]] for late Wednesday afternoon, having released the text only the preceding Sunday evening.[36] Hard-line conservatives objected publicly to the inclusion of Ukraine funding and the omission of a [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] digital currency ban, while leadership touted provisions targeting diversity and equity programs in the ranks to hold the conference together on the procedural vote.[36] Badlands criticism focused on the bill's use as a vehicle for unrelated domestic policy, noting that the [[road-to-housing-act|ROAD to Housing Act]] — said to contain 28 further acts — had been folded into the defense authorization.[36]

The same legislation moved to limit the [[pentagon|Pentagon]]'s ability to reduce forces abroad, requiring the retention of 76,000 troops in Europe and 28,500 in South Korea unless the department could assure Congress that reductions would not compromise security, and mandating that the Supreme Allied Commander Europe post remain held by an American general.[36] It also allocated $400 million for Ukrainian security assistance with conditions for reclaiming military equipment.[36] Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] rejected the notion that Congress possesses such power over the disposition of forces, urging readers to "mock these illegitimate usurpers at every opportunity."[36]

## Senate health care divisions and blue slips (December 2025)

Three days before a scheduled vote on a Democratic proposal to extend expiring Obamacare subsidies, Senate Republicans had not coalesced behind any single alternative, with leaders content to let members freelance even as Democrats and some in their own ranks complained about the absence of direction.[36] Democrats said their entire 47-member caucus would back a three-year extension, while any Republican counter-proposal appeared likely to fall short of the conference's 53 votes and expose internal divisions in a side-by-side vote.[36]

Separately, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] blamed Senate Republicans and the chamber's blue slip tradition — which lets home-state senators veto nominees to district courts and U.S. attorney offices — for stalled U.S. attorney nominations, after [[alina-habba|Alina Habba]] announced her resignation as New Jersey's top federal prosecutor.[36]

## FY2025 appropriations and the March 14 shutdown deadline

By late February 2025, Congress was almost five months behind in passing a final spending agreement for fiscal year 2025 and had been relying on a series of continuing resolutions to fund the federal government and avoid a shutdown.[37] Senate Appropriations Vice Chair [[patty-murray|Patty Murray]] acknowledged that another short-term [[continuing-resolution|continuing resolution]] would likely be needed to extend the March 14 deadline, at which point lawmakers would have to pass a final spending bill, enact a further CR, or face a partial shutdown.[37]

## Netanyahu's July 2024 joint address

Congress hosted Israeli prime minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] for an address to a joint meeting on July 24, 2024, in which he dismissed war crimes criticism from the [[united-nations|United Nations]], human rights groups and the [[international-criminal-court|International Criminal Court]] as "utter nonsense" and pressed lawmakers to expedite billions of dollars in U.S. weapons transfers.[38] Badlands commentator [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] recorded that the 52-minute speech drew 58 bipartisan standing ovations — a record for any congressional address of any length — surpassing Netanyahu's own 2015 mark of 28 ovations in 48 minutes, with [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]]'s 2022 address (21 ovations in 23 minutes) third.[38] That Congress could be "completely united" on a single issue for 52 minutes was itself, in his telling, some other kind of record, and evidence that the institution is "bought and paid for by foreign interests."[38] Thousands of demonstrators marched around the city during the address, and Capitol Police joined D.C. police in making fifteen arrests.[38]

## Ukraine and Israel aid linkage (October 2023)

In October 2023 the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration was reported to be considering merging its request for additional Ukraine war spending with new aid for [[israel|Israel]], an approach described as intended to "jam the far right" — a reference to Republicans in Congress who oppose the Ukraine proxy war but are staunch supporters of Israel.[39] No final decision had been made at the time, and arms were already flowing to Israel under existing authorities.[39] See the [[2023-us-arms-surge-to-israel|2023 US arms surge to Israel]].[39]

## December 2025: Venezuela briefings and the warrior dividend

[[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]] said in December 2025 that he had been told by a member of Congress that lawmakers were briefed that a war with [[venezuela|Venezuela]] was coming and would be announced in President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s address to the nation on the evening of December 17; the address made no mention of Venezuela.[40] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that Congress really was briefed as though an invasion were imminent and then "rug-pulled," consistent with his view that Trump is running a psychological warfare operation against the Republican Party.[40]

The same address announced a $1,776 "warrior dividend" for roughly 1.45 million service members, totaling about $2.5 billion drawn from tariff revenue; it was unclear how the administration would appropriate the funds without prior congressional approval.[40]

## December 2023 surveillance extension

On December 14, 2023, Congress approved a short-term extension of the nation's warrantless surveillance powers, deferring to the new year any decision on how to reform the law.[41] The measure — extending [[section-702|Section 702]] of the [[foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act|Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act]] until mid-April — was included in the [[national-defense-authorization-act|defense policy bill]] headed to the president's desk after House passage, and drew objections from several conservative lawmakers who resented its attachment to must-pass legislation.[41] Badlands commentary noted that 118 House Republicans opposed the measure and characterized the bipartisan majority vote as proof of a [[uniparty|uniparty]] whose members fundraise against surveillance abuse while voting to continue it.[41]

## Trump's claim of strike authority without a declaration of war (October 2025)

On Thursday, October 23, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] insisted he could continue launching strikes against alleged drug traffickers abroad without Congress first passing an official declaration of war, saying "I'm not going to necessarily ask for a declaration of war."[42] He suggested his administration would soon begin targeting alleged cartel members inside countries such as [[venezuela|Venezuela]] in addition to striking alleged drug boats in international waters, and said he would notify Congress before beginning any operations on land while contending the plan would face no pushback from lawmakers.[42]

Badlands commentary noted the same week that Congress remained deadlocked over the [[october-2025-government-shutdown|October 2025 government shutdown]], with [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] proposing that members of Congress be paid the minimum wage.[42]

## Late January 2024 activity

On January 31, 2024 congressional panels featured in several major news stories at once.[43] [[fbi|FBI]] Director [[christopher-wray|Christopher Wray]] told the [[house-select-committee-on-the-chinese-communist-party|House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party]] that the Bureau had dismantled the China-backed [[volt-typhoon|Volt Typhoon]] router-hacking operation aimed at American water, power, pipeline, and transportation infrastructure.[43] The [[house-homeland-security-committee|House Homeland Security Committee]] voted 18–15 along party lines to approve two articles of impeachment against Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]], including a charge that he misled Congress about having "operational control" of the border.[43] The [[senate-judiciary-committee|Senate Judiciary Committee]] spent four hours grilling social media chief executives over child safety, with [[mark-zuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]] apologizing directly to bereaved families; despite apparent bipartisan consensus, the hearing left unanswered whether Congress would impose new regulations on the platforms.[43]

## 2025 elimination of public broadcasting funding

Congress eliminated federal funding for the Corporation for Public Broadcasting in 2025, with the House approving a White House request to claw back $1.1 billion in already appropriated funds in June and the Senate Appropriations Committee's 2026 bill zeroing out CPB funding for the first time in over 50 years.[45]

## Legal definition as an assembly of sovereignties

A November 2023 Badlands Media essay by [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]] cites the entry for "Congress" on page 373 of the 4th Edition of [[blacks-law-dictionary|Black's Law Dictionary]] (1951), which defines the word in international law as "an assembly of envoys, commissioners, deputies, etc., from different sovereignties who meet to concert measures for their common good, or to adjust their mutual concerns."[49] The author treats this definition as evidence that the several American states retain a national character, since an assembly of envoys presupposes distinct sovereigns rather than administrative subdivisions of one nation.[49] On that reading, Congress is the deliberative organ of a [[union-of-states|union of sovereign states]] rather than the legislature of a unitary nation.[49]
