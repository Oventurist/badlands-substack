# Samuel Morse

Samuel F. B. Morse was an American painter and inventor trained by [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] at the [[royal-academy-of-fine-arts|English Royal Academy of Fine Arts]], and is described in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as one of the students who most masterfully carried forward West's method of embedding Platonic ironies and anomalies in art.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Science and communications

The discoveries of [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] in electricity are said to have given rise directly to Morse's advances in electrical communication, achieved with the help of a variety of scientists around the Humboldt brothers including Carl F. Gauss, Wilhelm Weber and Ampère.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) The article groups Morse with Franklin, Ampère, Weber and Joseph Henry as representing a school of thought in the sciences that it argues must be revived to overcome the paradoxes of twentieth-century standard model cosmology and quantum mechanics.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Foreign Conspiracy Against the Liberties of the United States

Morse later exposed what the article characterises as a Jesuitical operation in the Americas in his 1841 book *Foreign Conspiracy Against the Liberties of the United States*, describing Prince Metternich's Holy Alliance and its deployment of [[jesuits|Jesuits]] throughout the Americas to undo the American Revolution, writing that they came "in the shape of hundreds of Jesuits and priests; a class of men notorious for their intrigue and political arts, and who have a complete military organization through the United States."1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Morse as artist and cultural warrior

A second Badlands article by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] argues that Morse is best understood not through his career as an inventor, which began in 1833, but through the first two decades of his adult life, which were devoted primarily to painting and to the institutions he founded in painting and photography.[2] Morse stands at the centre of Christian Schussele's 1862 canvas [[men-of-progress|*Men of Progress*]], his right hand on the table beside the telegraph apparatus.[2] The [[telegraph|telegraph]] itself was developed with [[joseph-henry|Joseph Henry]], Alexander Dallas Bache, Alexander von Humboldt, Carl Friedrich Gauss and Wilhelm Weber, building on the electrical discoveries of [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]]; Morse declared in 1833 that "if the presence of electricity can be made visible in any part of the circuit, I see no reason why intelligence may not be transmitted instantaneously by electricity," and became known as the "Lightning Man."[2]

From 1827 to 1828 Morse toured the United States delivering a lecture series justifying a new idea of painting aligned with the ideals of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, arguing that the foundations of a just taste in the fine arts should be laid "not in authority however ancient, but in the never changing principles of nature," since "authority in all its forms usurps the place of truth and reason."[2] He held that it was possible, "by the diffusion of the highest moral and intellectual cultivation through every class," to raise the lower classes in refinement — a rejection of the two-culture system by which hereditary elites had kept a separate elite music, literature and painting apart from a slave culture for the masses.[2] His *Niagara Falls from Table Rock* is cited as evidence of his technical gifts.[2]

## The battle with the American Academy of Fine Arts

Morse founded the [[national-academy-of-design|National Academy of the Arts of Design]] in New York, serving as its president from its founding until 1845, with [[william-dunlap|William Dunlap]] as co-founder and vice president, in direct opposition to the [[american-academy-of-fine-arts|American Academy of Fine Arts]] of [[john-trumbull|John Trumbull]] and [[robert-livingston|Robert Livingston]].[2] Against Trumbull's claim that artists "are necessarily dependent upon the protection of the rich and the great," Morse replied that no man of independent feeling could fail to "feel disgust at language like this," and denounced the rival body as an "anti-republican institution" whose aristocratic plant could not grow in the American climate.[2] [[pierre-beaudry|Pierre Beaudry]] records that Trumbull was forced to resign by popular consent and that the Academy of Fine Arts disintegrated within months, after which the National Academy became the engine of the [[hudson-river-school|Hudson River School]].[2]

## Paris, Lafayette and the Gallery of the Louvre

Brought to France by [[james-fenimore-cooper|James Fenimore Cooper]], Morse spent months in the Louvre from 1831 producing [[gallery-of-the-louvre|*Gallery of the Louvre*]], a tableau containing thirty-seven reproductions of the museum's greatest works, which toured the United States to acclaim.[2] He had earlier been commissioned, through Cooper, to paint the [[marquis-de-lafayette|Marquis de Lafayette]] during the latter's 1826 American tour, embedding visual ironies — gathering dark clouds, judging busts of Franklin and [[george-washington|George Washington]], and an unresolved ambiguity between motion and stasis — that expressed Lafayette's inability to choose between aristocracy and republicanism.[2] Morse, Cooper and Lafayette lived several doors from one another in Paris, working, planning and playing music together, Morse on the flute.[2] Morse was among the crowd calling for Lafayette to declare a republic in 1830, and afterwards wrote a manifesto for his own academy stating that "we have taken the English Royal Academy for our model, as far as the different circumstances of form of government and state of taste will admit" — a paradox the article attributes to his training in England during the War of 1812 under [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]]'s Royal Academy circle.[2]
