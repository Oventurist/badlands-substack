# Boniface VIII

**Pope Boniface VIII** was the Roman pontiff who issued the bull [[unam-sanctam|Unam Sanctam]] in 1302.[1]

In the Badlands Media essay [[soul-governance|Soul Governance]], Boniface appears as the originator of the legal architecture the article calls soul theft.[1] The essay quotes his declaration that "every human creature [is] subject to the Roman pontiff" and insists that he "was not speaking symbolically" but advancing a claim of legal jurisdiction over persons.[1]

That claim, in the essay's account, was subsequently given effect through [[cestui-que-vie-trust|Cestui Que Vie trusts]], which presumed the individual lost at sea and reduced him to cargo under maritime law, with the [[vatican|Vatican]] serving as the port of registry.[1] The essay credits the [[jesuits|Jesuits]] with maintaining and elaborating the resulting system through coded oaths and the transformation of courts into quasi-ecclesiastical institutions.[1]

The article treats Boniface's bull as the ancestor of contemporary biometric and digital identity regimes, which it describes as "a digital version of Unam Sanctam, restated in code."[1]

Conventional historiography places Boniface VIII's pontificate at the height of the conflict between papal and royal authority, particularly with Philip IV of France, and reads Unam Sanctam as an assertion of papal supremacy in that dispute rather than as a mechanism of personal ownership.[1]
