# Au10tix

Au10tix is a veteran Israeli identity-verification company that supplies identity-verification services to large online platforms, most prominently X (formerly Twitter).[1] In June 2024 the company became the subject of a social-media controversy after accounts described in Israeli business media as "anti-Israel influencers" demanded that X terminate its partnership with the firm.[1]

## The June 2024 X controversy

Activists on X alleged that Au10tix's founder, Ron Atzmon, and its chief executive, Dan Yerushalmi, were veterans of Shin Bet, Mossad and the IDF's Unit 8200 signals-intelligence formation, and that the company allegedly passed the details of X users to Israeli security authorities.[1] The Israeli outlet Globes reported that these specific claims were factually false: Atzmon is an Israel Navy veteran, while Yerushalmi served as an adjutant in the IDF Communications Corps and previously worked as a vice-president of sales at Check Point.[1] Globes further reported that user information for major customers such as X, Google, PayPal and Uber is stored on Microsoft servers located in the United States, the United Kingdom and Japan.[1]

The story, which might otherwise have subsided, escalated when X owner Elon Musk posted "I will investigate this," reigniting the controversy around the company.[1] As of the report, Musk had not contacted Au10tix directly.[1] A user survey run on X by the American Marine veteran and activist Lucas Gage found that 92 percent of some 50,000 respondents favored removing the Israeli company's service; the intervention of the Israeli blogger Ella Kenan brought in Israeli voters who slightly improved the result for Au10tix.[1]

## Badlands commentary and the ICTS lineage

Badlands Media contributor GhostofBasedPatrickHenry used the episode to argue that the "Digital Age" is in reality a "Surveillance Age" underwritten by a matrix of Israeli software and technology firms whose products, in his framing, are designed to violate the Bill of Rights.[1] He compared Au10tix to NSO Group's Pegasus spyware and to the cybersecurity firm CHEQ, which met with Musk over bot removal on X.[1]

The commentary noted that while the Shin Bet/Mossad/8200 claims about Au10tix's executives have been questioned, Au10tix's parent company, ICTS International, was founded by former members of Shin Bet, Israel's internal security service.[1] Au10tix was also described as providing solutions that meet the compliance requirements of Section 326 of the Patriot Act, tying the firm's commercial model to post-9/11 identity-verification mandates.[1]

## Replacement by Stripe (June 2024)

By late June 2024, [[x|X]] had replaced Au10tix as its identity-verification vendor, a development reported via BRICS-focused accounts on X and covered in the Badlands News Brief of June 27, 2024.[2] [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]], who had first written about the Au10tix controversy on June 5, recalled that anons had raised the alarm that the third party contracted to verify users' identities for payment processing was created and owned by Israeli intelligence officers.[2] The concern was acute for users critical of Israel on the platform, because verification required a government-issued photo ID and the user's home address, and because [[mossad|Mossad]] has been known to target political dissidents who create problems for the Israeli government.[2]

In this later account the firm's parent company is identified as [[i-sec|I-SEC]], described as an Israeli cybersecurity company founded by Mossad agents that specializes exclusively in airport security, and alleged to have been responsible for the security failures of 9/11 while also profiting from the resulting [[patriot-act|Patriot Act]].[2] Au10tix's stated raison d'être was compliance with Section 326 of that Act, which requires financial institutions to obtain, verify, and record identifying information for customers opening or modifying accounts.[2] After [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] promised to "look into it," he appeared to end the X–I-SEC/Au10tix relationship, with verification transferred to the payment processor [[stripe|Stripe]], which already held the same ID data through its wire-transfer processing role.[2]
