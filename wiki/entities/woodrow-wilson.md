# Woodrow Wilson

Woodrow Wilson was the U.S. President who signed into law the two 1913 measures at the center of the article's argument.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

Wilson signed the [[revenue-act-of-1913|Revenue Act of 1913]] on October 3, 1913, a law that dramatically lowered tariffs, implemented an income tax, and allowed for the establishment of the [[internal-revenue-service|Internal Revenue Service]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) The bill was moved through the House of Representatives by Representative [[oscar-underwood|Oscar Underwood]] and won approval in the [[united-states-senate|United States Senate]] after extensive lobbying by the Wilson administration.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

On December 23, 1913, Wilson signed the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act]], creating the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve System]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) The article concludes that with these moves Wilson "had successfully enslaved every American to the fiat monetary system," obliging every American to pay income tax while their money continued to be devalued.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

## From Princeton to the Federal Reserve

In 1908, while president of Princeton University, Woodrow Wilson hailed [[jp-morgan|J.P. Morgan]] as a hero of the [[panic-of-1907|Panic of 1907]], stating that "all this trouble could be averted if we appointed a committee of six or seven public spirited men like J.P. Morgan, to handle the affairs of our country."[4]

When the [[aldrich-plan|Aldrich bill]] stalled, the bankers switched their attention to the Democrats and began heavily financing Wilson as the party's presidential nominee; Wall Street banker [[bernard-baruch|Bernard Baruch]] was put in charge of the "Wilson project," and historian James Perloff wrote that Baruch brought Wilson to Democratic Party headquarters in New York in 1912 "leading him like one would a poodle on a string," where Wilson received an "indoctrination course."[4] After Wilson's election on November 5, Morgan, [[paul-warburg|Paul Warburg]], Baruch and others advanced the Glass-Owen Bill establishing the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve System]].[4]

## Later Regrets

By 1916 Wilson had begun to realize the gravity of what he had done, stating that the United States had become "one of the worst ruled, one of the most completely controlled governments in the civilized world — no longer a government of free opinion ... but a government by the opinion and duress of a small group of dominant men," and that some of the biggest men in commerce and manufacture were afraid of a power "so organized, so subtle, so watchful, so interlocked, so complete, so pervasive" that they dared not speak above their breath in condemnation of it.[4] Just before his death he said: "I am a most unhappy man. I have unwittingly ruined my country. A great industrial nation is controlled by its system of credit. Our system of credit is concentrated. The growth of the nation, therefore, and all our activities are in the hands of a few men."[4]

## Colonel House and the Federal Reserve Act

A March 2023 Badlands Media essay on [[trust-law|trust law]] and the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] argued that, faced with substantial opposition to the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act]], international bankers assigned a "handler" to Wilson in the guise of an adviser — [[edward-mandell-house|Colonel Edward Mandell House]] — who exerted what the author described as a hypnotic influence over the president in order to secure passage.[2] The essay states that Wilson later bitterly regretted the outcome.[2]

Its documentary basis is a passage from Congressman [[louis-t-mcfadden|Louis T. McFadden]] in the Congressional Record of June 10, 1932, quoting a letter House wrote on December 23, 1913 to what McFadden called his "hidden master in Wall Street."[2] McFadden contended that, left free of executive pressure, Congress would not have passed the Act, and that it became law because Wilson stood firm under the guidance of New York financiers acting through House.[2]

## Origin of the "America First" slogan

In "The Overdrive Timeline," Erik Carlson wrote that the term "America First" was first coined by Wilson — whom he calls a Deep State puppet — during his campaign for re-election, on a promise to keep the United States out of the First World War.[5] Carlson notes that immediately after his victory Wilson brought the country into the war, and contrasts this with the [[america-first-committee|America First Committee]] formed in 1940 to keep the United States out of the Second World War.[5]
