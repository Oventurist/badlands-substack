# United States Department of Defense

The United States Department of Defense (DOD) is the federal executive department responsible for the American military. In "America Has a CIA-Run Economy," it appears chiefly as the parent institution of the research bureaucracy that, in the author's account, seeded the modern American technology industry.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## DARPA and the taxpayer-funded technology pipeline

The article identifies the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency ([[darpa|DARPA]]) as "a research and development agency of the United States Department of Defense that focuses on developing new technologies for the military."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy) It stresses that US tax dollars are used to create this technology, which then "often finds its way into American society and the American economy," citing the internet as probably the biggest and most important such technology — one that ended up in every American's office, home and pocket.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

From this premise the article builds its central claim: that consumer technology companies presented to the public with folksy origin stories — a hoodie-wearing tech nerd from Harvard, a couple of friends working out of a mother's garage, a man selling books online from a one-person office — are in fact vehicles for Defense Department research, sometimes with films made to solidify those origin stories in the public mind.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## LifeLog

The Department of Defense is also named as the parent of the Information Processing Techniques Office that ran [[lifelog|LifeLog]], the DARPA program intended to compile a massive electronic database of every activity and relationship a person engages in, including credit card purchases, websites visited, the content of telephone calls and emails, physical location via wearable GPS sensors, and biomedical data.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy) LifeLog was cancelled on 4 February 2004 after criticism of its privacy implications — the same day, the article notes, that Facebook started up.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## Relationship to the intelligence community

In the article's framing, the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]] takes technology the Defense Department created and uses it to influence and keep tabs on Americans, creating companies and installing public faces for them because Americans would not voluntarily hand their data to an intelligence agency.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy) The author extends this to specific products, suggesting that DARPA "also probably created SIRI" for [[apple|Apple]], and that what comes out of Seattle and Silicon Valley "via DARPA" constitutes an intellectual-property advantage no other country can match.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/america-has-a-cia-run-economy)

## Outreach to civilian manufacturers (2026)

In 2026 the Department of Defense expanded its weapons-production effort into the civilian industrial base, approaching automakers and other manufacturers to boost capacity for munitions, vehicles, and defense systems.[2] Analysts read the outreach as a structural move toward a sustained wartime production footing, in which commercial manufacturing flexibility is repurposed for military supply chains and production is distributed across both defense contractors and civilian industry.[2]

The shift reflected lessons from supply shortages earlier in the Russia–[[ukraine|Ukraine]] war, where limited stockpiles and slow production exposed weaknesses in Western defense readiness.[2] Commentators expected second-order effects across labor, technology, and capital allocation, with investment redirected toward defense-linked sectors and concerns raised about escalation, the normalization of conflict-driven growth, and the durability of a permanently mobilized industrial base.[2]

## 2023 email server exposure

In February 2023 the Department of Defense secured an exposed server that had been spilling internal U.S. military emails to the open internet for roughly two weeks.[3] The server sat on [[microsoft|Microsoft]]'s Azure government cloud for Department of Defense customers and held about three terabytes of internal military email, much of it pertaining to [[united-states-special-operations-command|U.S. Special Operations Command]]; a misconfiguration had left it without a password, so anyone knowing its IP address could read the mailbox data in a browser.[3] The incident is covered at [[2023-defense-department-email-server-exposure|2023 Defense Department email server exposure]].[3]

## Confirmation of the Chinese surveillance balloon (2023)

On Thursday, February 2, 2023, the Department of Defense publicly confirmed that an object seen over the Billings, Montana area the previous day was an intelligence-gathering balloon "most certainly launched by the People's Republic of China," a statement that transformed a local airspace closure at [[billings-logan-international-airport|Billings Logan International Airport]] into the national [[chinese-spy-balloon-incident-2023|Chinese spy balloon incident]].[4]

## Transgender service member policy memo (2023)

In July 2023 the outlet The Dossier published a previously unavailable 34-page Department of Defense memo on the "care of service members who identify as transgender," detailing benefits granted to that cohort including deployment waivers and indefinite waivers of physical fitness standards.[5] The memo circulated after President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] reversed the [[transgender-military-ban|transgender military ban]] instituted under [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[5] Critics argued the policies undermined [[military-readiness|military readiness]] at a time when the armed forces faced their worst recruiting environment since the end of conscription in 1973, and that the department was functioning "more of a social welfare and social justice program than an entity purposed with defeating an aggressing army."[5]

## Return of Ukraine-diverted munitions to Israel (October 2023)

On Monday, October 23, 2023, the Department of Defense admitted it was sending back to Israel some of the weapons it had drawn from its stockpiles there and shipped to Europe for Ukraine, while insisting it could supply both conflicts.[6] A senior defense official told reporters at a [[pentagon|Pentagon]] press briefing: "We withdrew 155 [shells] from the stockpile in Israel for our forces in [European Command] and now we are sending that back to Israel for their use."[6]

The department had first acknowledged in January 2023 that, to supply Ukraine with 155 mm artillery shells for its ground war with Russia, it dipped into the U.S. stockpile maintained in Israel.[6] That stockpile exists for American forces in the region but also for Israeli forces in emergencies such as the October 7, 2023 [[hamas|Hamas]] attacks, after which the United States flew additional weapons to Israel.[6] The [[new-york-times|New York Times]] reported that "half" of the shells in the Israel stockpile had been shipped to Europe the previous winter.[6]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the United States relocated much of that stockpile to [[volodymyr-zelensky|Zelenskyy]] during the "Slava Ukraine! psyop" and was now sending it back, remarking that "it's almost as if the US has no standards in who they support," and noting that Republicans in [[us-congress|Congress]] were pushing to separate additional Ukraine funding from Israel aid — a move Senator [[john-fetterman|John Fetterman]] answered on X with "F*ck that."[6] She added that everything the Biden administration and the 118th Congress did appeared to be an escalation, and asked, "What's the carbon footprint of World War III?"[6]
