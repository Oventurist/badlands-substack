# Trilateral Commission

The **Trilateral Commission** is one of the elite policy forums Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] uses to map the membership of what he calls the foreign policy establishment.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away)

[[jake-sullivan|Jake Sullivan]], who worked on the [[joint-comprehensive-plan-of-action|Iran Deal]] at the policy level, is listed as a Trilateral Commission affiliate alongside his ties to the 7th Floor group, the [[carnegie-endowment-for-international-peace|Carnegie Endowment]] and the Rhodes Scholarship.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away) [[tom-donilon|Tom Donilon]] is likewise described as a regular fixture at the Trilateral Commission, together with [[bilderberg|Bilderberg]], the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]] and the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/and-iran-iran-so-far-away)

## The 1970s post-industrial turn

A September 2024 analysis by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes Trilateral Commission agents as having "took over the reigns of government in the 1970s" and unleashed a full-spectrum programme that shattered the spirit of progress animating western culture.[2] Under this post-industrial logic, breakthroughs in nuclear fission and fusion, space technology and post-colonial industrial development were systematically sabotaged and replaced by a governing priority of scarcity management, population reduction and global governance.[2]

Ehret identifies [[james-schlesinger|James Schlesinger]] as "an important Trilateral Commission ideologue" who moved from the directorship of the CIA in 1973 to the Department of Defense and then the Department of Energy, where he oversaw the cancellation of nearly all nuclear projects planned for the 1980s.[2] The same account holds that the Commission imposed a development trap on China beginning in the 1970s, from which China broke free by banning [[george-soros|George Soros]] and his operatives starting in 1989.[2]

## Founding and Cold War role

Badlands Media's account of [[operation-cyclone|Operation Cyclone]] states that the Trilateral Commission was founded by [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]] and [[david-rockefeller|David Rockefeller]] with the blessing of the [[bilderberg]] Group, as a body created to coordinate imperialist strategy between Washington, Western Europe and Japan.3 Its membership has included a range of household names, from Antony Blinken to Jeffrey Epstein.3 The late author Jim Marrs wrote that President [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]]'s administration "was so filled with members of the Trilateral Commission that conspiracy researchers had a field day. Even the Establishment media began to talk."3

## Harvard participation

"The Harvard Control Grid" notes that the Trilateral Commission was founded by [[david-rockefeller|David Rockefeller]] and unites elites from North America, Europe and Asia, with Harvard alumni and former executives such as [[larry-summers|Larry Summers]] as longtime participants.[4] The article describes its policy objectives as "subtle, but unmistakable: preserve global economic interdependence, manage democratic instability, and contain populist uprisings through technocratic oversight."[4] It groups the commission with the [[council-on-foreign-relations|CFR]] among Rockefeller-founded bodies through which Harvard's endowment stewards participate in setting elite consensus.[4]

## Origins as a CFR offshoot

Badlands Media's "The Men Behind the Curtain – Part 2" presents the Trilateral Commission as the spiritual successor of the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]], describing both as the epitome of covert organizations that guide public policy in directions contrary to the public interest.[6] By the late 1960s, the account holds, CFR chairman [[david-rockefeller|David Rockefeller]] concluded that the council had become too well known and conspiracy-minded reporters were having a field day, so he instigated a slightly more public offshoot that would officially bring Japan into the globalist fold.[6] The article further suggests the group may have laid the groundwork for the union between Big Tech and the intelligence community exposed by the [[twitter-files|Twitter Files]].[6]

The intellectual origin belongs to [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]], then head of Columbia University's Russian Studies Department, who had researched closer cooperation between Europe, North America and Asia at the [[brookings-institution|Brookings Institution]] and called in *[[foreign-affairs|Foreign Affairs]]* for "a council representing the United States, Western Europe, and Japan."[6] The proposal was carried to the [[bilderberg-group|Bilderberg group]] at Knokke-Heist, Belgium, in April 1972, where it reportedly met with enthusiasm amid concern over Nixon's dollar devaluation, import surcharges, détente with China, and the OPEC oil shock.[6]

## Founding meeting at Kykuit

With the blessings of both the CFR and the Bilderbergers secured, organizing began on July 23–24, 1972 at [[kykuit|Kykuit]], Rockefeller's 3,500-acre estate in Pocantico Hills, Tarrytown, New York.[6] Attendees hand-selected by Rockefeller and Brzezinski included [[mcgeorge-bundy|McGeorge Bundy]], Brookings foreign policy director Henry Owen, Robert Bowie, [[c-fred-bergsten|C. Fred Bergsten]], Bayless Manning, Karl Carstens (later president of Germany), Guido Colonna di Paliano, François Duchêne, René Foch, Max Kohnstamm, Kiichi Miyazawa (later Prime Minister of Japan), Saburo Okita, and Tadashi Yamamoto.[6]

The Commission was officially founded on July 1, 1973, with Rockefeller as chairman and Brzezinski as founding North American director.[6] North American members included Georgia governor [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]], congressman John B. Anderson, and Time, Inc. editor-in-chief Hedley Donovan; foreign founding members included Reginald Maudling, Lord Eric Roll, *Economist* editor Alastair Burnet, FIAT president Giovanni Agnelli, and Raymond Barre.[6] Its early annual publication, *Trialogue*, stated that the Commission "was formed in 1973 by private citizens of Western Europe, Japan, and North America to foster closer cooperation among these three regions on common problems" — language critics read as collusion among multinational bankers and corporate elites pursuing one-world government.[6] The Commission is based in New York, Tokyo and Paris, administered by a 35-member executive committee that meets roughly every nine months, rotating among the three regions.[6]

## Funding

Spokesmen insisted the group received no government funding.[6] A 1978 report showed commission funding from mid-1976 to mid-1979 of $1,180,000, much of it from tax-exempt foundations such as the [[rockefeller-brothers-fund|Rockefeller Brothers Fund]], which contributed $120,000 in 1977 alone; other donors included the Ford Foundation, the Lilly Endowment, the German Marshall Fund, and corporations such as Time, Bechtel, Exxon, General Motors, Wells Fargo and Texas Instruments.[6]

## The Triangle Papers and policy outcomes

During its first years the Commission issued Task Force Reports known as "The Triangle Papers."[6] The best known, *[[the-crisis-of-democracy|The Crisis of Democracy]]* (1975) by [[samuel-p-huntington|Samuel P. Huntington]], argued for "a greater degree of moderation in democracy" and for leaders with "expertise, seniority, experience, and special talents" to "override the claims of democracy."[6] Huntington later became coordinator of security planning on Carter's National Security Council, where he prepared Presidential Review Memorandum 32, leading to the 1979 order creating the [[fema|Federal Emergency Management Agency]].[6]

Other examples cited include [[richard-n-cooper|Richard Cooper]], who headed the commission's monetary policy task force and recommended selling official gold reserves to private markets before becoming undersecretary of state for economic affairs while the [[imf|IMF]] sold part of its gold; [[john-sawhill|John Sawhill]], author of *Energy: Managing the Transition*, appointed deputy secretary of the Department of Energy; and [[c-fred-bergsten|C. Fred Bergsten]], who helped prepare *Interdependence and the Reform of International Institutions* and became assistant secretary of the Treasury for international affairs.[6] The pattern, the article argues, is of Trilateralists reaching positions of power where they implement policy recommendations they themselves drafted.[6]

## Carter, Reagan and later administrations

Brzezinski recruited Carter into the Commission in 1973, and the *Washington Post* observed in January 1977 that the president-elect, vice-president-elect Walter Mondale, and the incoming secretaries of State, Defense and Treasury — Cyrus Vance, Harold Brown and W. Michael Blumenthal — were all members.[6] Other Trilateralists in the administration included Henry Owen, Warren Christopher, Paul Warnke, ambassadors Andrew Young, Gerard Smith, Richard Gardner and Elliot Richardson, Richard Cooper, Lucy Benson, Anthony Solomon, Robert Bowie of the CIA, and Richard Holbrooke.[6] Republicans in the group included [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] and George Bush.[6] Carter's appointment of [[paul-volcker|Paul Volcker]] — a former North American chairman of the Commission — to head the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], reportedly on Rockefeller's instruction, further fueled suspicion, as did his replacement by [[alan-greenspan|Alan Greenspan]], also a Trilateral, CFR and Bilderberg member.[6]

Criticism mounted: [[barry-goldwater|Barry Goldwater]] wrote that "what the Trilaterals truly intend is the creation of a worldwide economic power superior to the political government of the nation-states involved."[6] The American Legion passed Resolution 773 in 1980 calling for a congressional investigation of the Commission and the CFR, and the Veterans of Foreign Wars approved a similar resolution the following year; both were introduced in the House by Congressman [[larry-mcdonald|Larry McDonald]], who died in the 1983 downing of Korean Air Lines Flight 007.[6] [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]] campaigned against the nineteen Trilaterals in Carter's administration but, after accepting George Bush as running mate at the 1980 convention, never spoke against the group again; Jim Marrs counted 28 CFR members, ten Bilderbergers and at least ten Trilaterals on Reagan's 59-member transition team.[6]

The Commission's agenda, the article concludes, continued through the Clinton and George W. Bush presidencies and culminated with [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]], who appointed eleven Commission members to top posts within his first ten days in office.[6] Though it now attracts less attention, it still promotes the globalist priorities of the Davos crowd and Klaus Schwab's [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] — climate change, inequality and the "digital revolution."[6]
