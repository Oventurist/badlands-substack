# Martin Luther King Jr.

Martin Luther King Jr. was an American civil rights leader assassinated in 1968. His autopsy and its ballistics record were revisited in April 2026 by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] as a structural parallel to the [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]] case, after the anniversary of King's death fell on the Saturday of Easter weekend.[1]

## Autopsy and ballistics

The official autopsy determined King died from a single gunshot wound.[1] As quoted by Badlands, the bullet entered the right side of his face about an inch to the right of and half an inch below his mouth, fractured his jaw, exited the lower face and reentered the body through the neck, severing multiple vital arteries, fracturing the spine in several places and coming to rest beneath the skin on the left side of the back, traveling in a downward and rearward direction.[1]

The recovered projectile was a deformed .30-06 caliber rifle slug designated exhibit Q64, found lodged beneath the skin near the left shoulder blade.[1] A Remington 760 Gamemaster rifle, designated Q2, was recovered near the crime scene and identified as the suspected murder weapon.[1] When the House Select Committee on Assassinations convened a firearms panel, experts found the bullet's class characteristics matched the rifle — both showing six grooves and six lands with a right twist — but concluded they were "unable positively to identify or eliminate the Q64 bullet as having been fired from the Q2 rifle."[1]

## Badlands parallel to Charlie Kirk

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] highlighted that the bullet said to have killed King was a .30-06, and that in both the King and Kirk cases "matching the bullet to the rifle proved far more difficult than investigators hoped."[1] She noted a commenter's framing of Kirk as "the 'conservative' Martin Luther King Jr in 30 years... a reboot," and pointed skeptically to the committee finding that no U.S. government agency participated in the assassination.[1] Both killings were characterized as strikes on prominent liberty activists at transformational moments of American populism.[1]
