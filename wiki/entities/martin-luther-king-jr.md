# Martin Luther King Jr.

Martin Luther King Jr. was an American civil rights leader assassinated in 1968. His autopsy and its ballistics record were revisited in April 2026 by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] as a structural parallel to the [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]] case, after the anniversary of King's death fell on the Saturday of Easter weekend.[1]

## Autopsy and ballistics

The official autopsy determined King died from a single gunshot wound.[1] As quoted by Badlands, the bullet entered the right side of his face about an inch to the right of and half an inch below his mouth, fractured his jaw, exited the lower face and reentered the body through the neck, severing multiple vital arteries, fracturing the spine in several places and coming to rest beneath the skin on the left side of the back, traveling in a downward and rearward direction.[1]

The recovered projectile was a deformed .30-06 caliber rifle slug designated exhibit Q64, found lodged beneath the skin near the left shoulder blade.[1] A Remington 760 Gamemaster rifle, designated Q2, was recovered near the crime scene and identified as the suspected murder weapon.[1] When the House Select Committee on Assassinations convened a firearms panel, experts found the bullet's class characteristics matched the rifle — both showing six grooves and six lands with a right twist — but concluded they were "unable positively to identify or eliminate the Q64 bullet as having been fired from the Q2 rifle."[1]

## Badlands parallel to Charlie Kirk

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] highlighted that the bullet said to have killed King was a .30-06, and that in both the King and Kirk cases "matching the bullet to the rifle proved far more difficult than investigators hoped."[1] She noted a commenter's framing of Kirk as "the 'conservative' Martin Luther King Jr in 30 years... a reboot," and pointed skeptically to the committee finding that no U.S. government agency participated in the assassination.[1] Both killings were characterized as strikes on prominent liberty activists at transformational moments of American populism.[1]

## Collaboration with Robert Kennedy

In the summer of 1967 King began a close collaboration with Senator [[robert-f-kennedy|Robert F. Kennedy]], telling his associates that the civil rights movement would put its full support behind Kennedy in the run-up to the 1968 elections.[2] Kennedy had earlier intervened in King's October 1960 arrest in Atlanta for driving with an invalid licence in racist territory, and both men advanced civil rights on their separate paths before their peak collaboration began as each made his resistance to the war in [[vietnam-war|Vietnam]] public.[2]

## Aftermath of the assassination

King's death on April 4, 1968 set off a wave of urban race riots that swept 120 cities, killed 39 people — mostly black — injured 2,600 and drew more than 75,000 troops onto American streets.[2] Kennedy's unprotected speech in the Indianapolis ghetto that night is credited with making Indianapolis the only major city to avoid rioting.[2] King's associate Ralph Abernathy later recalled telling Kennedy that he had a chance to be a prophet, "but prophets get shot."[2]

Badlands Media argues that in the vacuum left by King's murder, covert intelligence operations under [[cointelpro|COINTELPRO]] transplanted his movement of peaceful civil disobedience with the violence-prone "New Left" and bomb-building outfits such as the [[weather-underground|Weather Underground]].[2] It holds up King's wisdom, alongside that of [[john-f-kennedy|JFK]], [[malcolm-x|Malcolm X]] and Robert Kennedy, as the antidote to the engineered civil conflict of the present day.[2]


## 2018 national park legislation

In January 2018 [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] signed a bill enabling construction of a [[martin-luther-king-jr-national-historical-park|national park]] honoring King.[3] Badlands Media cited the signing as a rebuttal to characterizations of Trump as racist, noting that before entering politics he had been praised by black community leaders and had received the Ellis Island Award for advancing minorities in New York City in the early 1990s.[3]

## Carlson's ambivalent assessment (August 2023)

In "The British Cabal" (August 7, 2023), [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] used King as his central illustration of a [[deep-state|Deep State]] operation that backfired on its sponsors.[4] He wrote that there is substantial evidence King was supported by the American Communists — financially backed, with his rallies organised and the Communist-run press used to promote both King personally and the Civil Rights Movement generally.[4] The [[british-cabal|Cabal]]'s intention, on this account, was to bring communism into America through the African American community, on the assumption that Black Americans would take to it quickly and easily.[4]

That plan is said to have collapsed when King began to campaign for racial unity within America and to speak against the Vietnam War, making him a massive liability: the Cabal could not afford an anti-war, anti-[[military-industrial-complex|military industrial complex]], anti-division leader gaining influence.[4] Carlson wrote that the [[fbi|FBI]] first blackmailed King over his infidelity and eventually coordinated his assassination once the original plan had failed, because Americans were coming together, rejecting the war, and Black Americans were finding their voice.[4] He added that the FBI then infiltrated Black political groups and created fake ones to instigate violence that could be blamed on the genuine organisations, reducing their support both within the Black community and nationally.[4]

Carlson explicitly refused a verdict of wholly good or wholly bad, laying out the competing readings — that King united the country, that he was Communist-backed and unfaithful, that he was killed by the FBI, that as a Christian pastor he denied the virgin birth, the resurrection and the divinity of Jesus, and that he preached Jesus as an example to live by — before concluding "it's complicated" and that he believes God can use anyone or anything to do his will.[4]
