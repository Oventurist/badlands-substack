# Humza Yousaf

Humza Yousaf is a Scottish politician who served as First Minister of Scotland and leader of the pro-independence [[scottish-national-party|Scottish National Party]] (SNP) before resigning on Monday, April 29, 2024.[1] His resignation followed his decision to scrap the SNP's coalition agreement with the Scottish Greens, after which he failed to secure enough support to survive votes of no confidence that were expected later that week.[1]

## Background and political profile

Yousaf was born in Glasgow to Pakistani immigrant parents.[1] Critics characterized him as a "woke activist politician" heading into the 2023 leadership contest, pointing to his strongly pro-immigration positions and his consistent advocacy for diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) policies.[1] Commentators hostile to him argued that these stances should have served as warning signs to the Scottish public, but that an increasingly progressive electorate delivered him a narrow victory.[1]

Among the episodes most frequently cited by his opponents was a 2020 speech in which Yousaf criticized the "whiteness" of the Scottish government by enumerating senior posts held by white officials.[1] Critics noted that Scotland's population is roughly 96 percent white and argued that a majority-white government was therefore demographically unremarkable — framing Yousaf and like-minded progressives as intent on changing that composition.[1]

## Hate crime legislation and downfall

Yousaf's tenure became closely associated with Scotland's hate crime legislation, which drew backlash from across the political spectrum in the United Kingdom, including from more liberal figures such as Harry Potter author [[jk-rowling|J.K. Rowling]].[1] Coverage in alternative and right-leaning outlets tied the law's unpopularity directly to the collapse of his premiership.[1]

## Resignation statement

In an emotional address announcing his departure, Yousaf said that although "a route through this week's motion of no confidence was absolutely possible," he was "not willing to trade my values and principles or do deals with whomever simply for retaining power."[1] He added that after "spending the weekend reflecting on what is best for my party, for the government and for the country I lead," he had concluded that "repairing our relationship across the political divide can only be done with someone else at the helm," and that he had informed the SNP's national secretary of his intention to stand down so that a leadership contest could begin as soon as possible.[1]
