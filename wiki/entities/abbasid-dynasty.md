# Abbasid Dynasty

The Abbasid Dynasty of Baghdad is presented in Matthew Ehret's series as one of the central pillars of an 8th–9th century ecumenical alliance uniting Jewish, Christian, Muslim and Confucian civilizations.[1]

## Alliance with Khazaria

The Persian scholar al-Masudi (896–956) reported in his *Meadows of Gold* that the Jewish Khazars had established a military alliance with the Abbasid Dynasty, under which the Abbasids supplied an army of 10,000 Muslim soldiers to [[khazaria|Khazaria]] on the condition that if any future Jewish leader were to declare war on Islam, that army would fight for Islam instead.[1] The article characterizes this safeguard as a creative flank binding the self-interests of both cultures together in a way that made orchestrated imperial conflict nearly impossible.[1]

## Support for the Tang Dynasty

At a decisive moment in 755 CE, the [[tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] faced the [[an-shi-rebellion|An-Shi Rebellion]], in which the renegade general An Lushan declared himself emperor of the North, threatening civil war and the dissolution of the new [[silk-road|Silk Road]].[1] Caliph al-Mahdi, grandfather of [[harun-al-rashid|Harun al-Rashid]], sent 4,000 Muslim soldiers to help the Tang emperor put down the rebellion, preserving the ecumenical alliance.[1]

## The Islamic Golden Age

The article describes the Abbasid Dynasty as rightly known as the "Islamic Golden Age," ushering in bureaucratic, monetary and educational reform under a governing principle equivalent to the Confucian Mandate of Heaven.[1] Under al-Mahdi, Harun al-Rashid and al-Mamun, [[houses-of-wisdom|Houses of Wisdom]] gathered Muslim, Christian and Jewish scholars, and paper mills were established in 832 CE in Samarkand, Cairo, Damascus and Baghdad, applying Chinese technology to broaden access to knowledge.[1] Islam's parallel to St. Augustine's doctrine of Witness was the Doctrine of Dimi, which asserted that Muslims must protect Jews because they had direct intercourse with the One God shared by all Abrahamic faiths.[1] The article notes that the Islamic Empire eventually fell into its own internal and external discord as the oligarchy broke up Charlemagne's kingdom.[1]
