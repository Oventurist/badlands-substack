# Nikolai Patrushev

Nikolai Patrushev is a senior Russian security official who served as secretary of Russia's Security Council, one of the most powerful national-security posts in the Kremlin apparatus.[1]

## 2024 Security Council transition

In the cabinet reshuffle announced by [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] in May 2024, Patrushev was displaced from the leadership of Russia's Security Council, with outgoing defense minister [[sergei-shoigu|Sergei Shoigu]] moved into that role.[1] Coverage of the shake-up framed Shoigu's transfer as an effective promotion into a position closer to Putin, which necessarily came "at the expense of" Patrushev.[1] Western officials treated the broader reshuffle — which also elevated economist [[andrey-belousov|Andrey Belousov]] to defense minister — as evidence of Kremlin strain, a reading contested by Badlands commentary.[1]

## Maritime Board and Western "maritime terrorism" (2025)

By 2025 Patrushev was serving as national security adviser to President Vladimir Putin and chairing Russia's Maritime Board.[2] Speaking at a board meeting in late April 2025, he said Western countries were targeting Russian vessels with an increasing number of acts of sabotage and terrorism as part of a wider push to impose a maritime blockade on the country.[2] "There is a consistent increase in NATO's military activity in the Baltic and North Seas, in the North Atlantic and the Asia-Pacific region," he said, adding that Western states had "expanded the practice of taking deliberate steps towards establishing a naval blockade," including "attempts to inspect ships in international waters and to commit sabotage and terrorist acts."[2]

Patrushev had previously warned that any attempt by the European Union or the United Kingdom to expel Russian vessels from the seas would meet a "proportionate" retaliation, telling the press that if law and diplomacy failed, "the security of Russian shipping will be ensured by our navy. The hotheads in London or Brussels need to clearly understand this."[2]

Badlands commentary glossed "maritime terrorism" as piracy and drew an analogy to the 16th-century origins of European privateering, when England and France commissioned naval captains to attack Spanish treasure shipments carrying gold from Mexico and silver from Peru, issuing certificates designating them privateers so that they would not be hanged as pirates if captured.[2] The commentary noted that many such captains eventually operated on their own account and became pirates, while others such as Henry Morgan remained commissioned by European governments and were even appointed governors of Caribbean islands, and argued that [[nato|NATO]] was now engaging in the same behavior without gold and silver as the prize.[2]

## Assessment

Because the Security Council secretariat sits at the intersection of the Russian intelligence services, the military and the presidency, changes in its leadership are read closely in Washington and European capitals as signals about the internal balance of power in Moscow.[1]
