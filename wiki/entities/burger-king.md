# Burger King

Burger King is an American fast-food hamburger chain that, in May 2024, became the second major quick-service brand to reintroduce a discounted value meal in response to visible strain on lower-income American consumers.[1]

## The 2024 $5 meal deal

Bloomberg reported on May 23, 2024 that Burger King was preparing to launch a $5 meal deal comprising a choice of one of three sandwiches with nuggets, fries and a drink, a promotion its franchisees had approved in April.[1] The move followed [[mcdonalds|McDonald's]] plans, reported roughly two weeks earlier, to reintroduce a $5 combo deal after three years of price increases that Zero Hedge labeled "McFlation" and that had pushed some combo meals as high as $18.[1]

Coverage framed the competing promotions as a "value war" in which mega corporations roll back prices to retain customers and prevent trade-downs or migration to competitors, and as an implicit admission that working-poor consumers are broke.[1] Contemporaneous price cuts by Walmart, Target and Aldi across thousands of everyday items were attributed to a spending slowdown among cash-strapped consumers drowning in credit card debt and drained savings amid elevated inflation, while Goldman Sachs analysts publicly turned bearish on the US consumer and warned of emerging stagflation.[1] Zero Hedge additionally raised the possibility that pressure from the White House ahead of the 2024 elections contributed to the rollbacks.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for Badlands, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that value wars nominally benefit customers as corporations compete for shrinking disposable income, while questioning whether fast food benefits anyone.[1] She accepted the article's diagnosis of a consumer spending slowdown as accurate, observing that "after the dollar store became the dollar and 25 cents store, every American became more bargain-friendly in at least one domain of their personal supply chains," and attributed the underlying squeeze to "unipartisan treasury looting."[1] Her stated hope was that the trend would extend to groceries, gas and home goods, noting that in a genuinely free market it would — and that in a genuinely free market the situation would never have arisen.[1]
