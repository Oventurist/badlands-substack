# Alfred Thayer Mahan

Alfred Thayer Mahan (1840–1914) was a United States naval officer and strategist whom [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presents as the intellectual author of America's turn away from its anti-imperial traditions and toward an Anglo-American imperial identity.[1] Mahan is commonly credited as a co-founder of modern geopolitics and as an inspiration for the British geographer Lord Halford Mackinder.[1]

## Naval career

Mahan graduated from the naval academy in 1859 and, according to Ehret, became renowned as a failure in actual combat, repeatedly crashing warships into moving and stationary objects during the Civil War.[1] Ehret argues that because practical reality was not his strength, Mahan devoted his post-war career to theorizing, studying maps of the world and celebrating Britain's naval power as the driving force of world history.[1]

## The Influence of Sea Power Upon History

Mahan's *[[the-influence-of-sea-power-upon-history|The Influence of Sea Power Upon History, 1660–1783]]* was published in 1890, the same year as [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]]'s *[[cosmopolitan-railway|The Cosmopolitan Railway]]*, and represented what Ehret calls a total break from the spirit of win-win cooperation that had defined American foreign policy until that point.[1] The historian Francis P. Sempa wrote that the book soon "became the bible for many navies around the world," with the Kaiser of Germany demanding that all of his officers read it — an embrace that occurred only after the Lincoln-admiring statesman [[otto-von-bismarck|Otto von Bismarck]] was ousted as Chancellor in 1890.[1] [[theodore-roosevelt|Theodore Roosevelt]] later ordered copies for every member of Congress.[1]

Throughout the work Mahan asserted that it was America's destiny to succeed the British Empire by rejecting rail development and embracing British colonial techniques.[1] Adopting the British imperial definition of "commerce," in which free trade serves as cover for the military dominance of weaker nations, Mahan argued that the United States need no longer adhere to what he considered the outdated habits of the [[monroe-doctrine|Monroe Doctrine]], since the new order of world empires demanded a different guiding philosophy to avoid extinction.[1] He wrote that "the advance of Russia in Asia, in the division of Africa, in the colonial ambitions of France and in the British idea of Imperial Federation" demanded that the United States act accordingly.[1]

## Hostility to rail development

Mahan dismissed continental rail corridors, writing that "a railway competes in vain with a river… because more facile and copious, water traffic is for equal distances much cheaper and because cheaper, more useful."[1] Ehret argues that this reflects the same incomprehension displayed by present-day critics of the [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]]: that the returns on rail investment are qualitative rather than simply monetary, uniting divided peoples, increasing manufacturing and industrial corridors, and raising national productive powers and cultural capacity for creative thought.[1] The contrary view was expressed by Russia's [[sergei-witte|Sergei Witte]], who said in 1890 that "the railroad is like a leaven, which creates a cultural fermentation among the population."[1]

## The Problem of Asia and racial doctrine

In *[[the-problem-of-asia|The Problem of Asia]]* (1900) Mahan expressed horror at the awakening of the Chinese nation, writing that "it is scarcely desirable that so vast a proportion of mankind as the Chinese constitute should be animated by but one spirit," and that should China "burst her barriers eastward, it would be impossible to exaggerate the momentous issues dependant upon a firm hold of the Hawaiian islands by a great civilized maritime power."[1] Ehret documents Mahan's adherence to social Darwinism, noting his description of "well recognized racial divergencies" between the branches of humanity then dubbed "Teutonic," "Slavic" and "Oriental," and his claim that "the Oriental, whether national or individual does not change" and that "the East does not progress."[1]

Mahan likened China to a carcass to be devoured, writing that "if life departs, a carcass can be utilized only by dissection or for food; the gathering to it of the eagles is a natural law, of which it is bootless to complain."[1] Championing an Anglo-American alliance to subdue and "civilize" China after the [[boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]], he stated that "of all the nations we shall meet in the East, Great Britain is the one with which we have by far the most in common in the nature of our interests there and in our standards of law and justice."[1] He also made explicit his belief that progress is produced by force and war, writing that "every step forward in the march that has opened in China to trade has been gained by pressure; the most important have been the result of actual war."[1]

## Legacy

With the assassination of [[william-mckinley|William McKinley]] and the rise of [[theodore-roosevelt|Theodore Roosevelt]] in 1901, Ehret argues, it was Mahan's worldview rather than Gilpin's that became the dominant American foreign policy doctrine for the following 120 years.[1] Statesmen including [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]], Secretary of State James Blaine, William Seward, President Grant, James Garfield and McKinley are described as having detested the paradigm Mahan represented.[1]
