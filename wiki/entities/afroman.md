# Afroman

Afroman, the stage name of Joseph E. Foreman, is an American rapper best known for his 2000 hit single "Because I Got High."[1] In March 2026 he became the central figure in a widely publicized free-speech case in Ohio after a jury ruled unanimously in his favor in a civil trial brought against him by law enforcement officers.[1]

## The 2022 raid

In 2022, seven officers of the Adams County Sheriff's Office in Ohio executed a no-knock raid on Afroman's home under a warrant seeking drugs, paraphernalia, and evidence of kidnapping.[1] Nothing illegal was found during the search.[1] Security cameras installed at the residence captured the entire operation, including footage of one deputy pausing to examine a fresh lemon pound cake sitting on the kitchen counter — an image that became emblematic of the raid's futility.[1]

## Satirical response

Afroman published the raid footage online and produced a series of satirical rap videos and songs mocking the officers and the fruitless search, among them "Will You Help Me Repair My Door?" and "Lemon Pound Cake."[1]

## Litigation and verdict

In 2023 the seven officers sued Afroman for defamation, misappropriation of likeness, false light, and infliction of emotional distress, claiming that the videos had humiliated them, traumatized their families, and damaged their professional reputations.[1] The case went to a three-day trial concluding on March 19, 2026, during which Afroman testified wearing an American-flag suit and argued that the raid itself had provoked his artistic response.[1] The jury ruled unanimously in his favor.[1]

Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] characterized the verdict as an important affirmation of the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] right to parody and criticize public officials, observing that a contrary result would have chilled artistic and political speech nationwide.[1] Afroman celebrated on social media, declaring, "We did it America! Yeah! We did it—freedom of speech!" and "Power to the people!"[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the verdict as a vindication of a long American tradition, quoting Turley's observation that parody and song have been used to criticize government officials and policies since the founding of the Republic.[1] She used the case to warn against what she described as a growing appetite on the political right for viewpoint discrimination now that its "side" controls the government, arguing that punishing speech critical of government is unprincipled regardless of who holds power.[1]
