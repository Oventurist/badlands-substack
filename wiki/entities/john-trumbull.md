# John Trumbull

John Trumbull was an American Revolutionary War painter, best known for his iconic canvas celebrating the signing of the Declaration of Independence, and the founder of the [[american-academy-of-fine-arts|American Academy of Fine Arts]] in New York.[1] In Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]], Trumbull is presented less as a hero of the early republic than as the figurehead of an Anglophile cultural apparatus that stifled the emergence of a genuinely American school of painting.[1]

## The American Academy of Fine Arts

Trumbull established the Academy together with a grouping of co-thinkers drawn not from the artistic community but from the financier and political-operative class, and Ehret argues that this network "did not have the best interests of Americans or the American dream at heart."[1] Trumbull himself was, by the article's own admission, an unarguably talented artist, but he was the only artist among the founding group in New York; the remainder — including the Academy's principal sponsor [[robert-livingston|Robert Livingston]], along with James Pinchot, Thomas Clark and Ogden Haggerty — were leading speculators and political intelligence operatives among the Federalists.[1]

The Academy's business model, as recorded by [[william-dunlap|William Dunlap]], was that of a "joint stock company," composed of persons of every trade and profession who regarded the privilege of visiting the exhibition as an equivalent for their subscription; such stockholders elected the directors and could themselves be elected, while artists could share those privileges only by purchasing stock and "might be controlled in everything respecting their profession by those who were ignorant of the arts."[1] Ehret contends that these patrons did not care about art itself, but valued the field for money laundering, speculation, entertainment and commercialism, cultivating a market almost exclusively in portraiture because it was the most profitable genre in the era before photography.[1]

## The debate with Samuel Morse

When [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]] and Dunlap founded the rival [[national-academy-of-design|National Academy of the Arts of Design]], Trumbull's Academy first offered a compromise in which artists could join the board of directors for $100, then reneged, admitting only two artists so as not to "sully" the board with too many and insisting that "experts" continue running the show.[1] A series of public debates followed. Trumbull argued in his oration against Morse that the abolition of the stockholders was "utterly inadmissible," that "the arts cannot flourish without patronage in some form," and that artists "are necessarily dependent upon the protection of the rich and the great," citing the endowments of Louis XIV in Paris and Rome and of George III in London, including a £5,000 gift from the royal privy purse to the Academy of London.[1]

Morse's reply — mocking the image of the artist who "must learn to bow and bow in the halls and antechambers of my Lord" and denouncing the Academy of Fine Arts as an "anti-republican institution" — won the public over.[1] According to [[pierre-beaudry|Pierre Beaudry]], Trumbull was forced to resign by popular consent, and within a matter of months the Trumbull–Livingston academy disintegrated entirely.[1]
