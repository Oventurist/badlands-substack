# Colorado Supreme Court

The Colorado Supreme Court is the highest court in the U.S. state of [[colorado|Colorado]]. In December 2023 it became the center of national controversy after ruling that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was disqualified from the state's 2024 presidential ballot under Section 3 of the [[fourteenth-amendment|14th Amendment]].[1]

## 2023 Trump disqualification ruling

The court's decision removed Trump from the 2024 election ballot in Colorado on the theory that he had engaged in insurrection.[1] Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] observed that the underlying 14th Amendment theory had previously been rejected by jurists in various states, including many Democrats, and criticized what he described as "unhinged coverage" of the ruling in national media.[1]

During the Colorado trial, a significant portion of the discussion was devoted to [[jefferson-davis|Jefferson Davis]], the president of the Confederacy, and the court's ruling reflected a judgment that the comparison between Davis and Trump was apt.[1] Commentators including [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the Davis analogy was subsequently extended by media figures such as [[mara-gay|Mara Gay]] of [[new-york-times|The New York Times]], who suggested on [[msnbc|MSNBC]] that opposing the Colorado ruling made a person a "modern day confederate."[1]

## Reception in the Truth Community

Writers at [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] framed the ruling and the rhetoric surrounding it as part of an escalating establishment effort to prevent Trump from returning to office ahead of 2024, predicting that comparable rhetoric would intensify as the campaign progressed.[1]

## Appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court (January 2024)

The court had issued its ruling on December 19, 2023, holding that Trump was to be barred from the state's ballot because he "engaged in insurrection" by inciting supporters to storm the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021.[3] Because Colorado election law permits only qualified candidates to be placed on the ballot, the court reasoned it would be "wrongful" for the secretary of state to list a candidate disqualified under Section 3, which bars from office anyone who previously took an oath of office and later engaged in insurrection.[3] Trump's lawyers announced their intent to appeal immediately.[3] Constitutional commentator [[horace-cooper|Horace Cooper]] predicted the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] would reverse narrowly while dodging the insurrection question, and argued the Colorado court should have been more prudent — a position [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] criticized as leaving an improper civil-case finding in force.[3] Further detail is collected at [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|the Colorado ballot disqualification case]].[3]

The Colorado Supreme Court's ruling barring [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the state's presidential ballot on [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] grounds was appealed to the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]], which by late January 2024 had signaled it was moving quickly on the case.[2] Trump's attorneys filed their petition on January 3, 2024, presenting the question of whether the Colorado court incorrectly ordered him excluded from the 2024 presidential ballot; oral argument was set for February 8, with written opinions expected the same day.[2]

## Insurrection finding and its downstream effects

In late 2023 the Colorado Supreme Court upheld the trial court's finding of fact that January 6 was an insurrection in [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]], the case seeking to disqualify [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] under the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]].[4] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that this confirmation, rather than the ballot-access outcome, was the operative result: it "gave this Secretary of State in Maine all she needed to act unilaterally in barring 45 from her state's primary ballot," referring to [[shenna-bellows|Shenna Bellows]] of [[maine|Maine]].[4] She contended that the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] must therefore rule on the underlying findings and not merely the Fourteenth Amendment question, warning that an uncorrected state-court error would bar veteran January 6 attendees from office and "green light the uniparty's next phase of lawfare-as-election-interference."[4]

## Masterpiece Cakeshop dismissal (October 2024)

On October 8, 2024, the court declined in a 4–3 decision to decide whether Christian baker [[jack-phillips|Jack Phillips]] could refuse to make a celebratory gender-transition cake for transgender attorney [[autumn-scardina|Autumn Scardina]], dismissing the case on procedural grounds.[5] Justice Melissa Hart wrote for the majority that the constitutional question of how governments should balance the rights of transgender individuals in places of public accommodation against those of religious business owners "has become the focus of intense public debate," but concluded: "We cannot answer that question."[5]
The ruling was the first contentious decision issued by the court since [[monica-marquez|Monica Márquez]] became Chief Justice in September 2024; Márquez joined the majority.[5] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the outcome as a notable shift for a body widely considered the most progressive state high court in the nation, and argued that the "technicality" cited by the Colorado press amounted to the constitutional protection of religious liberty and freedom of expression that the state's district and appeals courts had declined to honor in ruling against Phillips and [[masterpiece-cakeshop|Masterpiece Cakeshop]].[5]
