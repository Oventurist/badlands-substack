# DC Jail

The DC jail — referred to in dissident commentary as the "DC Gulag" — is the District of Columbia correctional facility where many pre-trial January 6, 2021 defendants have been held.[1]

## Conditions dispute

Congressman [[ken-buck|Ken Buck]] wrote in September 2023 that the DC jail "is a miserable place, rife with abuse and dangerous for even the most hardened criminal," that it had been a concern of civil rights activists for decades, and that while lawyers for several January 6 defendants had complained about lack of proper food, access to showers, and restrictions on religious services, "none of these allegations have been substantiated by a court."[1] He argued it was untrue that conditions were worse for January 6 defendants than for other inmates.[1]

Journalist [[mel-hawley|Mel Hawley]] and Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] disputed that framing, noting that Buck's standard amounted to requiring the allegedly corrupt courts to substantiate allegations of corruption in the courts.[1] They reported that DC detainees went two years without video or physical visitation with family and without religious services, that a jail chaplain hung up on inquiries about January 6 detainees, that prison guards repeatedly referred to detainees as white supremacists, and that during the first year of detention the only reading material provided in cells was the Nation of Islam's Final Call newsletter.[1]

The jail's deputy warden Kathleen Landerkin oversaw the January 6 detainees; she was documented posting vulgar anti-Trump material on social media, later deleted the account, and was ultimately fired.[1]

## Medical care and restrictive housing

In 2021 a federal judge held DC jail officials in contempt over the treatment of January 6 defendant [[christopher-worrell|Christopher Worrell]], after officials failed for months to obtain treatment for his broken wrist and then obstructed an inquiry into the matter; Worrell was also denied cancer treatment, and the judge stated the "court has zero confidence" that the jail would provide proper treatment and not retaliate against him.[1]

Buck stated that all January 6 defendants were held in "restrictive housing" — additional separation, limited visits and limited recreation — due to concerns about their safety in the general population, a status he said was deemed necessary by jail officials and confirmed by US Marshals and federal court personnel.[1] Critics responded that inmates were denied privacy with counsel, with correctional officers remaining in the room during legal calls, a denial of basic attorney-client privilege.[1]
