# Federal Open Market Committee

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) is the rate-setting body of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], responsible for adjusting the federal funds benchmark rate and communicating the central bank's policy outlook.[1]

## September 2024 meeting

At its September 18, 2024 meeting the committee voted 11 to 1 to lower the benchmark rate by 50 basis points to a target range of 4.75%–5.0%, the first cut in more than four years and a larger move than all but a small minority of surveyed economists had anticipated.[1] Governor Miki Bowman cast the lone dissent, favoring a 25 basis point cut; it was the first dissent by a Fed governor since 2005.[1] The committee simultaneously lowered the rate path implied by its "dot plot" projections without materially revising growth or unemployment forecasts.[1]

The committee's statement added a pledge that it was "strongly committed to supporting maximum employment" alongside its 2% inflation objective, said future adjustments would depend on incoming data, the evolving outlook and the balance of risks, and noted that job gains "have slowed" while inflation "remains somewhat elevated."[1] Critics, including [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] and Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], questioned the timing of a crisis-scale cut delivered under an ostensibly apolitical mandate less than two months before the November 2024 election.[1] For fuller treatment see [[september-2024-fed-rate-cut|September 2024 Federal Reserve Rate Cut]].

## September 2023 meeting

At its meeting concluding September 20, 2023 the committee left rates unchanged in a target range of 5.25%–5.50%, a 22-year high, while issuing a more hawkish forecast than markets expected and simultaneously signaling a "soft landing" through higher growth projections.[2] The dot plot showed policymakers still anticipating one more hike in 2023, with the 2024 and 2025 median projections each rising by a half percentage point — a signal that rates would stay higher for longer.[2] Twelve of the nineteen policymakers expected one further hike that year while the remaining seven favored holding steady.[2] The median 2023 growth projection jumped to 2.1% from 1% in June, and officials cut their unemployment forecast, now expecting the jobless rate to peak at 4.1% rather than 4.5%.[2] The statement retained prior language about "the extent of additional policy firming that may be appropriate" and acknowledged that job gains had "slowed" while remaining "strong."[2] For fuller treatment see [[september-2023-fomc-meeting|September 2023 FOMC Meeting]].[2]
