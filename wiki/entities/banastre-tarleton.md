# Banastre Tarleton

Banastre Tarleton was the British officer who commanded the dragoons — mounted troops — opposing the Continental Army during the American Revolution, and is characterized by Badlands as "blood-thirsty."[1]

Tarleton is described as the villain both of the Badlands "Patriot Chronicles" article on Francis Marion and of Mel Gibson's film *The Patriot*.[1] Countering Tarleton's dragoons required cavalry expertise that [[george-washington|George Washington]] himself lacked, and which was supplied by Count [[casimir-pulaski|Casimir Pulaski]] and officers such as Hungarian Colonel [[michael-kovats|Michael Kovats]].[1]

## Background and early career

Tarleton was the highborn son of an industrious money-lender and slave-trader, yet was notoriously lazy and incompetent in commerce.[2] He was good-looking and allegedly charming — bedding the famous mistress of the future King George IV, and afterward her daughter — as well as a gifted athlete with a sharp tongue for wit.[2] After his three brothers rejected his bid to join the family business, fearing he would squander the profits as their late father had, Tarleton took his £5,000 inheritance and blew most of it on women, gambling and drink, using the last £800 to purchase the lowest-level cavalry commission in the British Army, a common practice among sons of affluence.[2]

He came to America seeking gold and glory through conquest, regarding the colonists as nothing more than an opponent on a chessboard and developing a habit of bending the rules so long as results satisfied his superiors.[2] His ascent through the ranks after landing in New York was meteoric and, the article stresses, due to merit rather than nepotism; he had a passion for violence, and warfare suited his sadistic streak.[2] In December 1776, leading a scouting party in New Jersey, he captured General [[charles-lee|Charles Lee]] by threatening to burn down the farmhouse with everyone inside — an act the article suggests may inadvertently have saved the Revolution, since Lee was plotting to usurp Washington.[2]

[[henry-clinton|Sir Henry Clinton]] quickly made Tarleton a lieutenant colonel with a six-hundred-man unit of his own.[2] In lieu of the iconic redcoat he dressed his dragoons in green riding jackets so they would be unmistakable in the field — a choice that backfired given their misconduct — and adopted a fanciful plumed helmet recalling the paladins of the 13th century.[2] The helmet was later adopted by all British cavalry; the green jacket was not, though the British came to call him the "Green Dragoon."[2] Americans developed a very different name for him.[2]

## "Ban the Butcher"

At the outset of the [[siege-of-charleston|siege of Charleston]], Clinton ordered Tarleton to cut off all escape routes and ride down fleeing units, leading to the [[waxhaws-massacre|Waxhaws massacre]], in which he charged surrendering militia after the white flag was raised — 113 killed, 150 severely wounded and some 200 captured in minutes — and gave rise to the phrase "[[tarletons-quarter|Tarleton's Quarter]]."[2] On multiple occasions his men were accused of rape and found guilty by British military tribunal, and his conduct with the British Legion during the southern campaigns of 1780–1781 was later examined as war crimes in a 2012 Military Law Review article by Major John Kiel of the Office of the Judge Advocate General.[2]

With [[francis-marion|Francis Marion]] paralyzing British logistics, [[charles-cornwallis|Lord Cornwallis]] set Tarleton on him.[2] Tarleton answered Marion's ambush at Nelson's Ferry with a psychological operation of his own — false rumors of retreat to Camden, campfires left burning with food cooking, and a staged burning of the home of the deceased Brigadier General Richard Richardson to bait a night attack — while concealing four hundred men and wheeled cannon in the woods, behaving, the article says, "like a Francis Marion wannabe."[2] Warned by Richardson's son, Marion escaped into the swamp at Jack's Creek; after seven hours of pursuit Tarleton relented with the words that christened the Swamp Fox, declaring that "the devil himself could not catch him."[2]

He then returned to the Richardson plantation and beat the widow mercilessly, demanding the location of Marion's men or their families, before burning her home for real along with her cattle and cornfields and seizing her horses.[2] Tarleton went on a broader warpath of burning homes and crops, beating women and hanging men, and was fiercely despised throughout the colonies as evil personified.[2] At the Yorktown surrender ceremonies he was the only officer from either side explicitly excluded; when he asked whether a mistake had been made, he was promptly shut down.[2] Marion is contrasted with him directly as "a true leader of men. (Not a blood-thirsty warmonger, like Banastre Tarleton.)"[2] The character Colonel William Tavington in [[the-patriot-2000-film|The Patriot]] was based on Tarleton, whose portrait by Sir Joshua Reynolds accompanies the article.[2]
