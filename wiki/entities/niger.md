# Niger

Niger is a Sahelian state and member of the [[alliance-of-sahel-states|Alliance of Sahel States]], cited in Badlands commentary as a supplier of uranium.[1]

## Resources and destabilization thesis

Badlands commentary argues that money [[congress|Congress]] approved for [[ukraine|Ukraine]] was always intended to fund militant groups in West Africa so that the [[deep-state|Deep State]] could overthrow governments it had lost control of — specifically countries providing critical resources such as gold ([[burkina-faso|Burkina Faso]]) and uranium (Niger).[1] The same analysis holds that [[ecowas|ECOWAS]] and the [[united-nations|United Nations]] have used terrorist destabilization as justification for threatened military intervention in the Sahel following coups in the region.[1]

## 2023 coup and the French ambassador standoff

A military junta ousted Niger's democratically elected president, [[mohamed-bazoum|Mohamed Bazoum]], in July 2023 and continued to detain him a month later while facing sanctions from Western and regional African powers.[2] The junta exploited popular grievances toward former colonial ruler [[france|France]] and turned to the Russian mercenary group [[wagner-group|Wagner]] for assistance.[2]

In a letter delivered in late August 2023, the Nigerien Foreign Ministry ordered French ambassador [[sylvain-itte|Sylvain Itte]] to leave within 48 hours, accusing him of ignoring a summons and citing "actions of the French government contrary to the interests of Niger."[2] French President [[emmanuel-macron|Emmanuel Macron]] said the ambassador would remain at his post, insisting France was not Niger's enemy and that "one shouldn't give in to the narrative used by the coup leaders that consists of saying France has become our enemy."[2] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] linked the Niger coup to Russia and to the simultaneous expansion of the [[brics|BRICS]] bloc from six to eleven nations, framing both as reactions to the perceived tyranny of the G7 Western globalist bloc.[2]

## Nuland visit and regional pressure, August 2023

Acting Deputy Secretary of State [[victoria-nuland|Victoria Nuland]] made a surprise visit to Niamey on Monday, August 7, 2023, holding what she described as "extremely frank and at times quite difficult" talks with the junta leadership and urging a return to constitutional order; U.S. officials had still not formally applied the term "coup" to the takeover.[3] Her request to see the detained [[mohamed-bazoum|Mohamed Bazoum]] was denied, and she conceded Washington was unlikely to get its way.[3] Among the officials she met was self-declared defense chief General [[moussa-salaou-barmou|Moussa Salaou Barmou]], who had previously been trained by elite American operatives.[3] Nuland publicly warned the junta against forging ties with Russia's [[wagner-group|Wagner]] group.[3]

Regionally, the juntas of Mali and Burkina Faso sent delegations to Niamey to show solidarity with the coup leaders, while [[nigeria|Nigeria]] announced it would cut electricity supply to Niger in line with sanctions decided by [[ecowas|ECOWAS]] under Nigerian President Bola Tinubu.[3] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the coup, which occurred amid failed Russian grain-deal negotiations in Kenya, had been recast in the West as a "Wagner-sponsored coup" against Bazoum, who resistance leaders had called a "French Puppet," and warned that punishing ordinary Nigeriens with sanctions could backfire by generating popular support for restoring ECOWAS and Bazoum.[3] She cautioned that from the United States it was hard to read where the people of Niger actually stood and that "discernment is key."[3]

## French evacuation, August 2023

On Tuesday, August 1, 2023, [[france|France]] began evacuating hundreds of French and European citizens from Niger, a day after [[mali|Mali]] and [[burkina-faso|Burkina Faso]] warned that any outside intervention to restore the ousted government would be treated as a declaration of war.[4] The [[2023-niger-coup|takeover of the government]] was the seventh military coup in less than three years in West and Central Africa, and it destabilized a country the West had considered its most stable ally against a fast-growing Islamist insurgency in the Sahel.[4] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that Niger holds major gold and uranium reserves and that the [[white-house|White House]] counted "over $140 million in development, food security, and humanitarian aid to Niger this year alone."[4]

## Suspension of visa services for Americans, 2025

Niger's embassy in Washington announced on a Thursday that it would stop issuing entry visas to U.S. citizens effective August 28, 2025, with the suspension remaining in effect indefinitely.[5] The move essentially barred most Americans from traveling to Niger unless they already held a valid visa or met specific exemption criteria.[5] Embassy officials did not provide extensive details behind the decision, which came as relations between the two countries deteriorated significantly; in July the U.S. embassy in Niamey had suspended routine visa processing for both immigrant and non-immigrant applicants, citing ongoing "concerns with the Government of Niger."[5]

The breakdown stemmed largely from Niger's military coup and the collapse of long-standing security partnerships between Washington and Niamey, with experts describing visa restrictions as another tool both sides were using to apply pressure.[5] Badlands host [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] dismissed the significance of the move, remarking that few Americans were seeking visas to travel there in any case.[5]

## Junta rule and the Wagner question (August 2023)

Roughly two weeks after the ousting of President [[mohamed-bazoum|Mohamed Bazoum]], Niger remained under the rule of a military junta, and there were suggestions that the coup leaders had requested assistance from Russia's [[wagner-group|Wagner Group]], already present in neighbouring Mali.[6] US Secretary of State [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]] told the BBC that while he did not believe Russia or Wagner had instigated the events in Niger, the group was seeking to "take advantage" of them, and warned of Wagner "possibly manifesting itself" more widely across the Sahel.[6]

[[badlands-media|Badlands]] writers treated the episode as part of a wider African revolt against externally installed leadership. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] relayed remarks from a self-identified Nigerien participant in an X Space who argued that Bazoum was not a hostage but "a puppet," and that the problem extended across the continent: "It's not just Niger! This is everywhere in Africa!"[6] She wrote that the western establishment was fully committed to the Wagner coup narrative while lying "about everything," and voiced support for populations seeking to reclaim their sovereignty.[6] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] observed that the deposed government was backed by the [[european-union|European Union]], the [[united-nations|United Nations]] and the [[state-department|State Department]], while the coup was backed by Russia and Wagner, and noted the low casualty figures recorded to that point.[6]

## End of the French military presence (2023)

French President [[emmanuel-macron|Emmanuel Macron]] announced on September 24, 2023 that France would end its military presence in Niger by the end of the year, capping months of high tension since the July junta takeover.[7] He said Paris was ending military cooperation with "the de facto authorities of Niger because they don't want to fight terrorism anymore," while continuing to withhold recognition of the junta and to treat ousted President [[mohamed-bazoum|Mohamed Bazoum]] as the country's only legitimate authority.[7] Macron added that France was "not there to deal with internal politics and be hostages of putschists," and the withdrawal was to be organized in the following weeks.[7] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the withdrawal as the conclusion of French colonialism in the resource-rich nation and, noting that [[ecowas|ECOWAS]] had yet to accept defeat, expressed hope that Nigeriens would govern themselves without foreign interference.[7]

## US military withdrawal standoff (March 2024)

In March 2024 the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] was reported to be seeking a way for U.S. troops to remain in Niger despite the military-led government's weekend directive that they leave, following accusations by American officials that Niger could be poised to transfer uranium to Iran — allegations Niger rejected with outrage.[8] The country hosts a key base for U.S. counterterrorism operations in sub-Saharan Africa.[8]

Pentagon spokesperson Sabrina Singh said the United States was seeking "clarification" from Nigerien officials and remained engaged in lengthy and direct discussions with the junta leaders, while Washington warned the West African nation against forging deeper ties with "rogue" actors such as Russia and Iran.[8] White House press secretary [[karine-jean-pierre|Karine Jean-Pierre]] said the Biden administration was "closely monitoring the Russian defense activities" there in order "to assess and mitigate potential risk to U.S. personnel, interests and assets," with the [[wagner-group|Wagner Group]] cited as a Russian mercenary outfit that has made deep inroads into the region.[8]

Over that weekend Niger's military-led government, the National Council for the Safeguard of the Homeland (CNSP), declared U.S. flights over Nigerien territory to be "illegal."[8]

## Russian troops at Airbase 101, May 2024

In early May 2024, a senior US defense official told Reuters that Russian military personnel had entered an air base in Niger hosting American troops, a development that followed the decision by Niger's junta to expel US forces.[9] The military officers ruling the West African nation had told the United States to withdraw its nearly 1,000 military personnel from the country, which until the 2023 coup had been a key partner for Washington's campaign against insurgents who had killed thousands and displaced millions.[9]

The official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said Russian forces were not mingling with US troops but were using a separate hangar at Airbase 101, which sits next to Diori Hamani International Airport in Niamey, the Nigerien capital.[9] The move placed US and Russian troops in close proximity at a time of increasingly acrimonious military and diplomatic rivalry over the conflict in Ukraine, and raised questions about the fate of US installations in the country after a withdrawal.[9]

Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that the co-habitation of a base by Russian and American forces made sense within a framing in which US military operations against "rebel factions" — in Africa as in the Middle East — are in fact directed against [[cia|CIA]] proxy militias, and recalled the November 2017 Trump–Putin statement on fighting ISIS jointly.[9]


## Suspension of the BBC (December 2024)

In December 2024 Niger suspended [[bbc|BBC]] programming for three months, accusing the British state broadcaster of spreading false information that could destabilize the country amid its decade-long jihadist insurgency.[10] The state-owned outlet ANP published a letter from the media regulator ordering affiliated local press groups to halt the channel's programs "with immediate effect," and the communications ministry said the BBC had aired "erroneous information tending to destabilize social tranquility and undermine the morale of the troops."[10] The measure added the BBC to a growing list of Western media agencies sanctioned by Niger's military government and its allies in neighboring Burkina Faso and Mali.[10]

Authorities cited no specific broadcast, but the ban followed reports by several Western outlets that jihadists had killed 90 soldiers and at least 40 civilians at Chatoumane in Niger's Tera region near the Burkina Faso border — an attack Niger's leadership denied had taken place, dismissing the reports as "baseless assertions" and a "campaign of intoxication."[10]
