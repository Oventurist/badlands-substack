# José "Pepe" Figueres Ferrer

José "Pepe" Figueres Ferrer was a Costa Rican planter, engineer and three-term president whom Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presents as the central historical example of how a nation can install durable **safeguards** against banking and intelligence-agency capture of its government.[1] Lange argues that Figueres is "someone that nobody knows about except the people of Costa Rica, who regard him as a hero, much like we view Donald Trump," and that he deserves recognition as one of the great leaders of modern history.[1]

## Background and the 1948 civil war

According to a 1990 [[new-york-times|New York Times]] editorial quoted at length in the article, Figueres was "a planter who studied engineering at M.I.T." who "plunged into politics in 1948 to challenge a coup carried out by the army in alliance with Communist-led guerrillas."[1] The trigger, as Lange frames it, was an election in which the incumbent — described in the article as a figure controlled by the cabal — lost, and then directed the legislature controlled by his own political party to annul the result so that he could stay in power.[1]

That annulment provoked an uprising led by Figueres. Leading an irregular force of roughly 700 fighters, he prevailed over the army and the guerrillas in a conflict — the [[costa-rican-civil-war|Costa Rican Civil War]] — that lasted only 44 days.[1]

## The provisional junta

Figueres then headed a provisional junta that ruled for **18 months**. The New York Times summarised its record: it "put into effect basic welfare legislation, promoted education, nationalized banks and disbanded the armed forces. Then it stepped down, and ever since, Costa Ricans have settled their arguments constitutionally."[1] Lange emphasises the voluntary transfer of power: after defeating the military and removing what he calls the fraudulent government, Figueres handed authority back to the president the people had actually elected — "something that rarely ever happens" and something he argues should be celebrated in history rather than hidden.[1]

During those 18 months Figueres also oversaw the election of a new constitutional assembly, which adopted a new constitution designed to protect the people's rights and embed safeguards against capture.[1]

## The safeguards

Lange catalogues the protections Figueres put in place before stepping down:[1]

- **Abolition of the armed forces.** Dissolving the army removed the centralized instrument a cabal could use to seize control from the people; the safeguard was later written into the constitution. Lange argues this is a major reason the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]]-linked [[shadow-government|shadow government]] was never able to install a dictator in Costa Rica, unlike its neighbours whose militaries were trained at the [[school-of-the-americas|School of the Americas]].[1]
- **Term limits on the presidency.** Citing [[freedom-house|Freedom House]], the article notes the president is directly elected for a four-year term and may seek only a **non-consecutive** second term, with a 40 percent threshold to avoid a runoff. Barring consecutive terms makes consolidation of power far harder.[1]
- **Term limits in the legislature.** Deputies of the 57-seat unicameral Legislative Assembly are elected every four years by proportional representation and "may not run for two consecutive terms, but may run again after skipping a term," which prevents career politicians.[1]
- **The [[cedula|cédula]] national citizen ID**, used as the sole voting credential and issued only to citizens, which the article credits with eliminating voter fraud in post-1948 Costa Rican elections.[1]
- **Nationalization of the banking system** and the creation of a permanent [[public-banking-option|public banking option]] — four publicly owned banks competing with private institutions, whose purpose is to protect depositors' money rather than profit from it.[1]

## Politics and posture toward the CIA

Figueres won the presidency in his own right in 1953 and again in 1970, after having handed power back in 1949.[1] The New York Times recorded that during his three elected terms "nobody was in doubt about the views of Don Pepe, as everybody called him. He bitterly opposed the Somoza regime in Nicaragua, yet also broke with Fidel Castro when the Cuban revolution lurched leftward. He supported John F. Kennedy's Alliance for Progress, but not the C.I.A.'s clandestine wars with Cuba."[1] Lange reads this as evidence that Figueres supported [[john-f-kennedy|JFK]] but opposed clandestine CIA operations because he had seen firsthand how much they devastated Central America.[1] He was, in Lange's framing, an **"outsider"** in the same sense that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] is an outsider — a leader who cared more about the people than about power, viewed in Costa Rica much as Americans view George Washington.[1]

## Hero of Peace designation

Citing CulturaCR.net, the article reports that the legislative plenary approved on first debate bill 22,289 declaring the former president **"Hero of peace."** The measure passed two months after the same deputies removed the title of traitor of the Homeland from former president [[rafael-angel-calderon-fournier|Rafael Ángel Calderón Fournier]]; only deputies José María Villalta of the Frente Amplio and Paola Vega of the PAC voted against.[1]

## Significance in the Badlands argument

Lange uses Figueres as a "future proves past" template: the safeguards he installed have, in Lange's account, held for decades under sustained assault by the global cabal, the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]] and the [[world-bank|World Bank]], and could be adopted by many countries once that cabal is removed.[1] He frames Trump's project in the same terms — restoring the constitution and installing safeguards, including an American [[public-banking-option|public banking option]] and a federal citizenship-proving voter ID, so that power remains with the people long after Trump is gone.[1]
