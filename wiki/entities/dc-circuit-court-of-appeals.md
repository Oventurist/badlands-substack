# U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit

The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit is the federal appellate court with jurisdiction over the District of Columbia and, in practice, over much of the litigation involving federal agencies, executive privilege, and the Freedom of Information Act. Its docket makes it the most consequential intermediate court for disputes between the government and those seeking its records.

## The Biden–Hur audio ruling

In the matter reported in the July 22, 2026 Badlands Brief, the D.C. Circuit denied an emergency request by [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] for an injunction blocking release of audio recordings and transcripts of his 2016–2017 private conversations with ghostwriter [[mark-zwonitzer|Mark Zwonitzer]]. Those recordings had been obtained by the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] during Special Counsel [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]]'s classified-documents investigation and were slated for redacted release to the [[heritage-foundation|Heritage Foundation]], [[mike-howell|Mike Howell]], and the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]].

The panel held that Biden had not shown a likelihood of success on the merits. It emphasized a substantial public interest in allowing citizens to assess whether the government had gone easy on a former vice president, while observing that redactions would protect sensitive personal details and that much of the content was already public through Hur's report and Biden's memoir. The court extended an administrative stay until August 3, 2026 to permit any further review.

The opinion's operative language — quoted at length by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] — states that "when a highly visible investigation of a prominent public figure ends in a decision not to charge, the public has a strong interest in evaluating 'whether the government had the evidence but nevertheless pulled its punches,'" and that the requested materials are germane to that interest.

## Corpus significance

Badlands commentary treats the ruling as an unusual instance of a Washington institution siding with public disclosure against a former president, and reads it as an inflection point toward what the corpus calls the justice phase — particularly with respect to the [[autopen-scandal|autopen scandal]] and the question of who was actually exercising presidential authority.
