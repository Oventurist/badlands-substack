# John Kenneth Galbraith

John Kenneth Galbraith — also referred to as John K. or "Ken" Galbraith — was a Canadian-American diplomat, economist, policymaker and public intellectual identified by Badlands Media as one of the three progenitors of the Davos group alongside [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] and [[herman-kahn|Herman Kahn]].[1]

## Keynesian lineage

Galbraith was a disciple of [[john-maynard-keynes|John Maynard Keynes]], the economist celebrated by mainstream historians for revolutionizing how economics is taught and practiced in the West.[1] Badlands Media notes that dissenting researchers have characterized Keynes far less charitably, alleging he was a Bolshevik sympathizer, a pedophile and a servant of international banking cartels.[1] The article quotes Keynes's observation that "by a continuous process of inflation, governments can confiscate, secretly and unobserved, an important part of the wealth of their citizens," a process that "not one man in a million can diagnose."[1]

## Career and elite networks

Galbraith became as involved in elite machinations as his mentor, co-founding [[americans-for-democratic-action|Americans for Democratic Action]] alongside figures including Eleanor Roosevelt, Arthur Schlesinger Jr. and Ronald Reagan.[1] In 1948 he returned to [[harvard-university|Harvard]] as a lecturer in agricultural forestry and land-use policy and was soon installed as a professor, where he would meet [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]].[1]

## Role in founding the World Economic Forum

By the late 1960s Kissinger and Galbraith were considered two of the foremost lecturers, authors and educators in America, both increasingly focused on foreign policy for the United States and the emerging new Europe.[1] It was around this time that Kissinger introduced Schwab to Galbraith, who would go on to help Schwab make the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] a reality.[1] In 1970 Galbraith traveled through Europe with Herman Kahn supporting Schwab's recruitment drive for the first European Management Symposium, and in 1971 he delivered the keynote speech at the organization's historic first session.[1]
