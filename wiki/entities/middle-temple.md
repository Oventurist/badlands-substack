# Middle Temple

The Middle Temple is one of the London Inns of Court, cited by Badlands Media as evidence of British legal influence over the early American republic.[1]

## Role in the Badlands argument

In its analysis of [[thomas-jefferson]]'s warning about a [[bastard-system-of-laws|bastard system of laws]], Badlands Media notes that just after the Revolutionary War was won, four out of the five first [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] justices in the newly federalized Union were trained at the Middle Temple in London.[1] The article presents this alongside the observation that Delaware still practices equity law in the manner it was practiced in eighteenth-century England, as indications that Crown-aligned legal traditions were carried into the American system by way of the federalists.[1]

## Named justices and the inns of court (February 2023)

A February 16, 2023 Badlands Media essay expanded on the Middle Temple's role, describing it as a prestigious "inn" of the [[british-crown|Crown]] — an institution acting simultaneously as a law school and as a source of original law — and as one of four English [[inns-of-court|inns of court]] responsible for creating much of what is recognized today as [[equity-jurisprudence|equity jurisprudence]], including the chancery courts.[2] The essay stated that four out of the five first Supreme Court justices in the Union were trained at the Middle Temple in London, naming them as Edward Tilghman, William Rawle, Jared Ingersoll and Alexander J. Dallas.[2]

The author asked how odd it is that the very first [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] proceedings were presided over by men trained in London immediately after a war fought to gain independence from England, concluding that it stands to reason the separation from England was never as complete as Americans have been told.[2] Readers were referred to Charles Warren's *History of the American Bar* for more on the presence of Middle Temple-trained jurists in the early United States, and to the [[delaware-court-of-chancery|Delaware Court of Chancery]] as evidence that eighteenth-century British equity law still operates in an American state.[2]
