# Internal Revenue Service

The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) is the federal tax agency whose establishment the article attributes to the [[revenue-act-of-1913|Revenue Act of 1913]], signed into law by President [[woodrow-wilson|Woodrow Wilson]] on October 3, 1913.[1]

Together with the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], created later that same year, the IRS is described as one half of the apparatus by which every American came to be bound to the [[fiat-currency|fiat]] monetary system: obliged to pay income tax while watching the continued devaluation of their money.[1] The [[pujo-committee|Pujo Committee]] was formed to investigate the banking monopoly after the creation of both the Federal Reserve and the IRS.[1]

The article's closing vision — buying a house for $5,000 or a car for $1,000, with no debt and a full paycheck free of tax — is framed as achievable with "no IRS or no Federal Reserve."[1]

## As an example of unknowable law

A May 2023 Badlands essay by [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]] used the IRS to illustrate its argument that the [[rule-of-law|rule of law]] has broken down, asking whether IRS agents have the entire revenue code — Title 26 of the US Code — memorised, and if not, how they can possibly enforce it in real-time situations.[2] The author likened the practice to attempting a game of chess without knowing how all the pieces move, presenting the agency as a case study in law that is neither decipherable nor accessible to those obliged to obey it.[2]

## As an instrument of compliance

In an April 2024 essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] treated the IRS as the enforcement mechanism that keeps Americans paying into a system he regards as illegitimate, writing that he files and pays his taxes on time despite believing taxes are theft because he is unwilling to risk jail and leave his family destitute.[3] He posed the question directly: "If not for the threat of the IRS, who would continue to stay in line?" — pairing it with the observation that "if not for the funding of the system, there would be no system."[3]

## Creation and purpose

In *A Week to Remember*, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] observes that the income tax, the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the IRS were all created in the same year, around 1913, and insists this is not a coincidence.[4] He argues Americans "live in fear of the IRS, which is why it was created," casting the agency as the coercive backstop for a tax system he considers unnecessary to fund the government and useful chiefly for expanding the size and control of the state.[4]

## Trump tax-return leak lawsuit and 2026 settlement

In January 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], joined by Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump, filed a $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS and the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] over the leaking of his tax returns, accusing the agency of unlawfully disclosing his tax records from his first term through a government contractor.[5] In May 2026 Trump settled the suit; his legal team filed notice in federal court in Miami seeking dismissal with prejudice, meaning the case cannot be refiled, and argued that because the lawsuit remained in its early stages it could be dismissed voluntarily without court approval or IRS consent.[5]

Under the settlement the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] established the [[anti-weaponization-fund|Anti-Weaponization Fund]], totaling $1.776 billion and financed through the federal judgment fund, to review and address claims of alleged government weaponization and lawfare, with provision for formal apologies as well as monetary relief for eligible claimants.[5]

## Joint nonprofit terror-funding task force (2026)

In March 2026 IRS Criminal Investigations agents joined [[fbi|FBI]] agents in creating a joint task force to investigate nonprofits suspected of funding [[domestic-terrorism|domestic terrorism]] or political violence, an operation described to CBS News as a "mission control command center" based at the FBI, with the IRS agents serving on temporary one-year assignments.[6] The effort ran alongside a [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] task force probing [[antifa|antifa]]-linked funding and a directive for US attorney offices to assign domestic terrorism coordinators.[6] It followed a December memo from Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] that flagged tax fraud, alongside potential violence and anti-American or anti-religious ideologies, as a key concern in investigations of extremist groups.[6]

## Hunter Biden whistleblowers (2023)

In June 2023 two IRS whistleblowers released testimony alleging that the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] under Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] had buried evidence of [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s tax crimes and prevented U.S. Attorney [[david-weiss|David Weiss]] from filing charges in two different jurisdictions the previous year.[7] The IRS had sought felony charges and sent its recommendations to the Justice Department, where, as journalist Byron York summarized, they "came out as two misdemeanors."[7]

One of the whistleblowers was [[gary-shapley|Gary Shapley]], who had publicly revealed his identity weeks earlier.[7] According to the testimony, the agency was notified of potential evidence "in the guest house of former Vice President Biden" but was rebuffed by prosecutor [[lesley-wolf|Lesley Wolf]], who said no such search warrant "would ever get approved."[7] Representative [[jason-smith|Jason Smith]] added that Weiss had been denied special counsel status in the case.[7]

## Abolition proposals (2024)

In April 2024 Republican Georgia Representatives [[buddy-carter|Buddy Carter]] and [[andrew-clyde|Andrew Clyde]] introduced a resolution designating April 16 as "FairTax Day," tied to H.R. 25, the [[fairtax-act|FairTax Act]], which would replace the federal tax code with a national consumption tax and eliminate both the IRS and Tax Day.[8] Carter argued that Americans spend over six billion hours per year filing their taxes and that the current system "punishes success and puts unelected bureaucrats in charge of your paycheck."[8]

## Clinton Foundation criminal probe (2019)

Under President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s first administration, the IRS opened a criminal tax investigation into the [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]] and its dealings with other players on the global charity stage, only to abruptly stop working with whistleblowers in the spring of 2019, according to IRS memos and internal emails obtained under the Freedom of Information Act and reviewed by Just the News.[9] One memo records agents saying "Can't talk about the CF" as they cut off contact with two whistleblowers, one of them a decorated former federal money laundering analyst who had testified before [[congress|Congress]] about terrorism financing.[9] Badlands presented the documents as evidence that [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] and IRS investigations were deliberately shut down to protect political elites.[9]

Badlands contributor GMONEY, commenting in the same edition on Federal Reserve policy, wrote that "the Abolish the IRS movement can only succeed when enough of us stop voluntarily participating in outright theft."[9]

### AI-driven enforcement initiative (September 2023)

In a statement issued 8 September 2023, the IRS announced a "sweeping, historic" tax enforcement initiative funded by the Inflation Reduction Act and built around artificial intelligence and other advanced technologies for detecting tax evasion.[10] Commissioner [[danny-werfel|Danny Werfel]] said "there is a sea change taking place at the IRS in every aspect of our operations," describing a completed top-to-bottom review of enforcement and a renewed focus on those "abusing the nation's tax laws."[10] According to Werfel, improved technology and AI would help compliance teams detect cheating, identify emerging compliance threats and improve case selection so as to avoid burdening taxpayers with unnecessary "no-change" audits.[10]

### Third-party summons authority (Polselli, 2023)

In [[polselli-v-internal-revenue-service|Polselli v. Internal Revenue Service]], decided in May 2023, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] unanimously held that the IRS need not notify innocent bank account holders when it issues summonses for their records in aid of collecting a delinquent taxpayer's assessment, resolving a circuit split.[11] The opinion, written by Chief Justice [[john-roberts|John Roberts]], construed the exception at Internal Revenue Code § 7609(c)(2)(D)(i), which permits notice to be withheld when a third-party summons is issued "in aid of collection," an assessment or judgment exists against the delinquent taxpayer, and both the assessment and the collection activity pertain to the same taxpayer.[11] While a win for the agency, the Court expressed apprehension about the scope of its summons power and declined to define "in aid of the collection," leaving room for future limits.[11]

Badlands contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] framed the ruling as an assault on due process and privacy that would fall hardest on small business owners and self-employed citizens while leaving wealthy tax avoiders unexamined.[11]

## 2025 data-sharing deal with ICE and leadership exodus

In April 2025 several top IRS officials, including the acting commissioner, prepared to resign after the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration pushed the agency to use its protected tax records to help homeland security officials deport immigrants.[12] The [[irs-ice-taxpayer-data-sharing-agreement|agreement]] with [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|Immigration and Customs Enforcement]], disclosed in a court filing on Monday, April 7, 2025, was described as a fundamental departure from decades of practice at the tax collector, which had sought to keep information submitted by undocumented immigrants confidential.[12] Under its terms ICE officials can request information about people ordered to leave the United States or otherwise under investigation, though the filings said no information had yet been shared.[12]

Federal law tightly controls taxpayer information, protecting home addresses, earnings, and other data from disclosure even to other government agencies, and IRS officials had for weeks warned during negotiations that the plan could be illegal.[12] The deal fueled further turmoil at the top of an agency already rocked by mass layoffs and several leadership changes during filing season, its busiest period of the year.[12]

## Hunter Biden laptop referral (2023)

On February 1, 2023, [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s attorney [[abbe-lowell|Abbe Lowell]] sent the IRS one of three letters — the others going to the Delaware attorney general and the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]]'s National Security Division — requesting investigations into what he called attempts to "weaponize" the contents of the [[hunter-biden-laptop|Hunter Biden laptop]].[13]

## October 2025 shutdown furloughs

During the [[october-2025-government-shutdown|October 2025 government shutdown]], the IRS furloughed nearly half of its workforce on Wednesday, October 8, 2025, according to an updated contingency plan posted to its website.[14] Most IRS operations were closed, the agency said in a separate letter to its workers.[14] Only 39,870 employees, or 53.6 percent of the workforce, remained working as the shutdown continued, and it was unclear which workers would stay on the job.[14]

The agency's initial Lapsed Appropriations Contingency Plan, which provided for the first five business days of operations, had stated that the department would remain open using [[inflation-reduction-act|Inflation Reduction Act]] funds.[14] The furloughs followed the failure of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and [[congress|Congress]] to strike an agreement to fund federal operations, with the shutdown entering its second week and no discernible endgame in sight.[14] [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] contributor [[gmoney|GMONEY]] commented that "the IRS will literally have nobody left and people will still comply," a remark reflecting the Badlands view that the agency's power rests more on voluntary compliance than on enforcement capacity.[14]

## 2023 filing season warnings

Ahead of the 2023 filing season the IRS issued several releases warning taxpayers about potentially lower refunds, prompting analysts to describe the change as a "double whammy" for families who may have anticipated an expanded child tax credit.[15] Badlands Media contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] described the expiry of COVID-era tax breaks as a major blow to an economy already near breaking point, compounded by the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s rising interest rates, which he characterized as a deliberate currency constriction justified as inflation control.[15] He tied the pattern to a debt-based model in which international banking interests extend cheap credit during crises and later trigger constriction, absorbing assets from bankrupted firms, comparing conditions to the run-up to the Federal Reserve system in 1907 and predicting continued pressure until a [[great-reset|Great Reset]] inaugurates a new global financial system.[15]

## First chief executive officer, 2025

In October 2025, Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] appointed [[frank-bisignano|Frank Bisignano]], the head of the [[social-security-administration|Social Security Administration]], to serve simultaneously as the first-ever chief executive officer of the Internal Revenue Service — a structure that bypassed the Senate confirmation process, with Bessent remaining acting commissioner.[16] Bessent cited shared "customer service and technological goals" between the IRS and SSA, while Bisignano pointed to his experience running "multiple mission-critical organizations at the same time," including his tenure at JPMorgan Chase as co-chief operating officer and CEO of its mortgage banking unit.[16]

### 2025 abolition push
On February 19, 2025, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick said on Fox News that President Donald Trump's goal was to "abolish" the Internal Revenue Service.[17] The remarks came as the agency was reportedly poised to lay off thousands of workers, and built on Trump's pledge to create an "External Revenue Service" to oversee tariffs and other potential foreign revenue.[17] Trump had also floated abolishing federal income taxes as part of a plan of "tariffing and taxing foreign nations to enrich our citizens."[17] Lutnick framed the sequence as DOGE cutting $1 trillion in spending followed by eliminating "tax scams" and raising a trillion dollars of revenue, so that "all the outsiders pay."[17]

## April 2025 Harvard tax-exemption controversy

Republicans had contended for years that the IRS was weaponized against conservatives, but in April 2025 it was President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] who publicly raised the prospect of the agency removing a valuable tax benefit from a perceived opponent.[18] The administration's attempt to revoke [[harvard-university|Harvard University]]'s tax-exempt status was reported as potentially undercutting the agency's efforts to assure the public that politics is not part of tax enforcement.[18]

In the Oval Office on Thursday, April 17, 2025, Trump alleged that the Biden administration had engaged in "very, very illegal" use of the IRS for political ends; asked why his moves against Harvard differed, he replied that he considered Harvard "a disgrace" and described the school as anti-Semitic.[18] He hinted at future actions against other universities and nonprofits, naming [[citizens-for-responsibility-and-ethics-in-washington|Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington]].[18] The request that the IRS cancel Harvard's exemption — which permits tax-deductible donations and shields net earnings from income tax — was one prong of a broader administration battle with the university.[18] In response to the episode, Senator [[mike-lee|Mike Lee]] called for abolishing both the IRS and the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]].[18]

## 2023 Hunter Biden disclosure lawsuit

In September 2023 the agency was sued by [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]], who alleged that IRS agents unlawfully disclosed his tax return information and that the agency failed to establish safeguards ensuring the confidentiality of his records.[19] The complaint, filed in federal court in Washington, D.C. and assigned to Judge [[timothy-kelly|Timothy Kelly]], sought disclosure documents, $1,000 per unauthorized disclosure, and attorneys' fees, and centered on statements by agency whistleblowers [[gary-shapley|Gary Shapley]] and [[joseph-ziegler|Joseph Ziegler]].[19] The matter is covered at [[hunter-biden-v-internal-revenue-service|Hunter Biden v. Internal Revenue Service]].[19]

## Whistleblower disclosures of June 2023

IRS criminal supervisory special agent [[gary-shapley|Gary Shapley]], a fourteen-year veteran of the agency, alleged in June 2023 that [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] had received preferential treatment from investigators examining his tax affairs and his alleged false statements on a gun permit application.[20] Shapley's testimony, released through congressional Republicans, became the proximate trigger for House Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]]'s threat to open an impeachment inquiry into Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] by July 6, 2023.[20]

## Civil War origins and 1913 revival

Badlands Media commentary notes that the IRS had existed since 1862, formed to collect money for the [[american-civil-war|Civil War]], and largely faded afterward once there was no longer a real need for it.[24] The agency was reformed on 2/25/1913 — a few weeks after ratification of the [[sixteenth-amendment|16th Amendment]] on 2/3/1913 and ten months before the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] was created.[24] The article argues the new income tax existed to pay interest to the bankers running the Federal Reserve, and that because Americans balked at the idea the tax began at one percent and rose gradually, boiling-frog fashion, to modern rates; had it begun at 15 or 20 percent, it contends, there would have been another revolution.[24] It calls 1913 a worse year than 2020, as it produced the IRS, the income tax and the Federal Reserve together.[24]

## Role in the "four pillars" framework

Badlands contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] treats the IRS, alongside the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the income tax, as one of the three 1913 creations that mark the capture of the financial system — the second of his [[four-pillars-of-government-control|four pillars of government control]].[21] In his account, income tax and inflation are used to make Americans debt slaves, and the IRS exists to ensure that no one wanders "too far off of the debt plantation."[21] Americans are allowed to keep enough of their paycheck to survive but not to thrive, with debt and taxes serving as the chains that hold them captive; doubters, he suggests, should try not paying their mortgage or property taxes and see how long they are permitted to remain in their homes.[21] He reads [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s "no tax on tips" proposal as a signal that income tax and excessive interest rates would soon end, and links it to the July 2024 attempt on Trump's life.[21]

## Claim that no law compels income tax (March 2023)

A March 28, 2023 Badlands Media essay on [[trust-law|trust law]] and the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] asserted that the arrangement between the Federal Reserve and the American people is not absolute, offering as evidence its claim that there is no law binding anyone to pay income tax to the IRS.[22] The author presented this as proof that Americans are less bound to what the piece called the private credit trust of the Federal Reserve system than commonly assumed, and referred readers to an accompanying video for the details rather than setting out the legal argument in the text.[22]

The point served the essay's broader conclusion that new arrangements outside the existing credit trust remain legally available, and that legal literacy is the precondition for pursuing them.[22]

## Predicted abolition in "Illusions of Democracy"

In his January 2024 article "Illusions of Democracy," [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] describes the IRS as the collection arm of a debt-based monetary system.[25] He argues Americans are trapped in an arrangement in which money does not belong to them but is a debt instrument on which interest is paid continuously, purposely devalued each year by a system that creates inflation by printing more debt — and that the IRS was created to collect those debt payments from the American people.[25] On that basis he states plainly: "I don't believe the IRS will even exist when we win this war."[25]

Lange pairs the claim with a call for safeguards protecting citizens' savings from being stolen through inflation and taxation, arguing that a protected personal savings account is a vital structural protection and that the government must be prevented from draining it.[25]
