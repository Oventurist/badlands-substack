# Hellfire Club

The Hellfire Club is referenced in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as the emblem of an occultism embedded throughout the British imperial elite.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

Discussing [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]]'s painting of Saul and the Witch of Endor — in which King Saul, having expelled the oracles from his kingdom, secretly consults a witch and by believing her forecast of defeat and death ensures his own destruction — Ehret suggests one message West may be communicating concerns this well-known occultism within the British elite, which West and [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] understood viscerally.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance) He observes that a republican painter treating such a theme would have been making a great many enemies among the intelligentsia of the Hellfire Club's devotees and their heirs.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-new-american-renaissance)

## Origins and structure

A second Badlands essay, "In Defense of Benjamin Franklin," describes the Hellfire Club as having been formed in the early eighteenth century as a central agency of occult operations across the British Empire.[2] Its ceremonies revived the [[eleusinian-mysteries|Eleusinian Mysteries]], performed by leading figures of London society in underground caverns beneath [[medmenham-abbey|Medmenham Abbey]], leased by [[francis-dashwood|Sir Francis Dashwood]], Chancellor of the Exchequer of England.[2] Dashwood dubbed his branch "The Order of Knights of St. Francis," sometimes called "The Medmenham Monks," and placed above the abbey entrance a banner reading "Do What thou Wilt," later adopted by Aleister Crowley as the basis of his theology of Thelema.[2]

Member [[john-wilkes|John Wilkes]], a Royal Society member described as the grandfather of Lincoln's assassin, recorded that "No profane eye dared to penetrate the English Eleusinian Mysteries of the Chapter Room where the Monks assembled on solemn occasions."[2] The satirist [[william-hogarth|William Hogarth]] mocked the club in his portrait of Dashwood dressed as St Francis of Assisi with an erotic novel in place of a bible.[2] [[albert-pike|Albert Pike]] is quoted on the Templar–Jesuit lineage the essay reads into the order's naming.[2]

## Philosophy and political reach

[[bernard-mandeville|Bernard Mandeville]] is identified as the leading philosopher of the Hellfire Club and the spiritual father of laissez-faire economics, holding that evil, vice and corruption were the ideal means of the state's controlling its servants, or the drones of the hive.[2] Quoting [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]], the essay states that in 1720 — the year of the [[south-sea-bubble|South Sea Bubble]] — the Hell-Fire clubs played a major part in an intended shock treatment against Britain, the most prominent founded that year by the new Lord Wharton, and that in 1721 a King's Order-in-Council banned the clubs at least in their public forms.[2]

By the 1750s, the essay asserts, literally every member of the British government belonged to Dashwood's Hellfire Club, and the agency was rapidly penetrating masonic lodges beginning with the Grand Lodge of England established in 1717.[2] Upon its creation the club is said to have taken control of the management of global networks of witch covens and black mass cults already seeded into the English colonies in the wake of the 1642–1660 Civil War.[2] In Boston, [[increase-mather|Increase Mather]] charged the printer [[james-franklin|James Franklin]] with promoting a "Hell-Fire Club" in 1722.[2] The essay's central claim is that [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]]'s documented involvement with England's Hell-Fire Club during 1728–1730 was a counter-intelligence operation on behalf of [[cotton-mather|Cotton Mather]]'s republican resistance, not genuine membership.[2]
