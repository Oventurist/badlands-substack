# Arabic Naming Conventions

The article devotes a section to explaining Arabic names, which it treats as a major obstacle to Western readers but fundamentally simple and not unlike the naming conventions of medieval Europe or Game of Thrones.[1]

## Patronym

Arabic uses a system of patronym to identify an individual: if your name was John, your father Roger, and your family name Smith, you would say "I am John, son of Roger, of the House Smith," or informally "John, son of Roger."[1] In the Middle East everybody has a single name and is identified through patriarchal lineage, which matters because the entire societal structure rests on a tribal system of clans: the nuclear family is the base unit, many families make a clan, many clans a tribe, and many tribes a "Skull."[1]

## Laqab and kunya

A person of significance may carry a descriptive laqab, an honorary title, which the Romans called an agnomen — the article offers Francis "The Swamp Fox" Marion, Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson, and Dwayne "The Rock" Johnson as parallels, noting that as in Europe such titles are no longer common.[1] Syrian President [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al-Assad]]'s surname means "the lion"; in the 19th century an ancestor was given the laqab al-Wahsh, "the wild beast," for his prowess as a fighter, and in 1927 the family changed it to the nobler version.[1] A kunya is a nickname — as with [[hashim-ibn-abd-manaf|Hashim]], "he who feeds the starving" and "the pulverizer of evil," or [[abu-bakr|Abu Bakr]], "the father of camels."[1]

## Articles and prefixes

The al- prefix in a surname can denote a person's place of origin, while "Al" as used in "Al Saud" is a dynastic designation referring to a specific family.[1] "Ahl" is a broader term: "Ahl al-Bayt" means "People of the House [of the Prophet]," a group the [[shia-islam|Shia]] limit to Muhammad's cousin, daughter, son-in-law, two grandsons and eleven wives, while the [[sunni-islam|Sunni]] expand it to all of the Banu Hashim.[1] Only [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] still uses these articles in its names; others have dropped them for more westernized conventions.[1]

Finally, although Arabic is a phonetic language it uses its own alphabet of glyphs, which is why a single word or name yields a variety of romanized spellings for Western readers.[1]
