# 1 Samuel 8

1 Samuel 8 is the biblical chapter in which the Israelites demand a human king, and God — speaking through the prophet Samuel — warns of the consequences. In Badlands Media commentary it functions as the archetypal case study of a people voluntarily choosing subjugation, and is the scriptural spine of the essay "Revolution Founded at the Crossroads" (October 31, 2024).[1]

## Narrative context

Samuel is described as the last of the Judges, one of many figures raised up by God during that era to deliver Israel from oppression that followed the nation's repeated rejection of God in favor of man-made gods.[1] The author argues that when humanity forsakes God for idolatry it "inevitably trends towards corruption, lawlessness, and evil, bringing individual and societal consequences."[1] Against that backdrop the Israelites approach God through Samuel and ask for "a king to judge us like all the nations."[1]

## The warning

The essay reads God's response as a systematic inventory of tyranny. Kings would take sons and daughters as servants in the military, fields, factories, kitchens, and other industries — meaning, in the author's gloss, that "kings would demand sovereignty."[1] Kings would seize the best property and redistribute confiscated wealth to others, reaping without sowing, which the author likens to socialism.[1] Kings would demand a portion of the grain, wine, and sheep, along with laboring donkeys and the finest young men and servants — that is, taxes.[1] The list ends with the declaration "and you will be his servants," and the author notes that the Hebrew root ʿeḇeḏ also means slave, subject, and worshipper.[1]

The author's summary is that God classifies rulers who enrich themselves by politically or physically forcing taxes, confiscation, and labor services as practicing slavery — an application of "Thou shalt not steal" (Exodus 20:15 KJV).[1]

## Free will and the doubling down

The chapter is also used to argue that God permits what He forbids in order to preserve free will: freedom and love, if forced, become tyranny and rape respectively, so God designed humans with the ability to choose and allowed the Israelites their king despite forewarning grave consequences and invoking the Exodus from Egypt.[1] After hearing the warnings the Israelites doubled down, adding that they wanted a king to "go out before us and fight our battles" — which the author reads as running from God, "attempting to encode more logic to get around God," and placing trust in man-made god-kings.[1]

## Modern application

The essay treats 1 Samuel 8 as directly parallel to the present, arguing that contemporary god-kings likewise "demand sovereignty, worship, and taxes," and that the choice facing individuals — reject idolatry and accept freedom, or beg for a ruler — is unchanged.[1] The chapter thus anchors the essay's claim that [[individual-sovereignty|individual sovereignty]] is a spiritual decision before it is a political one.[1]
