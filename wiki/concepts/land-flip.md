# Land Flip

A land flip is the fraud technique described by Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] as central to the mechanics of the [[savings-and-loan-crisis|savings and loan crisis]].[1]

In a land flip, a person buys a parcel of land and sells it to a friend at a much higher price; that friend sells the same parcel to another associate at a higher price still, and the cycle repeats several times — often on the same day.[1] The artificially inflated valuation is then used as collateral to obtain a far larger loan from a savings and loan that the borrowers never intend to repay.[1] The scheme simultaneously launders money through successive purchases and strips depositors' funds out of the thrift.[1] None of this, Lange notes, caught the eyes of government regulators.[1]

The pattern was documented in Texas litigation in which a federal judge awarded a Dallas thrift more than $464 million in damages from civil fraud and racketeering suits against borrowers from an associate institution.[1] The suits charged that Richard Dover and John Ballis illegally appropriated loans from Western Savings Association with the help of its owner Jarrett Woods, and the Sunbelt lawsuit accused Dover and attorney [[john-riddle|John Riddle]] of completing a series of real estate transactions known as land flips that raised property values and created illusory profits for Western Savings.[1] Ballis was accused of borrowing more than $55 million from Western Savings to acquire raw land for commercial and residential developments.[1]
