# Trump v. Barbara

Trump v. Barbara is the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] case in which the Court heard oral arguments in early April 2026 on a legal challenge to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order seeking to limit birthright citizenship. The Badlands Brief of April 2, 2026 covers the argument as a potentially "transformational review" of American citizenship law.

## The executive order at issue

The challenged executive order interprets the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] to the United States Constitution as guaranteeing citizenship only to persons born in the United States and "subject to the jurisdiction thereof." Lower federal courts blocked the order and related efforts, finding that the Constitution's text and long-standing precedent protect birthright citizenship regardless of the parents' immigration status.

## Oral argument

Oral arguments were heard on April 1, 2026 — exactly 156 years after the April 1, 1870 ratification of the [[fifteenth-amendment|Fifteenth Amendment]], a coincidence Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] highlighted alongside the Union victory at the Battle of Five Forks on April 1, 1865. The Court's audio and transcript for the argument are identified in the source by docket number 25-365.

Associate Justice [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]] pressed the government's theory in a widely quoted exchange, asking whether a boy born in the United States to an Iranian father who entered the country illegally — automatically an Iranian national at birth with a duty of military service to the Iranian government — is "subject to a foreign power." He extended the point to children born to Russian, Mexican, and other foreign-national parents, concluding, "It sure seems like that's a — that makes them subject to a foreign power."

Associate Justice [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]], in an exchange Badlands commentator Chris Paul called "a rather shocking admission on the part of the Court," argued that language used in Supreme Court opinions was written not to be taken seriously as legal opinion but "to help the public accept the outcome of this case" — performing public relations on behalf of the Court. Ashe in America and Chris Paul read the remark as support for the position that legal precedent can amount to "nothing more than public relations."

## Legal stakes

The key precedent is [[united-states-v-wong-kim-ark|United States v. Wong Kim Ark]], in which the Court held that children born in the United States to non-citizen parents are citizens under the Fourteenth Amendment. Legal analysts cited in the coverage indicated that overturning or narrowing that precedent would require the Court to reinterpret more than a century of constitutional law. The Brief notes that the current Court's conservative majority has shown a willingness to revisit precedent in other areas, while immigration and constitutional-text cases have produced mixed outcomes.
