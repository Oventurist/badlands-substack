# Absurdity Doctrine

The Absurdity Doctrine is a canon of legal interpretation that was repeatedly invoked by justices and attorneys during the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] appeal in [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]] in December 2023, when the court considered whether Section 3 of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] applies to the Office of the President.[1]

## Definition

As described by [[john-manning|John Manning]] of Harvard Law, the doctrine recognizes that, despite the best efforts of legislators to make the law make sense, the law can sometimes be absurd: there may be unintended consequences, or society may evolve such that applying a law produces unexpected or unfair outcomes.[1] The doctrine gives the legal system a way to address unforeseen issues without undermining the overall authority of the legislation — likened to editing a contract to fix unintended consequences rather than voiding the entire agreement.[1]

## Criticism in the Trump ballot context

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that in a zero-trust environment, where weaponized justice is the norm, this level of judicial subjectivity is dangerous, even while conceding that the doctrine's name was fitting for the case at hand.[1] In her framing, the effort to disqualify [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the ballot because his opponents could not fairly defeat him politically amounted to election interference, and the courts' willingness to entertain the case was itself absurd.[1]

The doctrine also surfaced in the trial court. Judge [[sarah-wallace|Sarah Wallace]] disagreed with Trump's contention that she was required to consider only the "objective meaning" of his language — the absurdity clause argument — and found that "objectivity" is not a required element of the [[brandenburg-test|Brandenburg test]] for incitement.[1]
