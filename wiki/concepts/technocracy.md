# Technocracy

Technocracy is a proposed system of social and economic organization in which scientists, engineers and technical experts, rather than elected politicians or market actors, direct the production and distribution of goods and services. In Badlands Media's treatment, technocracy is not merely a historical curiosity but the operating template of the contemporary ruling class — a project to fuse technology and data collection into a permanent apparatus of governance and control.[1]

## Origins

The movement predates the modern globalist institutions usually blamed for it. In the depths of the Great Depression, the newspaper mogul [[william-randolph-hearst|Randolph Hearst]] — proprietor of Hearst Communications and its thirty major metropolitan newspapers — was searching for a way to rescue a media empire damaged by economic collapse and by his own reputation for sensationalized stories, faked interviews and doctored photographs. That style of reporting, the article notes, distorted real events for readers who could neither recognize fake news nor particularly care, leaving the public detached from reality.[1]

In the same period, [[nicholas-murray-butler|Nicholas Murray Butler]], president of [[columbia-university|Columbia University]], was publicly endorsing a new economic system being designed by scientists and engineers and marketed as a cure for America's economic woes — a replacement for capitalism and free markets. The system was called "Technocracy," and it caught Hearst's attention. Hearst saw commercial opportunity in the growing public curiosity and published a series of articles exploring the idea, generating widespread debate among intellectuals and the general public.[1]

A contemporary definition from *The Technocrat Magazine* in 1930 described technocracy as "the science of social engineering, the scientific operation of the entire social mechanism to produce and distribute goods and services to the entire population."[1]

## The technetronic era

The concept was later carried forward by [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]], characterized in the article as a Deep State politician, whose book *Between Two Ages: America's Role in the Technetronic Era* developed the notion of a "technetronic society" in which technology and control over it shape social and political dynamics. Badlands argues that Brzezinski did not invent technocracy but built upon an idea that had already captivated the ruling class for half a century, and that his legacy is carried on today by figures such as [[bill-gates|Bill Gates]], [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]] and [[yuval-noah-harari|Yuval Noah Harari]].[1]

## Contemporary form

Technocracy's modern expression, according to the article, runs through the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] and the [[great-reset|"great reset"]] agenda — the promotion of digital currencies, artificial intelligence and pervasive data integration to "revolutionize" industries, governance and individual lives. Over the last several decades, advancing technology has made it dramatically easier for governments and corporations to monitor and track individuals both online and offline, raising concerns about the erosion of privacy and the abuse of power.[1]

The article identifies [[digital-identification|digital identification]] as the lynchpin that makes the whole system function: once populations are coerced onto a digital control system, each subsequent advancement arrives regardless of public sentiment until people are desensitized to the encroachment. The author compares this to the endless stream of smartphone software updates, except that what is being updated is society itself and the individual's ability to interact with it.[1]

Concrete manifestations catalogued in the piece include the [[maisha-namba|Maisha Namba]] digital ID program in Kenya, [[smart-epants|SMART ePANTS]] and other [[smart-clothing|smart clothing]] initiatives, and the privacy failures of [[smart-homes|smart homes]].[1]

## Assessment

The article concludes that "forced technocracy is the trajectory," and that families and communities cannot prepare for it by ignoring it. Framing the situation as worldwide [[fifth-generation-warfare|fifth-generational warfare]], the author argues that no war is won by turning a blind eye to an enemy's technological advances, and expresses the belief that technocracy is ultimately doomed to fail — though whether it takes the world down with it remains to be seen.[1]
