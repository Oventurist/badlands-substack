# Coomer v. Lindell

Coomer v. Lindell is the defamation trial brought by former Dominion Voting Systems executive [[eric-coomer|Eric Coomer]] against [[mike-lindell|Mike Lindell]], [[mypillow|MyPillow]] and FrankSpeech in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado, which entered its second week in June 2025.[1] The case is one of numerous defamation suits Coomer has pursued over the claim that he took part in a September 2020 call with Antifa-aligned activists and boasted of rigging the presidential election.[1]

## Course of the trial

Jurors in Denver heard testimony from [[joe-oltmann|Joe Oltmann]], the podcaster who originated the allegation, over two days in the trial's first week.[1] The examination — conducted in part by attorney Brad Kloewer and punctuated by frequent bench conferences called by Judge Nina Y. Wang — produced admissions that Oltmann had misled a judge about his availability for questioning and refused to identify those who facilitated his alleged discovery of election manipulation.[1] Courtroom observers described the jury as unusually attentive during his testimony, leaning in and taking notes.[1]

On the Friday of that week, new evidence was introduced showing that a local activist/BLM/Antifa Zoom call had taken place in September 2020, complicating the narrative previously presented to the jury that no such call ever occurred.[1] The affidavit corroborated several points in Oltmann's hand-written notes while continuing to dispute his account of Coomer's participation.[1]

The following Monday's session was expected to feature testimony from Lindell himself, from his former employee Brannon Howse, and from Dennis Montgomery via video deposition clips addressing PCAPs, China, and Hammer and Scorecard.[1] The Clerk of the Court reopened the overflow room in anticipation of heavy public interest.[1] Court order placed trial updates from the courtroom on delay.[1]

### Lindell's testimony

Taking the stand in June 2025, Lindell testified that he had called "50 to 100 people traitors," a moment captured in courtroom sketches broadcast by Denver station 9News.[2] The Wednesday session proceeded in fits and starts with frequent breaks as the defense proffered video clips drawn from *Absolute Proof* and *Kill Chain*; attorneys argued the clips at the end of the day and the court said it would rule on them individually the following morning.[2] Lindell was expected to take the stand for a third consecutive day.[2]

Testimony was still awaited from J. Alex Halderman, [[brannon-howse|Brannon Howse]] and [[dennis-montgomery|Dennis Montgomery]], and a charge conference to define the jury instructions took place on the Tuesday evening.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], attending in person, published rolled-up daily threads covering the first seven days of trial and mirrored her coverage on Truth Social for readers who avoid X.[2]

### Close of evidence

Plaintiff Eric Coomer rested his case on Thursday, June 12, 2025, and the defense came close to resting the same day.[4] At the close of proceedings, attorneys for Lindell asked for the opportunity to call one additional witness before the case went to the jury, and the judge asked for the proffer the following morning.[4] Jury instructions were expected to be read as early as 9 a.m. Mountain Time on Friday, followed by closing arguments; if the jury did not reach a verdict by close of business Friday, deliberations would resume Monday.[4] The jury heard testimony from Professor Halderman — who consumed most of the day — and from Peter Kent on Wednesday.[4]

In an interview that Thursday, [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]] confronted Lindell about reportedly "getting blown out" at the trial, telling him that mainstream media coverage was "universally horrible" and that reporters said "the jury will not be out long to rule against you."[4] Lindell replied that "If we lose this, Steve, I really believe it's how, you know, anyone could get sued at any time with talking about anything that they decide that we don't want to hear from you," and said he hoped God and prayers would produce a favorable jury outcome.[4] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] said she had withheld her prediction on the outcome until the case was with the jury and would deliver it on [[badlands-daily|Badlands Daily]].[4]

## Significance

Because the jury serves as the finder of fact, the contested existence and content of the "Antifa call" is central to the liability determination.[1] Badlands correspondent [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], covering the trial in person, noted the jury possessed none of the accumulated context available to long-time observers of the story and was working only from what was presented in the courtroom.[1] Separately, Lindell was served in the Colorado courtroom on Friday in connection with his long-running litigation with FedEx.[1]

## Motion to seal exhibits

In early June 2025, attorneys for [[eric-coomer|Eric Coomer]] filed a motion for a protective order over certain exhibits, asking the court to restrict public access to the courtroom while the exhibits were displayed or discussed, to seal the corresponding portions of the trial transcript, and to require that both the exhibits and the sealed transcript portions be filed under seal when attached to post-trial motions.[3] The itemized exhibits included Coomer's employee file, his resignation and separation agreement, and two exhibits originating with the plaintiff's therapist.[3]

USAF (Ret.) Colonel Shawn Smith posted on X that the motive had "less to do with this trial, and more to do with the effect on his other pending cases, and the potential *jurors* in those cases," accompanying the post with Coomer's consolidated list of defamation cases.[3] Badlands contributor [[canncon|CannCon]] responded "Democracy Dies in Darkness," saying he understood the therapist material being privileged but not the employee file and resignation letter — in which he expressed particular interest — and certainly not the settlement demands or the Newsmax agreement.[3]

## Day 6: Lindell takes the stand

On Monday, June 9, 2025 — day six of the trial — MyPillow CEO [[mike-lindell|Mike Lindell]] took the stand and remained committed to his campaign against voting machines and his claim that the 2020 presidential election was stolen.[5] He continued attacking [[eric-coomer|Eric Coomer]], the former [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]] executive, while seeking to distance himself from claims that he specifically coordinated attacks against Coomer at a 2021 convention he organized about the theory.[5] Lindell characterized Coomer as motivated by money and political grievance, saying: "(I) cost Dr. Coomer what? He's out there suing people for money."[5] Coomer had filed suit in April 2022, accusing Lindell of defaming him in a series of statements and media appearances that caused emotional and physical distress and cost him his career in election security.[5]

Coomer's attorneys used winding and often contentious questioning to tie Lindell's election fraud claims, broadcast on [[frankspeech|FrankSpeech]], to [[mypillow|MyPillow]] promo codes underwriting the effort — necessary because the plaintiffs must show Lindell, FrankSpeech and MyPillow are all liable for the alleged harm.[5] Video clips from FrankSpeech, RSBN and NewsMax were played for the jury, along with a full-length CNN clip from Anderson Cooper's program in which a field reporter interviewed Lindell.[5] Badlands trial correspondent [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that Lindell admitted watching the clip when it aired, making it relevant and admissible, but considered it the first piece of evidence in the trial that felt unduly prejudicial because it showcased openly biased reporting, including terms like "safe and secure" and "debunked."[5]

## Legal standard and the public-figure question

Ashe in America explained that for defamation purposes a person becomes a public figure when their involvement in public affairs subjects them to increased public scrutiny; as Vice President of Product Strategy and Security for a major US voting vendor during the 2020 election, and given his own heavily biased public statements, Coomer qualified as a public figure in the context of the case.[5] The legal standard requires a plaintiff to prove that a false statement of fact was published to a third party, that it harmed their reputation, and that it was made negligently or with actual malice depending on the plaintiff's status.[5] Public figures must prove "actual malice" — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth — and because Lindell's testimony made clear he believes what he said, Coomer's angle was likely reckless disregard, which is why his team spent most of the day asking Lindell about all the ways he should have known his statements were false.[5]

Late in the day Coomer's attorney passed the witness and Lindell's attorneys took over, focusing on his modest upbringing, business history and unexpected success.[5] Questioning was expected to continue at 9 a.m. MT Tuesday, with the charge conference — where jury instructions are finalized, setting the guardrails for deliberations — scheduled for Tuesday afternoon.[5]

## Closing arguments and jury deliberations

Attorneys for both sides delivered closing arguments on Friday, June 13, 2025, capping the two-week trial and putting the decision in the hands of the jury.[6] [[eric-coomer|Eric Coomer]], the former director of product strategy and security for Denver-based [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]], sought $62.7 million in damages from [[mike-lindell|Lindell]] for what the lawsuit described as targeting "a private individual with false allegations of criminal conduct on a scale unprecedented in American history" — specifically the claim that Coomer helped steal the 2020 election for Joe Biden.[6] The suit argued that Lindell used his public platforms, [[mypillow|MyPillow]] and the website FranksSpeech, to profit from and spread those false claims.[6]

The jury did not reach a verdict after deliberating for a few hours on Friday and was set to return Monday at 7 a.m. Mountain Time.[6] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] explained that the jury had to determine whether Lindell had acted with "reckless disregard for the truth," the route by which the plaintiffs sought to establish malice — a requirement given that the judge had already found the statements to be "defamation per se."[6] Central to Lindell's defense against recklessness was the argument that he had a reasonable basis for his beliefs and that the "expert class" had blocked him from accessing the resources needed to test them, a theme discussed on that Saturday's Devolution Power Hour, where she said [[j-alex-halderman|Halderman]] "essentially confirmed that he is a blocker."[6]

## Opening statements and the belief defense

In the trial's opening phase, defense attorney [[chris-kachouroff|Chris Kachouroff]] told the jury that it was not necessary to prove [[mike-lindell|Lindell]]'s voting-machine claims were true, saying: "This trial is about whether Mike Lindell believed his statements were true at the time he made them. It doesn't have to be true, but he has to believe at the time he made them that they were substantially true."[7] KUSA anchor Kyle Clark summarised the position for viewers as an argument that it did not matter whether what Lindell said was true, because he believed it to be true.[7] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that reporting framing this as a retreat was misinformation, since the same affirmative defenses appeared in the April pretrial order, the difference being clinical brief versus narrative opening statement.[7]

## Media order and contempt warning

On Tuesday, June 3, 2025, the court declined to hold Lindell in contempt but issued him a warning, and Lindell agreed not to discuss witness testimony with the press during the trial.[7] Members of Lindell's team and others characterised this as "gagging" and "censoring," but Ashe in America reported that there had been no change to the court's media order since the start of trial, that there was no gag order or media blackout, and that the dispute concerned Lindell tweeting during the trial and discussing testimony while it was happening.[7] She noted that the jury saw none of the controversy, which was handled before jurors entered and after they left, and that while one could argue the court was restricting Lindell's First Amendment rights, no one could claim censorship of anyone other than the defendant himself.[7]

## Cross-examination of Coomer and testimony of Matt Crane

The jury saw [[eric-coomer|Coomer]] cross-examined by defense attorneys and read his #UnTrumpMe Facebook posts; courtroom sources described the jury as mostly engaged, though two unrelated observers independently reported that one juror was dozing.[7] Coomer was followed on the stand by [[matt-crane|Matt Crane]], a friend of Coomer's who appeared in a photograph from a barbecue at Coomer's house alongside state and local election officials, including Hillary Rudy, who works for Colorado Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]].[7] Crane's face also appeared on a promotional poster for the election vendor Global Mobile.[7]

According to sources in the courtroom, Crane admitted he was invested in the outcome of the case, struggled with questions about his wife's relationship with Coomer as colleagues at [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]], and asserted that the risk-limiting audit was a security measure ensuring voters could trust the 2020 elections while not knowing that only four states used risk-limiting audits in 2020.[7] "He looked like a fool in front of the jury," one witness to the cross-examination said.[7]

[[joe-oltmann|Joe Oltmann]] — whose 2020 statements comprise the central defamation claims in the case — was expected to be called by Coomer the following day, with his attorney entering an appearance in the case on Wednesday evening.[7]

## Lindell's pre-trial posture (June 2025)

Ahead of the trial's start in early June 2025 at the federal courthouse in Denver, [[mike-lindell|Lindell]] told Rolling Stone he intended to take the stand himself: "Of course I'm gonna testify at my own trial! … I have nothing to hide. I am a former crack addict, I've always been open about that … So I have nothing to hide at this trial."[8] The magazine characterised the [[mypillow|MyPillow]] chief executive as one of the loudest promoters of the claim that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] won the 2020 election and of theories that voting machines were rigged against him.[8]

## Badlands analysis of the defense case

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that because truth is an absolute defense against defamation, Lindell should be able to admit evidence that his statements were accurate, and that despite the judge's rulings on motions in limine restricting some of [[eric-coomer|Coomer]]'s writings and habits from evidence, the case was winnable.[8] She noted that the executive in charge of strategy and security at the voting machine company had been openly hostile toward a presidential candidate on social media before administering an election credibly alleged to have been rigged, and pointed to emails surfaced in another case in which Coomer wrote that "our products suck" while describing installation problems and unmitigated vulnerabilities.[8]

She further stressed that the central claim — that Coomer was on an Antifa call — was Lindell repeating [[joe-oltmann|Joe Oltmann]]'s reporting rather than making an original assertion, contending that "this isn't defamation" and that "this case never should have gotten this far."[8] She previewed the trial in the [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]] program alongside [[canncon|CannCon]] and Neil Johnson, and said she would be in the courtroom posting updates.[8]
