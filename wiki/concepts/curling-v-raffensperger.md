# Curling v. Raffensperger

Curling v. Raffensperger is federal litigation in the Northern District of Georgia challenging the security and reliability of the state's electronic voting system.[1] The case was originally brought by Democratic-aligned plaintiffs in the wake of Donald Trump's 2016 victory, and took years to reach trial.[1]

## Subject matter

The suit centers on Georgia's voting equipment, in particular the [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]] ImageCast X ballot-marking devices analyzed in the [[halderman-report|Halderman Report]] commissioned in the case and unsealed in June 2023 by the court presided over by Judge [[amy-totenberg|Amy Totenberg]].[1]

## Raffensperger's refusal to testify

In December 2023 it was reported that Georgia Secretary of State [[brad-raffensperger|Brad Raffensperger]] would refuse to testify before the court in January 2024 regarding the state's voting machines.[1] Badlands Media writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the refusal compounded Raffensperger's decision to defer Dominion's security patches until after the 2024 election, writing that "nothing says 'trust our election system' like a nontechnical politician unilaterally deciding to keep vulnerabilities in place — and then refusing to testify about it."[1]

## Significance

Commentators expected the case, proceeding to trial in a presidential election year, to "shine a massive spotlight" on an election system that American officials describe as "the gold standard" and the "safest and most secure" in history.[1] Observers noted it was unclear how Curling could be tried without directly addressing the machines themselves.[1]

## Bench trial granted (November 2023)

In November 2023, Judge [[amy-totenberg|Amy Totenberg]] granted a bench trial in the case, rejecting the state's request that she rule on the arguments and facts without trial, and set proceedings for January 2024.[2] A footnote in her ruling said the evidence "does not suggest that the Plaintiffs are conspiracy theorists of any variety" and credited leading cybersecurity experts and computer scientists with showing that Georgia's voting system threatens the constitutional rights of voters.[2] Totenberg also urged the Georgia legislature to address the "varied challenges" with the existing system.[2]

[[mike-lindell|Mike Lindell]] hailed the ruling on [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]]'s *War Room*, predicting it would "expose everything."[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the litigation had been spinning through the courts since 2017, that the ruling followed a Georgia coordinated election plagued by county-level issues, and that the case offered another opportunity for election-fraud evidence to be heard in court.[2]

## Dismissal (March 2025)

U.S. District Judge [[amy-totenberg|Amy Totenberg]] dismissed the case in a ruling issued Monday, March 31, 2025, even while maintaining substantial concerns about the security of Georgia's electronic voting system.[3] Totenberg held that the plaintiffs — the Coalition for Good Governance and a group of Georgia voters — lacked standing because they could not demonstrate that the electronic voting system disenfranchises them from exercising their right to vote, and she characterized some of their objections to the touchscreen ballot marking devices as policy disagreements rather than constitutional violations.[3] The dismissal came a day after Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] directed the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] to drop a separate Biden-era challenge to [[georgia-senate-bill-202|Georgia Senate Bill 202]].[3]

## Origins and the unsealing of the Halderman Report

The suit was filed in 2017, during [[brian-kemp|Brian Kemp]]'s tenure as Georgia Secretary of State, over concerns with the [[diebold-election-systems|Diebold]] paperless touchscreen voting machines then in use.[4] In 2020, Judge [[amy-totenberg|Amy Totenberg]] ordered Georgia to replace those machines, and the state adopted the [[dominion-imagecast-x|Dominion ICX]] [[ballot-marking-device|ballot marking devices]] against the advice of election security experts, including the lone cybersecurity expert on the governor's commission to recommend a new voting system.[4] The [[halderman-report|Halderman Report]] prepared for the case by [[j-alex-halderman|Dr. J. Alex Halderman]] and [[drew-springall|Prof. Drew Springall]] was unsealed in June 2023, almost two years after submission.[4] Also in the litigation, Halderman testified regarding an October 2020 order permitting a "de minimis" software change to fix a ballot display problem, criticizing [[pro-v-and-v|Pro V&V]] for having performed "only cursory testing" of the new software.[4]

## Trial date and state posture (November 2023)

In mid-November 2023 it was reported that Judge [[amy-totenberg|Amy Totenberg]] had scheduled the trial to begin on January 9, 2024, in a case originally brought by Democrats in 2017 over the state's use of voting technologies.[5] Totenberg wrote that "the Court cannot wave a magic wand in this case to address the varied challenges to our democracy and election system in recent years," while suggesting that "reasonable, timely discussion and compromise in this case, coupled with prompt, informed legislative action, might certainly make a difference that benefits the parties and the public."[5]

Georgia officials remained resistant. Deputy Secretary of State [[jordan-fuchs|Jordan Fuchs]] said the plaintiffs had never offered a security solution that did not involve hand-marked paper ballots, adding, "We don't negotiate with election deniers. If they have an idea that wouldn't take Georgia back to the days of hanging chads and stuffed ballot boxes, they should offer it."[5] Critics noted that hanging chads were the 2000 Bush v. Gore controversy that produced the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]] and, with it, the multi-billion-dollar election industry and black-box voting equipment.[5] Findings by [[j-alex-halderman|J. Alex Halderman]], released in June 2023 after being sealed since July 2021, remained unpatched ahead of the 2024 election, and Halderman has stated that patching would not repair the vulnerabilities.[5] The devices at issue are used statewide, including in [[fulton-county|Fulton]] and [[spalding-county|Spalding]] counties, where the [[2023-georgia-municipal-election-discrepancies|2023 municipal election discrepancies]] were reported.[5]
