# Online Streaming Act (Bill C-11)

The Online Streaming Act, known as Bill C-11, is Canadian legislation identified in Badlands Media coverage as one of the principal instruments in the multinational regulatory attack on online speech.[1]

## Placement among censorship instruments

Ahead of the 2024 U.S. election, Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] published a piece for Badlands Media outlining a multinational offensive against free speech and cataloguing the tools available to censors.[1] That inventory comprised Canada's C-11, the United Kingdom's [[online-safety-bill|Online Safety Bill]], and the European Union's [[digital-services-act|Digital Services Act]].[1]

## Relative weight

In the April 2025 assessment, the article concluded that of these instruments the Digital Services Act was the one with "the most teeth," and the vehicle in which recently dismissed disinformation practitioners had placed their hope — attributing this to Europe having been the true progenitor of the censorship apparatus in the first place.[1] C-11 is accordingly presented as part of a coordinated Western regulatory pattern rather than an isolated national measure.[1]

## Scope and criticism

Bill C-11 amends Canada's Broadcasting Act to bring online streaming platforms such as Netflix, YouTube and Spotify under the regulatory purview of the [[canadian-radio-television-and-telecommunications-commission|Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission]] (CRTC); it was introduced in 2022 by the Liberal government under [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]] and is now enacted.[2] Critics contend that it grants the CRTC excessive power to dictate which content is prioritized online, and that the obligation on streaming and social media services to promote "Canadian content" (CanCon) is government overreach capable of limiting user choice and suppressing diverse voices.[2]

Principal objections concern unclear definitions — "Canadian content," "online undertaking" — and the CRTC's broad discretion, which invites inconsistent or excessive regulation and leaves platforms and creators uncertain.[2] The commission's process for defining CanCon opened with consultations in May 2023 and was due to produce a draft in mid-2025, leaving open whether a creator must be Canadian-born or whether filming in [[canada|Canada]] suffices, a vagueness contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued can be weaponized.[2] He characterized C-11 as a regulatory mess designed behind window dressing and jargon as a pretense for state-sponsored censorship, and grouped it with the UK's [[online-safety-act-2023|Online Safety Act]] and the EU's [[digital-services-act|Digital Services Act]] as frameworks passed over widespread opposition — top-down impositions rather than democratic consensus.[2] See [[censorship-war|Censorship War]].[2]

## Role in the multinational censorship campaign (DeLarme, 2024)

Canada's C-11, the Online Streaming Act, was identified by Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] as one of the three principal legislative prongs of the multinational offensive against online speech, together with the UK's [[online-safety-bill|Online Safety Bill]] and the European Union's [[digital-services-act|Digital Services Act]].[3] DeLarme first catalogued these frameworks in his article "The War on Dissent: Western Governments are Creating Legal Frameworks to Attack Alternative Media," arguing that they represent coordinated action by governments and NGOs rather than isolated national policy.[3]
