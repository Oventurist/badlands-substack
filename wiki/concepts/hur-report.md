# Hur Report

The Hur Report is the February 2024 special counsel report by [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]] on President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s handling of classified documents, which declined to bring charges but drew far more attention for its characterization of Biden's memory than for its legal conclusions.[1]

## Release and immediate reaction

The report was released on Thursday, February 8, 2024, and concluded that Biden had carelessly kept classified documents and notebooks at his home but that the evidence was not strong enough to charge the president with crimes.[3] Running 345 pages, it portrayed the 81-year-old Biden as someone who haphazardly stored notebooks and classified material at home and struggled to recall key dates in his life, and its description of him as "an elderly man with a poor memory" prompted a furious response at a hastily called news conference hours later.[3] Republicans seized on the characterization to attack the Democratic incumbent as unfit for office.[3]

At the [[white-house|White House]] that evening, Biden demanded of reporters, "How in the hell dare he raise that," referring to the finding that he could not remember the year his son Beau died of cancer, and added, "It wasn't any of their damn business. … I don't need anyone to remind me when he passed away."[3] In an exchange that ranged from possible national security crimes to his mental faculties to the US response to the war in the Middle East, Biden insisted he had never improperly shared classified information and was fit both to serve and to seek reelection.[3]

## Badlands interpretation

[[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] read the episode through the lens of a two-tiered justice system, contrasting [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] being "drowned in fake charges as a form of election interference" with Biden slipping away from prosecution "into a haze of Alzheimer's, in full public view, in real time."[3] Esler argued that little about Biden's decline was actually being hidden and that the salient question was why the narrative had shifted — why the [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] and legacy media had suddenly turned on Biden, what new narrative was being constructed, and to whom the [[deep-state|Deep State]] was reacting.[3]

Invoking Carl von Clausewitz on friction and framing the conflict as a struggle between two hostile, irreconcilable wills, Esler suggested that [[fifth-generation-warfare|fifth generation warfare]] keeps most of the struggle covert but that the underlying reality of war clarifies it.[3] He argued that if "they never thought she would lose," the entire Biden presidency must be reactionary rather than part of an uninterrupted plan, with Trump as the interruption, and noted that "Panic in DC" was trending on Twitter that night.[3] [[just-human|Just Human]] similarly judged the report "highly damaging to Biden," revealing severe memory issues and evidence of substantial crimes related to classified documents.[3]

## Findings on memory

Hur concluded that Biden's memory was so poor that he "did not remember when he was vice president, forgetting on the first day of the interview when his term ended, and forgetting on the second day of the interview when his term began," and further that Biden "did not remember, even within several years, when his son Beau died."[1] These passages reignited the national controversy over the president's cognitive fitness.[1]

## White House response

Biden gave what Badlands described as a bizarre press conference about the findings during the [[tucker-carlson-putin-interview|Tucker Carlson interview with Vladimir Putin]], in which he identified the Egyptian president as being from Mexico and shouted that questions about his mental state were "not the opinion of the press" — in response to questions from the press.[1]

The following day, the first formal [[white-house|White House]] briefing on the matter was characterized as a spin cycle. Ian Sams, spokesman for the White House Counsel's Office, insisted the matter was resolved: "The special counsel's assignment when he was appointed was to determine whether any criminal conduct occurred. He found it didn't. That was the finding. The case is closed."[1] On the cognitive findings, the White House called the "gratuitous comments…inappropriate," saying they should not distract from the case being closed.[1] [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] characterized the memory findings as politically motivated.[1]

Badlands commentary noted the contradiction: the administration praised the portions of the report it liked while dismissing the memory findings as gratuitous and unreal, and concluded that "they can't have it both ways" and that the matter was "most definitely not closed."[1]

## Political consequences

The report triggered a wave of elite commentary on whether Democrats would replace Biden before the [[2024-democratic-national-convention|July convention]], including [[nigel-farage|Nigel Farage]]'s prediction that the party would ditch him, and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s concession that Biden's age was a "legitimate issue."[1] Badlands writers linked the episode to President Trump's earlier remark that the [[twenty-fifth-amendment|25th Amendment]] would be a problem for Biden.[1]

## 2024 FOIA fight over the interview tapes

In July 2024 the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] opposed the release of audiotapes of special counsel [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]]'s interview with President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], telling a federal judge that the recordings had been "properly withheld" and that a FOIA suit by two conservative groups and a coalition of mainstream media organizations should be rejected.[2] DOJ cited the risk of "deepfakes" and "privacy harms" to an uncharged individual and argued that forced disclosure "would create a perverse incentive" against executive branch cooperation with Congress.[2] It maintained there was no "meaningful countervailing public benefit" because a transcript had been released and Hur had testified publicly, while conceding that the tapes carried "new information not contained in the written transcript" in the form of the "sound, tone, and pacing" of Biden's answers.[2]

## Fallout: cognitive testing debate

In the week after the report's release, speculation about President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s mental acuity reached what the New York Post called a fever pitch, driven by a string of public lapses and the report's suggestion that Biden had "diminished faculties" and was a "well-meaning elderly man with a poor memory."[4] The controversy prompted calls for Biden, then 81, and former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], then 77, to take the [[montreal-cognitive-assessment|Montreal Cognitive Assessment]], whose inventor [[ziad-nasreddine|Ziad Nasreddine]] said it would be "a good thing" to assess cognition whenever suspicion arose.[4]

## Polling and narrative fallout (February 2024)

Days after the report's release, an ABC News/Ipsos poll conducted on the Ipsos Knowledge Panel found that 86% of Americans thought [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], then 81, was too old to serve another term as president — including 59% who thought both he and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] were too old and 27% who thought only Biden was.[5] Sixty-two percent said Trump, then 77, was too old; 91% of independents said Biden was too old and 71% said the same of Trump.[5] Those figures were up sharply from a September ABC News/Washington Post poll in which 74% said Biden was too old and 49% said the same of Trump.[5]

The findings intensified scrutiny of Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]], 59, whose readiness to step into the presidency moved from quiet talk into open debate; Democratic strategist [[jennifer-palmieri|Jennifer Palmieri]] said the special counsel's report "sort of accelerated that moment."[5]

Badlands writer [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] argued that the establishment's narrow "age" framing was composed of omissions as much as misinformation: it danced around Biden's cognitive struggles and obscured the implications of a human puppet occupying the Office of the President, acknowledging just enough incapacity to justify removing him while avoiding the darker reality of a takeover of the presidency — a reading he tied to [[devolution|Devolution]].[5] He further warned that removing Biden for being "an old man" could be mirrored and projected onto Trump, and read Trump's cultivated public closeness with [[vivek-ramaswamy|Vivek Ramaswamy]] as a counter-move injecting youthful leadership into his campaign.[5] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the same polling as a "take the win" moment, noting the establishment's own polls showed little public mandate surviving its destruction of its own figurehead.[5]

## Audio release (May 2025)

The [[wall-street-journal|Wall Street Journal]] obtained and published the underlying audio of the October 2023 [[robert-hur|Hur]] interviews in May 2025, more than a year after the Biden administration released transcripts in March 2024 but refused to make the recordings public.[6] The tapes captured [[joe-biden|Biden]] speaking at times with a frail voice, digressing at length, mixing up his Senate tenure with his vice presidency when asked where he kept his papers in 2017 and 2018, and struggling to recall the year his son Beau died.[6] Their release coincided with new books on Biden's decline by [[jake-tapper|Jake Tapper]] and Alex Thompson and fed directly into Republican arguments about the [[autopen-scandal|autopen]].[6]
