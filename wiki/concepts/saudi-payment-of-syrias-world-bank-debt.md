# Saudi Payment of Syria's World Bank Debt

In April 2025, Reuters reported that [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] planned to pay off [[syria|Syria]]'s arrears to the [[world-bank|World Bank]], a step that could unlock international financing for the reconstruction of the war-torn country.[1]

## Background

Syria underwent a change of power in late 2024 when the jihadist group [[hayat-tahrir-al-sham|Hayat Tahrir al-Sham]] (HTS) launched a surprise offensive that took Damascus and ousted long-time President [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar Assad]].[1] HTS leader [[ahmed-al-sharaa|Ahmed al-Sharaa]] became president and formed a new transitional government in March 2025.[1] Al-Sharaa visited Saudi Arabia in February 2025 on his first official foreign trip since assuming office.[1]

## Mechanics

The World Bank is an international financial institution headquartered in Washington, but Syria's continued status under US sanctions makes international transactions difficult.[1] Approximately $15 million in arrears must be paid off before the institution can issue grants and other forms of assistance.[1] An official from the Saudi Finance Ministry declined to confirm to Reuters that Riyadh planned to settle the debt.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands commentary argued that Assad's departure from power made increasing sense in light of these developments, noting that Syria had been under heavy sanctions since 2004 with individuals targeted based on their support for Assad, yet the Syrian economy thrived until the rise of ISIS in 2011.[1] The commentary highlighted that the timing of sanctions coincided with Assad's announced intention to develop a large international rail network connecting Iran to Turkey, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia, with Aleppo serving as the main hub — a transportation network the commentary contends would have revolutionized the Middle East had it been built.[1]

Instead, the commentary argues, chaos was introduced into the region through the invasion of Iraq and the overthrow of Saddam Hussein, followed by the Arab Spring of 2011, which destabilized nearly every regional government and sparked a migrant crisis still reverberating worldwide.[1] With Assad gone, the sanctions were expected in theory to be dropped despite his replacement by what the commentary described as radical Islamic terrorists ruling Syria; if sanctions are lifted and the debt repaid by the Saudi royal family, construction contracts could be awarded, insured, and funded by loans, assuming the region is not plunged back into warfare.[1] The commentary closed by posing the question of whether the outcome would be a Golden Age or more forever wars.[1]
