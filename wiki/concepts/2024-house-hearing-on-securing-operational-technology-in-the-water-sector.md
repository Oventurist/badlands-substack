# 2024 House Hearing on Securing Operational Technology in the Water Sector

On Tuesday, February 6, 2024, the [[house-committee-on-homeland-security|House Committee on Homeland Security]]'s Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Protection Subcommittee held a roughly 90-minute hearing entitled "Securing Operational Technology: A Deep Dive into the Water Sector."[1] It was one of more than half a dozen House hearings running largely simultaneously that day.[1] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], a former management consultant specializing in large-scale technology transformations, covered it on the grounds that it concerned the water supply and the cyber infrastructure governing it.[1]

## Participants

The subcommittee was chaired by Representative [[andrew-garbarino|Andrew Garbarino]] (R-NY), with ranking member Representative [[eric-swalwell|Eric Swalwell]] (D-CA), four additional Republicans, and three additional Democrats.[1] Members who questioned witnesses included [[rob-menendez|Rob Menendez]] (D-NJ), [[august-pfluger|August Pfluger]] (R-TX), [[carlos-gimenez|Carlos Gimenez]] (R-FL), and [[troy-carter|Troy Carter]] (D-LA).[1]

Four witnesses testified: [[robert-m-lee|Robert M. Lee]] of [[dragos|Dragos, Inc.]]; [[charles-clancy|Charles Clancy]] of the [[mitre-corporation|MITRE Corporation]]; [[kevin-morley|Kevin Morley]] of the [[american-water-works-association|American Water Works Association]]; and [[marty-edwards|Marty Edwards]] of [[tenable|Tenable]].[1] The Badlands account treated the composition of that panel as an illustration of [[public-private-partnership|public-private partnership]] governance.[1]

## Subject matter

Garbarino opened by citing the late-2023 cyber activity against [[operational-technology|operational technology]] devices across multiple sectors, including water and wastewater systems, by Iranian-affiliated actors — a reference to the November 2023 intrusion at the [[aliquippa-municipal-authority|Aliquippa Municipal Authority]] in Pennsylvania.[1] Witnesses repeatedly drew parallels to the energy sector and to [[critical-infrastructure|critical infrastructure]] interdependency, and criticized the government's sanctions-centered response as failing to scale to the strategic threat.[1]

Asked about their nightmare scenarios, the witnesses named denial of drinking water or manipulation of chemical levels at scale, cascading multi-sector failures, and mass ransomware against the shared population of programmable logic controllers deployed across water, electrical, and manufacturing facilities.[1]

The most-quoted exchanges concerned whether the country could retreat from digitization. Gimenez pressed for closed systems in light of [[quantum-computing|quantum computing]] and [[artificial-intelligence|artificial intelligence]] threats; Lee replied that manual or intranet-only operation is no longer possible and that hardening should focus on selected strategic sites, since scaling across 50,000-plus water companies is unaffordable.[1] Morley separately argued for direct resourcing of local communities and contractors, which Pfluger paraphrased as making the effort more local and regional.[1]

## Interpretation

The Badlands account concluded that the vulnerabilities described for water apply equally to energy, banking, communications, and elections, driven by shared foreign supply chains and by two decades of centralization; that "complexity breeds and hides corruption"; and that the [[cisa|Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency]]'s authority over all of it raises the question of whether these vulnerabilities are "a bug … or a feature."[1] It closed by noting, with evident suspicion, that local water authorities in Colorado had begun sending customers [[fema|FEMA]] notices on water-related disaster preparedness.[1]
