# FBI Raid on Hannah Natanson

The FBI raid on Hannah Natanson was the January 14, 2026 execution of a federal search warrant at the Virginia home of [[washington-post|Washington Post]] reporter [[hannah-natanson|Hannah Natanson]], carried out as part of an investigation into the unlawful retention and disclosure of classified national defense information.[1] Agents seized Natanson's phone, two laptops — one personal and one issued by the Post — and a smartwatch.[1]

## Underlying investigation

The search was tied to a federal case against a [[pentagon|Pentagon]] contractor identified in court documents as [[aurelio-perez-lugones|Aurelio Perez-Lugones]], who is accused of unlawfully retaining classified national defense information, including allegedly taking home and printing sensitive intelligence material.[1] Authorities said classified information appeared in communications between Natanson and the contractor.[1]

## Official justification

Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] said the raid was conducted at the Pentagon's request and stressed a zero-tolerance approach to leaks of classified information, stating that the individual suspected of the leak was "behind bars."[1] [[fbi|FBI]] Director [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] echoed the national security rationale on social media.[1]

## Reaction

Press freedom groups and journalists widely condemned the search as "highly unusual" and "aggressive," warning that it could chill investigative journalism and undermine [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] protections.[1] The Washington Post editorial board framed the raid as a serious threat to constitutional press protections while emphasizing the continued importance of robust reporting despite government pressure.[1]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that she did not like the action, placing First Amendment freedoms at the top of the rights she considers most important and describing herself as vehemently opposed to violating journalists' protections in the name of national security.[1] At the same time she invoked the oath of office's pledge to defend against "all enemies, foreign and domestic," arguing that the oath contains no press-credential exception and that "no one is above the law."[1] She drew a parallel to the [[tina-peters|Tina Peters]] appeal oral arguments, in which a judge rejected the state's contention that destroying election records was permissible so long as backups existed, summarizing both cases with the maxim "all means all."[1]
