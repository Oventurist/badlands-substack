# Senate Confirmation Process

The **Senate confirmation process** is the constitutional mechanism by which the [[us-senate|United States Senate]] provides advice and consent on presidential nominees to executive-branch and judicial offices. It received sustained attention in early February 2025 as the second [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration worked to seat its Cabinet.[1]

## Mechanics

As summarized by [[badlands-media|Badlands]] contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], the sequence runs as follows: the President nominates a person for a position; the Senate committee or committees of jurisdiction hold hearings; and after hearings those committees vote to advance the nomination out of committee with a favorable or unfavorable recommendation.[1] Once a nomination clears committee it moves to the Senate floor, where — reportedly — only one nominee may be debated at a time, creating the principal bottleneck.[1] When the Senate votes to invoke cloture, a thirty-hour clock begins that limits further debate; when the clock expires the chamber votes, which explains why confirmation votes are frequently scheduled at unusual hours.[1]

Because the minority may consume the full thirty hours per nominee, the process stretched to roughly a day and a half per Cabinet post in early 2025, a delay Badlands attributed to Democratic senators dragging out debate.[1] They did not always use every available minute: [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]]'s vote had been expected around one o'clock Wednesday morning but instead took place Tuesday evening.[1] Ashe in America summarized the whole apparatus by quoting the pitchman Billy Mays — "There's got to be a better way!"[1]

## Status as of February 4, 2025

Confirmed at that point were Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]], CIA Director [[john-ratcliffe|John Ratcliffe]], Secretary of Defense [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]], DHS Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]], Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]], Transportation Secretary [[sean-duffy|Sean Duffy]], EPA Administrator [[lee-zeldin|Lee Zeldin]], Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum, Secretary of Energy [[chris-wright|Chris Wright]], Secretary of Veterans Affairs Doug Collins, and Attorney General Pam Bondi.[1]

On the floor: the Senate invoked cloture on Department of Housing and Urban Development nominee Eric Scott Turner, with the thirty-hour clock beginning around 8:30 p.m. Tuesday; OMB Director nominee [[russ-vought|Russell Vought]] had been voted out of the Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee the previous Thursday; HHS nominee [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy, Jr.]] was voted out of the Finance Committee Tuesday with a favorable recommendation; Agriculture nominee [[brooke-rollins|Brooke Rollins]] cleared the Agriculture Committee on Monday; Director of National Intelligence nominee [[tulsi-gabbard|Tulsi Gabbard]] advanced from the Intelligence Committee Tuesday with a favorable recommendation; and Secretary of the Army nominee [[dan-driscoll|Dan Driscoll]] remained in the Armed Services Committee.[1]

Still in committee: Commerce nominee [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]] faced a Commerce Committee vote Wednesday; Small Business Administrator nominee [[kelly-loeffler|Kelly Loeffler]] faced an SBA Committee vote Wednesday; FBI Director nominee [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] faced a Judiciary Committee vote Thursday; and U.S. Trade Representative nominee [[jamieson-greer|Jamison Greer]] had a Finance Committee hearing Thursday.[1] The Senate was scheduled to convene at 10:30 a.m. on Wednesday, February 5, 2025, and conduct two votes at 11:30 a.m.: confirmation of Turner and cloture on Vought.[1]

## Second Trump administration, February 2025

By the close of business on Wednesday, February 5, 2025, twelve of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s second-term cabinet nominees had been confirmed: Secretary of State Marco Rubio, CIA Director John Ratcliffe, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth, DHS Secretary Kristi Noem, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, Energy Secretary Chris Wright, Veterans Affairs Secretary Doug Collins, Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]], and HUD Secretary [[scott-turner|Eric Scott Turner]], the last confirmed that day by a vote of 55-44.[2]

Awaiting floor votes at that point were Robert F. Kennedy (HHS), Brooke Rollins (Agriculture), Tulsi Gabbard (Intelligence), Dan Driscoll (Army), Howard Lutnick (Commerce), and Kelly Loeffler (SBA), with Lutnick and Loeffler having advanced from their committees with favorable recommendations.[2] On the same day, [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] faced a Judiciary Committee vote on his nomination for FBI Director, and the Finance Committee held a hearing on Jamieson Greer's nomination as U.S. Trade Representative.[2] Two additional meetings were added to the Senate calendar for February 12, 2025, including a HELP Committee hearing on Lori Chavez-DeRemer's nomination as Secretary of Labor and unspecified "hearings to examine pending nominations" in the Judiciary Committee.[2]

The Senate invoked cloture on OMB Director nominee [[russ-vought|Russell Vought]] on February 4, 2025; absent an agreement, his confirmation vote was scheduled for approximately 7:00 pm on Thursday, February 6, with the chamber expected to remain in session for the full 30 hours of post-cloture debate.[2]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that the pace was slower than under Barack Obama, all but two of whose nominees had been confirmed by the equivalent point in 2009, but faster than under Joe Biden, who had only four confirmed nominees at the same stage in 2021.[2]

## January 2025 Trump cabinet hearings

The January 2025 round of confirmation hearings for Donald Trump's second-term cabinet drew commentary on both preparation and partisanship.[3] Representative [[ryan-zinke|Ryan Zinke]] told Newsmax that Trump's slate was "enormously talented" and "very well prepared," claiming the nominees were better prepared on policy than some Democratic senators questioning them.[3] Badlands coverage of [[doug-burgum|Doug Burgum]]'s Senate Energy Committee hearing found Republicans focused on land access and Democrats on green solutions, with baseload electricity demand the central technical dispute, and reported that [[lee-zeldin|Lee Zeldin]]'s EPA hearing was similarly energy-focused.[3] The overall takeaway offered was that legislators were most concerned with protecting federal funding streams and enabling public-private partnerships in every sector.[3]

A parallel controversy concerned Democratic questioning about whether Donald Trump "lost" the 2020 election.[3] The Federalist argued that the query includes an undefined term — "lost" — carrying different meanings for Trump supporters and for anti-Trump questioners: if "lost" merely meant that Joe Biden became president, the answer is straightforward, and nominee Pam Bondi repeatedly and expressly attested that Biden is the president of the United States.[3] What senators such as Dick Durbin and Richard Blumenthal sought, the argument ran, was a concession that Trump's election challenges were frivolous, unfounded, or wrong, a meaning Democrats inject into the word "lost" — as the legacy media had earlier done in hounding then-Senator [[jd-vance|J.D. Vance]].[3]
