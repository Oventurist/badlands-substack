# NAFTA

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) is presented in the Badlands Media series not as a trade policy but as an instrument of narcotics logistics. The author states that in the preceding article he connected the Bush and [[salinas-family|Salinas]] families to a massive increase in drug trafficking across the Mexican border under the guise of free trade, and that NAFTA "was all about increasing drug trafficking across the southern border in order to increase the [[cia|CIA]]'s profits."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

The mechanism offered is the delivery guarantee that [[juan-garcia-abrego|Juan García Abrego]] accepted from the [[cali-cartel|Cali Cartel]] in exchange for 50 percent of every Colombian shipment: because the [[gulf-cartel|Gulf Cartel]] now had to guarantee arrival, easier border crossings became commercially essential.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair) A third family — the Ruiz Massieus — is introduced in this article as also connected to the NAFTA scandal and drug trafficking.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-family-affair)

## Role in the unipolar order

In [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s account, NAFTA belongs to the institutional architecture of the unipolar world order that followed the collapse of the Soviet system in the early 1990s, alongside the European Union, the [[world-trade-organization|World Trade Organization]] and the 1999 destruction of [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]].[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-re-alignment-of-world-systems) Together these converted the trans-Atlantic into a cage of "post-sovereign nations" that no longer had actual control of their own powers of credit generation.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-re-alignment-of-world-systems) Ehret adds that NAFTA has since been seriously challenged by a restoration of protectionism in the United States, one of several developments he reads as marking the end of the unipolar script.[[2]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-re-alignment-of-world-systems)

## Deindustrialization and the Kissinger endorsement

Ehret elsewhere calls NAFTA a "deadly treaty" passed within the same period as the [[big-bang-deregulation|Big Bang]] deregulation and the rise of [[derivatives|derivatives]].[3] Speaking to the Los Angeles Times in 1993, [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] — who had just been knighted by [[queen-elizabeth-ii|Queen Elizabeth II]] as a member of the Order of St. Michael and St. George — described NAFTA as "the most creative step towards a new world order taken by any group of countries since the end of the Cold War."[3]

With the agreement made law, protective programs that had kept North American factories in the United States and Canada were struck down, allowing the export of a highly skilled industrial workforce's livelihood to Mexico, where skills, technologies and salaries were lower.[3] Stripped of productive assets, North America became increasingly reliant on exporting cheap resources and services, so that physically productive powers collapsed while monetary profits skyrocketed.[3] The same pattern was replicated in Europe through the [[maastricht-treaty|Maastricht Treaty]] and in the former Soviet Union through [[perestroika|Perestroika]].[3] Together with universal banking and Euro integration, NAFTA induced a cartelization of finance through newly legalized mergers and acquisitions at an unprecedented rate.[3]

## The narrative and the "real purpose"

In "The Legacy Of George H.W. Bush," [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] treats NAFTA as the definitive example of the American public being "sold a bill of goods."[4] Signed by [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]], Canadian Prime Minister [[brian-mulroney|Brian Mulroney]] and Mexican President [[carlos-salinas-de-gortari|Carlos Salinas de Gortari]] in December 1992, a month before Bush left office, it was remembered by the Washington Post as his biggest accomplishment and as the achievement of a champion of free trade and key architect of globalization.[4] Bush said shortly after signing that "the gulfs that separate the few who are very rich from the many who are very poor... must be bridged" and that economic reform "must ensure upward mobility and new opportunities for a better life for all citizens of the Americas."[4] Lange reads those three promises — upward mobility, new opportunities, a better life — as a [[shadow-government|shadow government]] narrative crafted to win approval, and a red herring: the wealth gap widened rather than closed.[4]

The agreement's main provisions called for gradual reduction of tariffs, customs duties and other trade barriers among the three members, some removed immediately and others over as long as 15 years, ensuring eventual duty-free access for a vast range of manufactured goods and commodities.[4] Critically, imported goods from NAFTA countries received "National Goods" status, banning any state, local or provincial government from imposing taxes or tariffs on them.[4] Economic Policy Institute chief economist Robert E. Scott called NAFTA "the template for dozens of trade deals done by the United States and other countries."[4] [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] campaigned against it in 2016 as "an absolute catastrophe for our country."[4]

Lange's thesis is that the barriers, not the tariffs, were the point. With [[raul-salinas-de-gortari|Raúl Salinas de Gortari]] using government trucks and railroad cars from Mexico's food-distribution agency to haul cocaine to the border under "green light days" arranged with bribed army and police, the shipments could then cross as "National Goods" exempt from state and local inspection.[4] The hidden purpose, on this account, was to massively increase drug trafficking across the southern border in order to increase the profits of the [[cia|CIA]] and the shadow government, while also allowing cabal-controlled corporations to relocate operations to the cheapest labour.[4] The agreement was negotiated on the Mexican side by [[raul-salinas-lozano|Raúl Salinas Lozano]], the Minister of Commerce and father of both the president and the alleged drug kingpin, and a personal friend of Bush.[4] NAFTA rules being written in spring 1993 also required Mexico to open its banking market to foreign banks, though the Salinas administration granted the newcomers only limited access, preserving [[citibank|Citibank]]'s privileged position.[4]
