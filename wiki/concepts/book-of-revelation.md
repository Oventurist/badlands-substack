# Book of Revelation

The **Book of Revelation** is the final book of the Christian New Testament and the interpretive foundation of the Badlands Media essay "Are We Entering the Millennial Reign?", in which the pseudonymous contributor Patriots in Progress ([[patriots-in-progress|PiP]]) treats the book as a live framework for reading contemporary events.[1]

## Authorship and setting

The article states that Revelation was written by the apostle John, who also authored the Gospel of John and 1–3 John, and that by the time of the vision John had been exiled to the island of Patmos as a death sentence for his faith and ministry in Christ.[1] The author calls this a light sentence relative to other apostles such as Peter, who suffered punishments up to and including being crucified upside down.[1] The vision is dated to roughly 80 A.D., while the parallel visions of Daniel are dated to roughly 600 B.C.[1]

## Interpretive difficulty

The essay describes Revelation as arguably the most debated book of the Bible because of its extensive use of symbolism, metaphor, apocalyptic language, complex themes, and prophecy.[1] Interpretation is further complicated by the historical distance between the vision of the future and the first-century moment of writing, by cultural differences, by the inadequacy of human language, and by millennia of accumulated interpretation — as well as by what the author calls the infiltration, division, misleading, and pacification of the church.[1] Summarising the state of the debate, the author declares that "WE ARE ALL WRONG," meaning not that nothing has been understood correctly but that it is unlikely anyone has understood everything correctly, and that close-minded certainty is therefore unwise.[1]

## Structure as used in the article

The essay walks through Revelation's major movements in sequence: the appearance of Christ at His second coming; the letters to the seven churches; [[the-tribulation|the Tribulation]] with its seals, trumpets, and bowls; [[the-beast|the Beast]] and the [[mark-of-the-beast|Mark of the Beast]]; the fall of [[babylon-revelation|Babylon]]; and the establishment of the [[millennial-reign|Millennial Reign]].[1] Three references to three-and-a-half-year periods (Revelation 11:2–3, 12:6, and 13:5) are noted as the textual basis many readers use to construct a literal seven-year Tribulation.[1]

## Christ's appearance and "eyes like a flame of fire"

The author highlights John's description of Christ with "eyes like a flame of fire," a simile also used in two other scriptural passages including Daniel's vision.[1] Because fire in scripture commonly represents purification and judgement, and because both John and Daniel plainly knew what fire looked like yet resorted to simile, the author asks what else ancient writers could have said if they had seen laser eyes — lasers not having been discovered until 1960.[1] This becomes the hinge for the essay's argument that the laser-eyes meme adopted by [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] holders worldwide may be a manifestation of prophecy rather than a random internet joke.[1]

## The letters to the seven churches

Of the seven churches addressed by Christ, five — roughly 71 percent — are rebuked, for turning from Christ, tolerating false teachings and prophets, participating in immorality, being spiritually dead, being lukewarm, and placing faith in worldly riches.[1] The rebukes were calls to repentance and renewed faithfulness, and the author argues the modern church exhibits approximately the same breakdown.[1] His leading example is the church's response to the COVID-19 mandates: an October 2020 report is cited stating that "39 percent of Christians now say they would want their congregation to abide by a government shutdown order," even as restrictions on churches — including attempts to restrict singing during worship — exceeded those on liquor stores, cannabis shops, and large retailers such as Walmart, Amazon, and Home Depot.[1]

## Prophecy as "future proves past"

The author argues that biblical prophecy has historically unfolded in a manner similar to the Q drops, on a "future proves past" pattern, with some prophecies still awaiting fulfilment.[1] He therefore urges continual assessment of the complete picture of world events against the prophecies and warnings of the Bible.[1] Throughout, he disclaims prophetic authority himself, insisting the piece is a thought exercise on matters inconsequential to salvation, which he defines per Christian theology as a personal relationship with Jesus Christ (Romans 10:9).[1]
