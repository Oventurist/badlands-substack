# Anderson v. Griswold

Anderson v. Griswold is the Colorado case in which plaintiffs sought to bar [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the state's 2024 primary ballot under the disqualification clause of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]], naming Colorado Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] as respondent.[1]

## Findings and appeal

The trial court made a finding of fact that January 6 was an insurrection, and that finding was upheld by the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]].[1] Trump nonetheless remained on the [[colorado|Colorado]] primary ballot, which led many legal commentators to treat the case as inconsequential on the assumption that no court would ultimately endorse the disenfranchisement of tens of millions of voters or the unequal treatment of voters in a presidential election.[1]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the opposite: that the durable output of the case was the insurrection finding itself, which functioned as a building block for further action — including Maine Secretary of State [[shenna-bellows|Shenna Bellows]]' unilateral removal of Trump from the [[maine|Maine]] ballot.[1] On that view, the appeals brought by the Colorado GOP and Trump require the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] to rule on the underlying factual findings and not merely on the Fourteenth Amendment question, since a narrow ruling would leave the predicate intact.[1]

## Stakes beyond ballot access

The concern raised was that an uncorrected insurrection finding could bar military-veteran January 6 attendees from running for office — an outcome said to be already operative in Colorado — and would "green light the uniparty's next phase of lawfare-as-election-interference," affirming that "fake insurrections are effective change tactics."[1] A proposed test case was the candidacy of [[ron-hanks|Ron Hanks]], a U.S. Air Force veteran and former state representative who attended January 6 and later announced a run for [[lauren-boebert|Lauren Boebert]]'s abandoned seat in Colorado's third congressional district.[1]

## Supreme Court oral argument, February 8, 2024

Oral arguments were heard by the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] on Thursday, February 8, 2024, on the question of whether the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] erred in ordering Trump removed from the state's 2024 ballot, and the justices appeared to be "leaning in favor" of the former president according to Fox News legal correspondent Shannon Bream.[2] Justice [[elena-kagan|Elena Kagan]] questioned whether one state should decide on behalf of the entire country who should be president, while Chief Justice [[john-roberts|John Roberts]] warned that a ruling for the plaintiffs would invite states to strike Democrats or Republicans from their ballots and cautioned about the "daunting consequences" of letting a handful of states pick the president.[2] Justice [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]] challenged petitioners' counsel [[jason-murray|Jason Murray]] on his claim that Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment was unambiguous.[2]

As the petitioning party, Trump's attorneys argued first, with [[jonathan-mitchell|Jonathan Mitchell]] advancing multiple interrelated grounds for reversal; the justices probed the evidentiary record below, particularly the admission of the January 6 Report and Professor Simi's testimony.[2] Justices [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]], [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]], and [[brett-kavanaugh|Brett Kavanaugh]] pressed Murray hardest, with Alito dissecting the hearsay problem and criticizing the insurrection findings, and Justice [[amy-coney-barrett|Amy Coney Barrett]] focusing on constitutional nuance.[2] Kavanaugh emphasized the lack of due process, quoting Colorado Justice Samour's dissent: "There was no fair trial either … I have been involved in the justice system for thirty-three years now, and what took place here doesn't resemble anything I've seen in a courtroom."[2]

Griswold was allotted ten minutes, argued by Colorado Solicitor General [[shannon-stevenson|Shannon Stevenson]], who at one point objected to Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] before Roberts clarified her role and challenged her to defend the insurrection determination and its standards.[2] Badlands commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] judged that Stevenson struggled to explain how a state disenfranchising its own voters in a federal election could survive equal protection scrutiny, but credited her for urging the justices to review the findings of fact and conclusions of law on insurrection.[2]

A closed-door conference vote was expected within days, with a decision possible in days or months, and the Court was expected to move quickly because the Colorado primary fell on March 5; many similar cases in other states were held in limbo pending the ruling.[2] Ashe assessed that the high court was unlikely to uphold the lower court's ruling but that it remained unclear how far the justices would go regarding the insurrection findings.[2] [[just-human|Just Human]] summarized the argument as the Court being "absolutely frigid" toward the case for removing Trump from the ballot.[2]

## December 2023 oral argument

On December 6, 2023, the Colorado Supreme Court heard argument on whether former president [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] should be kept off the state's Republican primary ballot under Section 3 of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]], one of a nationwide set of challenges in more than a dozen states and a case widely expected to reach the U.S. Supreme Court.[3] The first question before the court was whether Section 3 applies to the president as an "Officer" within the meaning of the text.[3] There was extensive discussion of Confederate president [[jefferson-davis|Jefferson Davis]] and of parallels between the Civil War and the chaos at the Capitol on January 6, and justices raised the possibility of unequal treatment of Colorado's Republican voters if they were denied the chance to vote for Trump.[3] Eric Olsen argued for the challengers that Trump's inclusion would dilute votes for "qualified" candidates, while [[scott-gessler|Scott Gessler]] argued for Trump that January 6 was better understood as a riot than an insurrection; Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] judged the arguments on both sides weak.[3]

## Possible outcomes and timeline

Ashe outlined four possible dispositions: upholding the district court on both the Fourteenth Amendment and insurrection questions; overturning both; upholding on the amendment while overturning the insurrection finding (her preferred result); or overturning on the amendment while upholding the insurrection finding, which would remove Trump from the ballot.[3] She noted that Olsen had defended Judge Sarah Wallace's civil-court finding that Trump intentionally incited insurrection, a criminal-style determination Ashe considered improper.[3] The ruling was expected early the following week and in any event before the statutory deadline of December 29, 2023, with implications for 22 other Section 3 challenges nationwide and an appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court considered certain regardless of outcome.[3]
