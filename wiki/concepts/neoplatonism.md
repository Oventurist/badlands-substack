# Neoplatonism

Neoplatonism is an ancient and highly influential spiritually-based political theology — described by [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] as "of sorts, a revival of Plato's philosophy" — that emerged in the 3rd century CE.[1] In Deschamps' analysis, published in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] as historical context for the [[october-7-attacks|October 7 attacks]] on Israel, Neoplatonism supplied the hidden ideological scaffolding for centuries of Holy Land conquest.[1]

## Core cosmology

In simple terms, Neoplatonism holds that there exists a divinely perfect, immutable realm beyond the orbit of the moon — a realm of perfect forms and ideas called the supralunary realm.[1] That divinely perfect realm was where aristocrats and monarchs claimed to derive their power and authority to rule on earth.[1] Correspondingly, occultists, religious adepts and aristocrats widely believed that man had fallen into the sublunary world as a result of Eve eating the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden.[1]

The post-Adamic world of sin, vice and suffering, on this view, would only end when man chose to redeem himself by carrying out prophecies related to Israel, retaking the holy land, and enthroning the messiah over all mankind.[1] The Messiah could remove sin, redeem the world, and make new covenants with man, while raising up his chosen people as rulers over the uncivilized and unclean masses.[1]

## Political function

Within the courts of many monarchs there was a keen interest in philosophy, alchemy, and the spiritual purpose and course of life on earth; specifically, it was believed that mankind could be restored to a divine state partly by taking back the holy land for God's people — a kind of religio-political alchemy.[1] Kings, queens and their intelligence-agency helpers favored this idea because it effectively endorsed the ends-justify-the-means method of rule: as long as one did what "God" wanted, all manner of horrors were justified, even necessary.[1]

The Neoplatonic worldview fit neatly with the aristocratic belief that monarchs and their assigns were divinely appointed to rule the uncivilized masses, and equally neatly with their conquest ambitions.[1] The quest for Jerusalem was woven into war propaganda reaching back to the Crusades — which Deschamps describes as several centuries of Holy Land campaigns to take the land back from Muslims, likely secretly influenced by the Neoplatonic worldview.[1] For ordinary people the Crusades meant one thing; for elites subscribing to this worldview they were a pathway to apotheosis — man becoming God.[1]

## Legacy

Deschamps traces the same current forward through [[sabbateanism|Sabbateanism]] and [[jacob-frank|Frankism]], the [[bavarian-illuminati|Bavarian Illuminati]], and ultimately into modern [[zionism|Zionism]] and the Christian factions that continue to fan the flames of crusade and conquest for Israel.[1]
