# Arab Spring

The Arab Spring refers to the wave of uprisings that swept the Middle East and North Africa beginning in 2011.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands Media treats the Arab Spring as a set of orchestrated "revolutions" rather than spontaneous popular movements.[1] In this account, the rest of the Middle East developed lasting bad blood with the Qataris after 2011, when it became obvious that [[qatar|Qatar]] was connected to the engineered uprisings and its state-linked outlet [[al-jazeera|Al Jazeera]] — described as a [[muslim-brotherhood|Muslim Brotherhood]] platform and therefore, in the outlet's reading, probably tied to London as well — became "the main cheerleader for the color revolutions."[1]

This framing underpins the later Gulf rupture: the 2017 Saudi-led embargo of Qatar, justified by [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] on the grounds that Doha was a terrorist haven enabling psychological warfare operations through outlets such as Al Jazeera, is presented as downstream of Arab Spring grievances.[1]

## Badlands account of Western sponsorship

Badlands contributor Joe Lange argues that the Arab Spring was not a spontaneous wave of protest but a coordinated set of insurgencies engineered from Washington.[2] Citing a 2011 New York Times report, he notes that American democracy-building campaigns played a bigger role in fomenting the protests than was previously known, with key leaders of the movements trained by Americans in campaigning, organizing through new media tools, and monitoring elections.[2] Groups and individuals directly involved — including the April 6 Youth Movement in Egypt, the Bahrain Center for Human Rights, and Yemeni youth leader Entsar Qadhi — received training and financing from the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]], the [[national-democratic-institute|National Democratic Institute]] and [[freedom-house|Freedom House]], according to interviews and diplomatic cables obtained by WikiLeaks.[2] The Republican and Democratic institutes were created by Congress and are financed through the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]], which receives about $100 million annually from Congress, while Freedom House draws the bulk of its money from the U.S. government, mainly the [[state-department|State Department]].[2] Lange characterizes these organizations as front groups for [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and shadow-government [[color-revolution|color revolution]] operations.[2]

## The Muslim Brotherhood thread

In this account the common thread among the leaders deposed or targeted — Zine El Abidine Ben Ali of Tunisia, [[muammar-gaddafi|Muammar Gaddafi]] of Libya, [[hosni-mubarak|Hosni Mubarak]] of Egypt, Ali Abdullah Saleh of Yemen, and [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al-Assad]] of Syria — is that all were suppressing the [[muslim-brotherhood|Muslim Brotherhood]] and preventing it from enforcing strict [[sharia-law|Sharia law]].[2] The Obama administration instead framed them as oppressive rulers facing democratic uprisings.[2] In Egypt the sequence produced the presidency of [[mohamed-morsi|Mohamed Morsi]], mass protests against his imposition of Sharia, and his removal by the military; Egyptian investigations concluded American NGOs had been behind the original overthrow and began arresting those involved, including IRI members.[2]

Lange further alleges that funding flowed through two channels: [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] asked [[qatar|Qatar]] to finance the Arab Spring revolutions through a fund dedicated to the [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]], while Senator [[john-mccain|John McCain]] funded the uprisings through the IRI, which he had chaired since 1993.[2] He also ties the [[benghazi-attack|Benghazi attack]] narrative to the Arab Spring, arguing the administration's claim that an [[innocence-of-muslims|anti-Islam video]] had triggered region-wide protests was designed to fold Benghazi into the same story.[2]

## Gene Sharp and the nonviolent-action playbook

In the second installment of the "How to Rent a Riot" series, Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] calls the Arab Spring one of [[gene-sharp|Gene Sharp]]'s "greatest hits," describing it as a series of antigovernmental protests, uprisings and armed rebellions that spread across much of the Arab world in the early 2010s.[3] It is frequently cited as the quintessential example of the usefulness of [[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]], with Sharp at its center.[3]

DeLarme writes that in January 2011 the public was told a "spontaneous," "indigenous" uprising had begun sweeping North Africa and the Middle East, and that it took almost four months before corporate media would admit U.S. involvement.[3] He points to the April 2011 New York Times article "U.S. Groups Helped Nurture Arab Uprisings," which named the April 6 Youth Movement in Egypt, the Bahrain Center for Human Rights and Yemeni youth leader Entsar Qadhi as recipients of training and financing from the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]], the [[national-democratic-institute|National Democratic Institute]] and [[freedom-house|Freedom House]].[3] In his framing the IRI represents [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] influence over the Republican party and the NDI the same over the Democratic party, both financed through the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]], which receives about $100 million annually from Congress.[3]
