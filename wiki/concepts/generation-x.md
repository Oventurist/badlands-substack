# Generation X

Generation X refers to Americans born between 1965 and 1979, a cohort aged roughly 45 to 60 as of late 2024 and numbering about 60 million people, making it one of the smaller American generations.[1] In a December 2024 Badlands Media essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argued that Gen X is in its prime earning years, accounts for more than half of American CEOs, and produced much of the innovation and technology in everyday use today.[1]

## The latchkey childhood

Carlson describes Gen X as the original [[latchkey-generation|Latchkey Generation]] — the first cohort of American children who routinely returned from school to an empty house or apartment because both parents worked, spending two or three hours unsupervised with no doorbell cameras or phone trackers.[1] Those afternoons were spent raiding the pantry for Top Ramen or Doritos in front of reruns of *Gilligan's Island*, *The Flintstones*, or *Saved by the Bell*, or roaming street corners, 7-Elevens, and woods depending on whether the child lived in a city, suburb, or rural area — with attendant risks ranging from trouble with the law to Evel Knievel–style bike ramps and three-story rope swings.[1]

The essay attributes this arrangement to two causes: sharply rising divorce rates and the mass entry of mothers into the workforce.[1] Carlson cites a divorce rate of 2.5 per 1,000 Americans in the 1950s, rising to 3.2 per 1,000 by the end of the 1960s, with roughly 50% of marriages begun in the 1970s ending in divorce.[1] He further argues that reliance on convenience among two-income households helped drive the explosion of processed food and fast-food industries in 1970s America.[1]

## Formative-era argument

Carlson's central claim is that Gen X was shaped during a "pocket of time when the propagandization of American society was in remission."[1] He notes that the human brain is not fully developed until age 25, meaning Gen X brains matured between 1990 and 2004, after the movements of the 1960s and before the later ones: 9/11 occurred in 2001, the trans movement in 2012, and the COVID narrative in 2020, by which time most of Gen X were well into adulthood.[1] Gen X is described as the first generation commonly raised amid divorce, processed food, and television, and the last both to precede the [[transgender-movement|LGBTQ and trans movements]]' peak influence and to grow up without the internet and social media.[1]

The [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]] presidency is presented as the defining backdrop: Gen X was aged 2 to 16 when Reagan took office in 1981 and 10 to 24 when he left in 1989.[1] Carlson credits the 1984 Los Angeles Olympics and Reagan-era cultural output — *Rambo* displacing *The Deer Hunter*, *The Cosby Show* displacing *Good Times*, *Vacation* displacing *Kramer vs. Kramer*, and *Red Dawn* displacing *All the President's Men* — with reigniting American pride, alleging the studios were commissioned by the Reagan administration to make inspiring films.[1] The 1983 made-for-TV nuclear war film *The Day After* is cited as a countervailing attempt to instill fear.[1]

## Sexual identity statistics

Carlson reports LGBTQ+ identification by generation as 4% of Baby Boomers, 7% of Gen X, 11.2% of Millennials, and 19.7% of Gen Z, and transgender identification as 0.2%, 0.3%, 1%, and 1.9% respectively.[1] He argues the near-doubling between adjacent generations cannot be genetic and therefore reflects societal and media influence, while stating he does not care how adults identify "as long as they leave the kids alone."[1]

## 2024 election claim

Citing 2024 election exit statistics — 42% of voters aged 18–29, 46% aged 30–44, 53% aged 45–64, and 49% aged 65 and over backing [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] — Carlson notes that the 45–64 bracket, the closest proxy for Gen X, was the only age group to give Trump a majority, and asserts that without it Trump would not have won.[1] He allows conventional explanations (high earners preferring lower taxes, parents of school-age children reacting to woke policy, business leaders preferring deregulation) but argues the deeper cause is Gen X's relative immunity to propaganda.[1] He also observes that Gen X occupies a technological "sweet spot," less enslaved to technology than younger cohorts but more capable with it than older ones.[1]

Finally, the essay notes the irony that Trump, a Baby Boomer, is removing a [[deep-state|Deep State]] cast drawn largely from the Boomer and Silent generations — the Obamas, Clintons, Bidens, Pelosi, Schumer, McConnell — and replacing them with appointees who are predominantly Gen X, suggesting Trump had to wait decades for his supporting cast to be ready.[1]
