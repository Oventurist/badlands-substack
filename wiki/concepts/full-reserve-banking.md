# Full Reserve Banking

**Full reserve banking**, also called 100% reserve banking or the sovereign money system, is a banking model in which banks do not lend demand deposits and instead lend only from time deposits.[1] It stands in contrast to [[fractional-reserve-banking|fractional reserve banking]], where banks may lend funds that are simultaneously held on deposit; under a full reserve regime a bank would be required to keep the full amount of each depositor's funds in cash, available for immediate withdrawal on demand.[1]

## Origins

The economist, statistician, inventor, eugenicist and progressive social campaigner [[irving-fisher|Irving Fisher]] proposed a full reserve system in 1935.[1]

## Adoption

Full reserve banking never took hold in practice. No country currently requires it across its primary credit institutions, although Iceland has considered the idea.[1] In a 2018 ballot referendum, Switzerland voted overwhelmingly to reject the [[sovereign-money-initiative|Sovereign Money Initiative]], which had full reserve banking as a prominent component of its proposed reform of the Swiss monetary system.[1] Badlands Media notes that the model has not been tried in modern times since the Bank of Amsterdam, leaving open the question of how it would function today.[1]

## Advocacy and criticism

The [[international-monetary-fund|International Monetary Fund]] revisited the idea in its report [[the-chicago-plan-revisited|The Chicago Plan Revisited]], which calls fractional reserve banking "problematic" and discusses the perceived benefits of a fully reserved system.[1] The Kucinich [[need-act|HR2990 N.E.E.D. Act]], drafted by the American Monetary Institute's Stephen Zarlenga, likewise called for a debt-free government money unit and for credit to be provided through full reserve banking.[1]

Critics from within the anti-fractional-reserve camp argue the reform is insufficient. To many detractors the underlying problem is not money creation but interest: whether loans are fully or partly backed, money still flows upward to lenders.[1] The writer and economic historian [[gary-north|Gary North]] held that a full reserve, gold-based system would eliminate theft, yet a borrower would still pay roughly $300,000 in interest on a $200,000 mortgage — merely in gold coin or gold-backed paper instead.[1] Badlands Media adds that returning to gold might do little to restore power to ordinary people, since gold-based lending is what elevated the [[rothschild-family|Rothschilds]] and similar houses in the first place.[1]
