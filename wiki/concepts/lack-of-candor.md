# Lack of Candor

"Lack of candor" is an administrative and disciplinary standard applied to United States federal employees. As defined in the July 2025 Badlands Media article "Shall We Play a Game?", it describes a failure to provide complete and accurate information in response to a lawful inquiry; it does not necessarily involve outright lying, but centres on knowingly withholding relevant information or supplying incomplete details.[1] In [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]]'s reading, the standard is the connective legal tissue running through the whole Russiagate affair, and the word "knowingly" that accompanies it is the element that converts an employment offence into evidence of conspiracy.[1]

## Application to Andrew McCabe

The charge was the stated basis for the dismissal of FBI Deputy Director [[andrew-mccabe|Andrew McCabe]] by Attorney General [[jeff-sessions|Jeff Sessions]] in March 2018. Sessions said that both the [[department-of-justice-office-of-inspector-general|Office of the Inspector General]] and the FBI's Office of Professional Responsibility had concluded that McCabe made an unauthorised disclosure to the news media and "lacked candor — including under oath — on multiple occasions."[1] Inspector General [[michael-horowitz|Michael Horowitz]] found he had not been forthcoming during the review, which concerned unauthorised disclosures in October 2016 relating to the bureau's [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] email probe and allegations that he had shut down the [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]] investigation.[1] McCabe published a Washington Post op-ed denying any lack of candor and denying that he had knowingly misled investigators.[1]

Lange emphasises the symmetry: McCabe had himself authorised a criminal inquiry into whether Sessions lacked candor before Congress about his contacts with Russian officials, and was then fired on the identical ground — an outcome the article labels a "boomerang."[1]

## Institutional lack of candor and the FISA court

The phrase also appears in its institutional form in the [[foreign-intelligence-surveillance-court|FISA court]]'s 99-page opinion signed by Judge [[rosemary-collyer|Rosemary Collyer]] on April 26, 2017, which criticised the [[national-security-agency|NSA]]'s Office of Inspector General and Office of Compliance for Operations for "an institutional lack of candor" over prohibited database searches.[1] Former acting Defense Intelligence Agency director David Shedd told the Daily Signal that the only pattern linking the two categories of [[fisagate|FISA abuse]] was the "absence of candor" with the court.[1]

## The "knowingly" argument

Lange connects the standard to 18 U.S. Code § 2385, quoted in Q drop 3638 of December 2, 2019, which penalises whoever "knowingly or willfully advocates, abets, advises, or teaches the duty, necessity, desirability, or propriety of overthrowing or destroying the government of the United States."[1] On this reasoning, McCabe's vigorous public resistance to the word "knowingly" reflected awareness that the term exposed him not merely to dismissal but to a treason-adjacent charge, and the same exposure is attributed to the wider set of [[barack-obama|Obama]] administration officials alleged to have participated in an attempted coup.[1] Lange also applies the standard to [[rod-rosenstein|Rod Rosenstein]]'s concealment from Horowitz of the memos documenting the proposal to record President [[donald-trump|Trump]].[1]
