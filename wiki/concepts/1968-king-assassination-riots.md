# 1968 King Assassination Riots

The murder of [[martin-luther-king-jr|Martin Luther King Jr.]] on April 4, 1968 triggered a new wave of urban race riots that swept through 120 American cities, killing 39 people — mostly black — and injuring 2,600.[1] More than 75,000 troops were deployed onto the streets of America during the period of tension.[1]

## Indianapolis

[[robert-f-kennedy|Robert F. Kennedy]] was aboard a plane bound for a presidential campaign rally in Indianapolis when word of King's death reached him.[1] Both the Indianapolis police chief and his own staff urged him to cancel the event for his own safety.[1] Kennedy ignored the advice, went directly into the city's ghettos, stood on a flatbed truck and delivered the news of King's death to thousands of poor, broken-hearted Americans standing on a razor's edge.[1]

Choosing to stand with the people entirely unprotected, Kennedy's words carried enough love and empathy to cut through the anger of the crowd, and Indianapolis became the only major American city in which no riots occurred.[1]

King's close associate Ralph Abernathy later told historian Arthur Schlesinger: "I was so despondent and frustrated at King's death, I had to seriously ask myself — can this country be saved? I guess the thing that kept us going was that maybe Bobby Kennedy would come up with some answers for the country… I remember telling him he had a chance to be a prophet. But prophets get shot."[1]

Two months later Kennedy himself was killed in Los Angeles.[1] Badlands Media presents the Indianapolis speech as the practical model for defusing the civil conflict it argues is being engineered in the present day.[1]
