# TSA Shoe Removal Policy

The TSA shoe removal policy was the [[transportation-security-administration|Transportation Security Administration]] requirement that airline passengers take off their shoes at security checkpoints, in force from 2006 until it began to be rolled back in July 2025.[1]

## Origins

The TSA introduced the shoe removal rule in 2006, following the 2001 attempted bombing by [[richard-reid|Richard Reid]], who tried to ignite explosives hidden in his shoes during a trans-Atlantic American Airlines flight.[1] The incident led the agency to focus on screening footwear for concealed threats, a measure that quickly became a staple of post-9/11 airport security procedures across the United States.[1]

Exemptions were added incrementally: in 2011 the TSA updated its policy to exempt children aged 12 and under, allowing them to keep their shoes on during screening unless an alarm triggered additional screening, and in 2012 passengers 75 and older were granted the same allowance.[1]

## 2025 rollback

By Monday, July 7, 2025, news outlets including CBS News and *The New York Times* reported that passengers flying at certain US airports were no longer required to remove their shoes in security lines.[1] Coverage noted that the practice had frequently drawn complaints over inconvenience, slower processing lines and perceived burdens on travelers, and that the change could speed up security lines and make air travel less burdensome for millions of travelers each year.[1] *Newsweek* published a map showing which states had airports no longer enforcing the requirement.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the change as "the end of a stupid and inconvenient era," tracing the 2006 rule and its later age-based exemptions.[1] She argued the blanket policy never made sense and was applied to all of society because, in her framing, "the mockingbirds" told the public that profiling for terrorists was racist.[1] She cited a 2010 statement by former TSA administrator [[kip-hawley|Kip Hawley]] — "We apply the same rules to everyone because it's more equitable and more effective than trying to guess who looks suspicious" — as confirmation of that intention, concluding that it took 15 years to allow passengers to keep their shoes on, and that the delay was "always because, equity."[1]
