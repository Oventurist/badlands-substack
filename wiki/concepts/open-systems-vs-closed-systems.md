# Open Systems vs. Closed Systems

"Open systems versus closed systems" is the organizing framework used by historian [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] to describe what he presents as the principal civilizational conflict of the present epoch: a contest between an oligarchical, finite-resource model of political economy and a development model premised on unbounded scientific and industrial progress.[1] Ehret situates the debate against the backdrop of what he calls a $1.2 quadrillion [[derivatives]] time bomb whose collapse, in his account, will force a new system online whether or not humanity has chosen which system that will be.[1]

## The closed-system premise

A closed system, in Ehret's usage, is one presumed to be finite and bounded, in which the population alive at any moment tends over time to consume more energy than the system generates, as resources and agricultural potential are drawn down and entropy increases.[1] The unavoidable political consequence, he argues, is that someone must decide who receives the diminishing returns and who are the "useless eaters" to be sacrificed "for the greater good," making fascism the natural governing mechanism of a closed-system elite.[1]

Ehret identifies this outlook with a lineage of thinkers he characterizes as misanthropes, including [[thomas-malthus|Thomas Malthus]], T.H. Huxley, [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] and [[al-gore|Al Gore]], who he says use political clout to impose an obsession with "balance," "mathematical equilibrium" and linear predictability on society.[1] In this worldview, the self-described "alphas" of master-slave societies seek to impose "might-makes-right" jungle law, and imbalance — being wild, unpredictable and open — is treated as unnatural and evil.[1] He links the same logic to [[transhumanism]] as the closed system's technological endpoint.[1]

## The open-system premise

Open-system thinking treats imbalance as the generative absolute of development rather than a defect to be suppressed.[1] Ehret cites a 2016 speech by [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] to the CPC central committee in which Xi described coordinated development as "the unity of balanced development and imbalanced development," holding that "balance is relative, while imbalance is absolute," and that coordination means equal opportunity and balanced resource allocation rather than equalitarianism.[1] By placing imbalance as absolute, Ehret argues, Xi defines progress as a sequence of creative leaps, each higher system requiring a reasonable distribution of resources without becoming permanently dependent on any one finite resource base.[1]

[[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] expressed the same principle, in Ehret's reading, in remarks on [[fusion-power|fusion power]] as a "colossal, inexhaustible and safe source of energy," attainable only through broad international cooperation between governments, business and different scientific schools, and warning against attempts to monopolize progress, limit access to education or obstruct the free exchange of knowledge.[1]

Programs such as China's [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]] — together with its space, polar, health and information extensions — are described as entirely rooted in open-system thinking, having won over 135 nations to the framework, with no presumed fixed limit to resources or endpoint to progress.[1] Ehret adds that, based on their words and actions, Putin, Xi, [[narendra-modi|Modi]], [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al Assad]], [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] do not think in closed-system terms.[1]

## Moral and legal expressions

At the heart of the open-system framework is the moral concept of [[win-win-cooperation|win-win cooperation]], which [[sun-yat-sen|Sun Yat-sen]] called the principle of "Right makes Might" in his Three Principles of the People.[1] Sun understood in 1924, as Xi and Putin are said to today, that a nation adhering to right-makes-might thinking will never lose the Mandate of Heaven (Tianxia).[1] In the Western tradition the same principle appears in the [[peace-of-westphalia|Principle of Westphalia]], which in 1648 established the first modern nation states around the "Benefit of the Other"; when Kissinger, [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Brzezinski]] or [[tony-blair|Blair]] speak of a "post-Westphalian age," Ehret argues they are attacking that principle rather than merely national borders.[1]

The principle is reflected again in the UN Charter, designed by the anti-colonial President [[franklin-d-roosevelt|FDR]] to achieve international cooperation on economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems and to harmonize the actions of nations.[1] Ehret contends that FDR's early death and a British-Deep State takeover of America prevented these open-system dynamics from ever coming to life.[1]

## Points of mutual interest

Ehret proposes a set of domains in which the nations of the UN Security Council could anchor open-system cooperation: space diplomacy, asteroid defense, Arctic and Far East development, and nuclear energy.[1] He points to America's return to manned spaceflight on May 28, 2020 and the [[artemis-accords|Artemis Accords]] of May 15, 2020, Russia's lunar program beginning with Luna 25, and China's Chang'e program and Helium-3 mining ambitions as convergent lines of development.[1] On asteroid defense he cites [[dmitry-rogozin|Dmitry Rogozin]]'s 2011 "Strategic Defense of Earth" revival of Reagan's [[strategic-defense-initiative|Strategic Defense Initiative]].[1] On infrastructure he cites the [[bering-strait-tunnel|Bering Strait crossing]], China's [[polar-silk-road|Polar Silk Road]], the merger of the BRI with the [[eurasian-economic-union|Eurasian Economic Union]], and Trump's executive order reviving the [[alaska-canada-railway|Alaska-Canada Railway]].[1] Education and cultural exchange, fission and fusion research, and counterterrorism are named as further common ground for the UN Security Council and [[brics|BRICS]] nations.[1]
