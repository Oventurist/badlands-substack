# De Jure Republic Theory

De jure republic theory is a constitutional argument, circulated widely in alternative-media and sovereignty circles, holding that a legal distinction exists between "the United States of America" — the federation of sovereign states united under the 1787 Constitution, sometimes called the de jure or "original" republic — and the post-1871 "United States" without the words "of America."[1]

## The argument

Proponents contend that the latter term, as codified in the District of Columbia Organic Act of 1871, refers to a federal corporation, citing the definition of "United States" at 28 U.S.C. § 3002(15)(A), and describe it as a municipal government operating within the ten-mile square of the District of Columbia that extends its jurisdiction by contract and consent rather than by organic constitutional authority.[1] On this reading, precise capitalization and punctuation in official language carry legal weight, and a phrase such as "USA Citizen" is treated as a deliberate signal.[1]

## Use in commentary

The theory received attention in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] of November 11, 2025, where [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] invoked it while discussing President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[trump-tariff-dividend-proposal|$2,000 tariff dividend proposal]], suggesting that a president using the term "USA Citizens" could be indicating allegiance to the organic, de jure republic rather than to the municipal corporation.[1] Paul acknowledged that mainstream commentators — "people who tell us elections are real" — dismiss the framing as a conspiracy theory and warn that entertaining it invites trouble, but argued that such critics had largely discredited themselves.[1]
