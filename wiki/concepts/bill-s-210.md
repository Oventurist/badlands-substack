# Bill S-210

Bill S-210, formally *An Act to restrict young persons' online access to sexually explicit material*, is Canadian legislation that would impose an age-verification requirement on pornographic websites.[1] It was introduced in 2021 by Independent Senator Julie Miville-Dechêne and, unusually, moved through Parliament in reverse: it originated in the Senate, passed that chamber in April 2023, and then went to the House of Commons for consideration.[1]

## Legislative progress

The bill passed second reading in the House of Commons in December 2023 and was set to be studied by the standing committee on public safety and national security in spring 2024.[1] Debate centred on whether the measure would actually protect minors and on the privacy implications of requiring identity verification to access adult sites.[1]

## Industry response

The owners of [[pornhub|Pornhub]], one of the largest pornography sites on the internet, opposed the bill, calling it the "wrong legislation" and saying they were considering blocking access to Canadian users entirely.[1]

## Commentary

[[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] compared the episode to the "Machiavellian" manner in which the government of [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]] induced Facebook and Google to block news for Canadians, producing the same reflex of a platform withdrawing service rather than complying.[1] While stating a strong personal opposition to the pornography industry — which he described as reprehensible, addictive, culturally corrosive and implicated in facilitating child sex trafficking — Esler argued the bill's central danger is that it does not specify how age verification would be carried out, offering no guidelines on how personal data would be tied to a website.[1]

He drew an analogy to Canadian vaccine policy, where the government did not mandate vaccine passports outright but required businesses to have a vaccination policy, with social pressure driving most businesses toward "medical tyranny."[1] Vague rules and laws, he wrote, have always been used by tyrants to sow social disorder, and the bill could expose porn-consuming Canadians to surveillance, data theft and extortion; he cautioned that when corrupt governments invoke child protection, the long-run application of the emotionally charged claim deserves closer scrutiny.[1]
