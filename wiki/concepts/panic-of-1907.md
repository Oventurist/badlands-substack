# Panic of 1907

The Panic of 1907 was the financial crisis that, in the article's account, provided the pretext for creating American central banking.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

After the Panic, Senator [[nelson-aldrich|Nelson W. Aldrich]] set up the [[national-monetary-commission|National Monetary Commission]], which would investigate a better economic system for the United States.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913) The Commission concluded that the European model was better, including the Central Bank of England, and its reports and recommendations became one of the principal bases in the enactment of the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act of 1913]], which led to the creation of the modern [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] system.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/1913)

## Badlands Media's "Financial False Flag" Account

Badlands Media describes the Panic of 1907 as a deliberately engineered crash. It is widely believed, the article states, that in 1907 [[jp-morgan|J.P. Morgan]] and his cohorts — colloquially the [[money-trust|money trust]] — secretly crashed the stock market, knowing that thousands of small banks were vastly overextended, some holding reserves of only 1 percent under the [[fractional-reserve-banking|fractional reserve]] principle, so that bank runs quickly became commonplace nationwide.[2]

The commonly accepted story, by contrast, holds that copper magnate F. Augustus Heinze attempted to corner the copper market, which through a series of convoluted events resulted in runs on many major banks, and that at the height of the panic Morgan arrived to save the banking community and quell the collapse in an act of heroic altruism, after which he was touted as a beacon of financial wisdom and savior of the nation.[2]

## Consolidation

Morgan absorbed the Mercantile Trust and six more trust companies and banks, including the two largest and hardest hit — The Trust Company of America and Lincoln Trust — with his Guarantee Trust and Manhattan Trust companies taking control and the Rothschild family's US emissary [[august-belmont|August Belmont]] at the helm of the merger.[2] He promised Charles Barney he would help save the Knickerbocker Trust, one of the country's largest trust companies and a competitor of Morgan's Bankers' Trust Co., but in what the article calls an act of calculated cruelty pulled the plug at the last minute; Barney committed suicide soon after.[2]

## Aftermath

In 1908, with the panic over, Morgan was hailed as a hero by then-Princeton University president [[woodrow-wilson|Woodrow Wilson]], who stated that "all this trouble could be averted if we appointed a committee of six or seven public spirited men like J.P. Morgan, to handle the affairs of our country."[2] Others regard Morgan as a monster who fed off economic destruction; either way, banking power was further consolidated into the hands of a few large banks, and the assault on New York City banks became the catalyst for one of the largest cases of corporate consolidation in American history.[2]

In the wake of the turmoil, President [[theodore-roosevelt|Theodore Roosevelt]] signed into law a bill creating the [[national-monetary-commission|National Monetary Commission]], which was supposed to study the banking problem and make recommendations to Congress but was, the article says, packed with friends and cronies of the financial elite.[2] Representative [[charles-a-lindbergh-sr|Charles A. Lindbergh]] later charged that "Wall Street speculation brought on the Panic of 1907," and many at the time believed the panic had been premeditated and manufactured by Wall Street elites to gain ultimate control over banking and industry — though no action was ever taken against those responsible.[2]

## The Knickerbocker collapse as trust deception

A July 11, 2024 Badlands Media essay revisited the panic as a case study in the exploitation of trust, centring on the [[knickerbocker-trust-company|Knickerbocker Trust Company]], "a bank based in New York City that was a central player in the Panic of 1907."[3] Chartered in 1884 by Frederick G. Eldridge, a friend and classmate of the financier [[j-p-morgan|J.P. Morgan]], Knickerbocker's main business as a trust company was serving as trustee for individuals, corporations and estates.[3] In 1907 its funds were used in a plan to drive up the cost of copper by cornering the market, a gamble that came undone through the dumping of millions of dollars in copper.[3]

When the National Bank of Commerce announced it would no longer clear Knickerbocker's checks, a run of depositors demanding their funds forced the firm to suspend operations, exacerbating an ongoing decline in the stock market that saw the Dow Jones Industrial Average lose 48 percent of its value from January 1906 to November 1907.[3] The essay noted that the banking crisis is also seen as the final event leading Congress to form the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve System]] in 1913, and presented the sequence as an example of institutions built on trust being used against the depositors who extended it.[3]
