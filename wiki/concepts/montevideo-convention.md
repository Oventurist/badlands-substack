# Montevideo Convention

The Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States is the treaty underpinning the customary international law standard for statehood. It was signed in Uruguay on December 26, 1933, during the Seventh International Conference of American States.[1]

## The four criteria

The convention establishes four qualifications recognized by nations and international organizations. Its text provides that "the state as a person of international law should possess the following qualifications: (a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) government; and (d) capacity to enter into relations with the other states."[1]

## Application to Palestinian statehood

Badlands Media invoked the convention in August 2024 to argue that Palestine substantially satisfies the statehood test: it has a permanent population, a functioning government, and demonstrated capacity for international relations, evidenced by Vladimir Putin's invitation to Palestinian Authority President Mahmoud Abbas to attend the BRICS summit in Kazan that October.[1] The analysis also cited Abbas's letter to Donald Trump after the July 2024 assassination attempt, and Trump's handwritten Truth Social reply ("Everything will be good."), posted the night before Benjamin Netanyahu addressed a joint session of Congress for the fifth time, as further evidence of Palestinian relations with the United States.[1]

The remaining criterion — a defined territory — was described as the outstanding gap, since codified borders would technically require Israel to sign a treaty with Palestine. The commentary argued that broad international recognition, combined with a returning Trump administration pursuing what he had described to Abbas as "the final, and most important, peace deal," could leave Israel with little practical choice but to capitulate.[1]
