# Filibuster

The filibuster is the [[united-states-senate|United States Senate]] procedure that effectively requires a supermajority to advance most legislation, and which became a central point of conflict between President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and Senate Republicans during June 2026.[1]

## Trump's demand for termination (June 2026)

Writing on [[truth-social|Truth Social]], Trump declared that "anybody who doesn't want to Terminate the Filibuster is a FOOL, a very stupid one, at that."[1] He argued that Democrats would abolish the rule within minutes of retaking power and would then add two new states, four additional Democratic senators, numerous House members, an expanded Supreme Court of twenty-one justices, and an insurmountable Electoral College advantage — leaving him, he wrote, as "the last Republican President."[1]

Trump directed the demand explicitly at Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] and the Republican Senate, pairing termination of the filibuster with immediate approval of the [[save-america-act|SAVE America Act]] and warning that Republicans who stood by would "go down on the wrong side of History."[1] In a companion post he criticized Utah's reported shift to an all-[[mail-in-voting|mail-in ballot]] format, arguing that mail balloting dishonestly handled advantages Democrats, and again urged either terminating the filibuster or passing the SAVE America Act.[1]

## Context in the pace dispute

The filibuster fight formed part of a wider dispute over the pace of the administration's 2026 agenda, in which senators including [[thom-tillis|Thom Tillis]], [[john-cornyn|John Cornyn]], and [[bill-cassidy|Bill Cassidy]] cited Senate procedure and narrow majorities as constraints on rapid action.[1] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized procedural resistance of this kind as the behavior of a destabilized old guard attempting to slow a transformational change program.[1] Badlands separately noted that the SAVE America Act had, in its assessment, no realistic chance of passing even as media coverage described it as tying the Senate in knots.[1]

## Trump's December 2025 demand to terminate the filibuster

On December 1, 2025, President Trump publicly pressured Senate Republicans to end their "gridlock" and vote to terminate the filibuster, framing it as the only way to stop Democrats from packing the Supreme Court with as many as twenty-one justices, a claim he attributed to former Attorney General [[eric-holder|Eric Holder]].[2] Trump had made a similar demand the previous month, warning that the 2026 midterm elections would be "brutal" for Republicans if they could not terminate the filibuster and pass legislation.[2] Badlands commentary questioned whether a corrupted system can be repaired from within it, with hosts remarking that "nothing can stop what is coming except the filibuster" and that the filibuster merely delays outcomes.[2]

## November 2025 push to end the filibuster

In early November 2025 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] pressed to kill the filibuster in order to break the deadlock over the record-breaking [[october-2025-government-shutdown|government shutdown]], a step that would upend a [[us-senate|Senate]] tradition dating to the 1800s and, critics warned, could haunt both parties for years.[3] Trump argued that the rule gave Democrats leverage, telling Republican senators on Wednesday, November 5: "We have to get the country open... And the way we're going to do it this afternoon is to terminate the filibuster."[3] Advisers told [[axios|Axios]] that the president planned to make life a "living hell" for Republicans until they ended the rule, noting that politicians including Trump and former President Biden had called for its abolition before while senators hesitated over the long-term consequences.[3]

The pressure campaign culminated in a breakfast with Republican senators that attendees described as "eerily silent" and "uncomfortable."[3] Trump warned the party would "get killed" and be viewed as "do-nothing Republicans" if it did not change the Senate rules requiring 60 votes for most legislation, telling them during the televised portion: "If you don't terminate the filibuster, you'll be in bad shape," and going further after the press was asked to leave.[3] He dismissed a suggestion from Senator [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]] that Republicans again use the reconciliation process as they had with the "big, beautiful bill," and blamed the party's sweeping losses in the [[2025-off-year-elections|off-year elections]] on the record shutdown, insisting behind closed doors that it had hurt Republicans far more than Democrats — the opposite of what GOP leaders had argued for weeks.[3]

Badlands contributor [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] responded that, filibuster or not, he had a hard time believing that "elected" officials would be the ones to fix a fraudulent election system of which they are the primary beneficiaries.[3] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] remarked of Trump's posture that "it's like he knows our supposed 'constitutional order' is defunct and inactive."[3]
