# Colonial Borders in Africa

The colonial borders of Africa are the national boundaries drawn by departing European colonial powers, widely criticized for disregarding ethnic, cultural, and historical realities on the continent.[1]

## Lavrov's assessment

In an interview with *Kommersant* published Monday, April 14, 2025, Russian Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] said the legacy of colonialism remains a key driver of instability across Africa, attributing much of the continent's turmoil to those borders.[1] Lavrov argued that colonial map-making fragmented ethnic groups across newly formed national frontiers, producing "chaos," and said colonial authorities drew maps "with a ruler," dividing entire communities.[1]

He cited the Tuareg people, now split between Algeria and Mali, along with the Tutsi and Hutu of Rwanda, as examples.[1] Despite the destabilizing effects, Lavrov noted that the [[african-union|African Union]] has decided not to revise the borders: "The African Union, in its wisdom, decided that for now it is best to live within these borders, to find ways to reach agreements, and to build good-neighborly arrangements so that people – relatives – can cross borders freely."[1]

## Unfinished decolonization

Lavrov also pointed to the unfinished process of decolonization, stating that 17 territories worldwide remain under the control of former colonial powers.[1] A similar observation was made in 2019 by [[united-nations|UN]] Secretary-General [[antonio-guterres|Antonio Guterres]], who said decolonization "is one of the most significant chapters of the Organization's history. But this story is still being written, as 17 Non-Self-Governing Territories remain."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] said a glance at a modern map of Africa makes Lavrov's point obvious, with boundary lines that appear arbitrarily drawn.[1] He asked how many people had died over the past century to preserve "imaginary – and culturally meaningless – borders" so that oligarchs could retain control of regional resources for their own enrichment, and concluded that the colonial system was deeply evil — crediting the "woke" movement with getting that one point right while warning against a binary political reflex that rejects ideas out of spite.[1]

He cited Lt. Colonel [[oak-mcculloch|Oak McCulloch]]'s repeated observation, made at [[gart-nashville|GART]] events and on stream, that changing national boundaries on a map is a bloody endeavor, and asked whether a new incentive structure could allow the process to evolve organically by decoupling control of natural resources from national borders.[1]
