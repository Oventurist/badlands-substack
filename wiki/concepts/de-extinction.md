# De-Extinction

De-extinction is the term used by biotechnology firms and the press for efforts to recreate extinct animal species through genetic engineering, cloning, or selective editing of the genomes of living relatives.[1] The phrase entered widespread mainstream circulation in April 2025, when it was applied almost uniformly across legacy outlets to an announcement by [[colossal-biosciences|Colossal Biosciences]] concerning three engineered wolf pups said to resemble the extinct dire wolf.[1]

## The 2025 dire wolf case

Colossal Biosciences reported that the pups, aged three to six months, had long white hair and muscular jaws and weighed around 80 pounds, on track for roughly 140 pounds at maturity.[1] Dire wolves went extinct more than 10,000 years ago and were considerably larger than gray wolves, their nearest living relatives.[1]

Independent scientists disputed the de-extinction framing. Vincent Lynch, a biologist at the University at Buffalo, said that current techniques allow researchers only to "make something look superficially like something else" — not to fully revive a lost species.[1] On that account, de-extinction describes a cosmetic resemblance produced by targeted gene edits rather than the restoration of an extinct organism's full genome, behavior, and ecological niche.[1]

## Criticism as marketing language

Commentary in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] argued that de-extinction functions primarily as promotional vocabulary. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the Associated Press "threw shade" with a headline describing genetically engineered wolves rather than revived dire wolves, in contrast to peer outlets that repeated the de-extinction claim, and concluded that the concept is "just marketing" likely to "end badly."[1] The episode was cited as an example of press deference to corporate scientific claims, with the same institutions that demand the public "trust the science" splitting over whether the underlying claim was accurate.[1]
