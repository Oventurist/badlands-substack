# A Mysterious Masterclass in Multi Sensory Deception

"A Mysterious Masterclass in Multi Sensory Deception," subtitled "Unscheduled Shenanigans with a Purpose," is an essay published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on August 30, 2024, written by the contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe]]. Framed as a travelogue of a side trip taken during the fifth stop of the [[great-american-restoration-tour|Great American Restoration Tour]] (GART) in Deadwood, South Dakota, the piece uses a roadside "gravity hill" attraction, [[cosmos-mystery-area|the Cosmos Mystery Area]], as an extended metaphor for how propaganda and narrative manipulation operate in what the author calls the information war.

## The Deadwood GART stop

The article opens by describing GART 5, held in mid-August 2024 in Deadwood, South Dakota. The author characterizes GART as unlike a conventional conference — "a relatively small, accessible event, where Badlands hosts and our brilliant audience come together for a few days to get to know each other, laugh and learn, and experience the local area" — and says she would "prefer to call it a festival as opposed to a conference." Returning attendees, she writes, come to prize the unscheduled, "off-book" portions of the weekend.

Editor's notes embedded in the piece promote digital replay tickets for the Deadwood weekend, including the premiere of a Badlands Media documentary titled *The Crucible of Donald Trump*, and advertise GART 6 in Myrtle Beach, scheduled to take place just after the 2024 presidential election, for which VIP packages were already sold out.

## Unscheduled shenanigans

Arriving on a Wednesday night, the author recounts abandoning a plan to write in order to join a group heading to Mount Rushmore. The truck was driven by "Dan" and co-piloted by [[steven-kwast|General Kwast]], with the author riding alongside "Diane" and Joni, the general's wife. Conversation on the drive ranged over space, emerging technology, "free (sort of) energy," airplanes, parenthood and Badlands programming. At Mount Rushmore they attended the evening program, in which the stories of the presidents are told as the sun sets, the monument is illuminated, the national anthem is sung, and veterans in the audience descend to the stage to retire the U.S. flag. The author calls the evening "beautiful."

On the return drive the group discussed the Cosmos Mystery Area, which General Kwast said he already knew of, and agreed to visit it the following morning.

## Inside the Cosmos Mystery Area

The author, joined by the Badlands figure Kitsco along with Dan, Diane, Kwast and Joni, visited Cosmos on Thursday morning. Admission was $15 for a roughly 40-minute tour led by what she describes as "a bubbly teenage guide with quick jokes and innovative plugs for tips." The attraction's origin story, quoted in the article, holds that "in 1952, two college boys searched the Black Hills looking for the perfect spot to build a summer cabin," stumbled into "the mysterious world of the Cosmos," camped out to investigate the "odd phenomena," and eventually repaired the cabin to create the demonstrations shown to visitors.

The tour began with an optical illusion involving apparent height, using volunteers, which the author says was immediately effective at destabilizing perceptions of who was taller. A hike up the hill passed "sporadic gnarly messes of branches growing in illogical directions amid perfectly straight ponderosa pines," explained by the guide with the refrain, "It's the cosmic energy, man."

Approaching the cabin's fenced courtyard, the author writes, "your equilibrium fully distorts." Though her first thought was "it's the way this thing is built," the intellectual explanation "did nothing to stop the physiological reaction." A volunteer inspected a tennis ball for magnets or rigging; placed at the bottom of a ramp, it rolled upward, as did poured water. "Gravity works differently here," the guide said. Inside the cabin's two rooms, guardrails lined the walls and the guide repeatedly asked visitors whether they felt dizzy; hanging from a bar pulled the author's body toward the front of the cabin, an experience she calls "very uncomfortable," and a second room featured ramps running up two sides "like an old school 'mouse trap' game," with balls again rolling upward. On exiting, normal sensation returned, though the walk downhill still required the handrail.

## "Fake but still fascinating"

The article's analytic turn argues that Cosmos is "a perfect metaphor for navigating the info war." The deception, the author writes, "begins with a little bit of truth": genuine anomalies do exist in the Earth's magnetic field, including the South Atlantic Anomaly, described as the region where the Van Allen radiation belt comes closest to the Earth's surface and produces "an increased flux of energetic particles and a weaker magnetic field," as well as seafloor magnetic anomalies that helped scientists understand plate tectonics.

Because science acknowledges that such phenomena can occur, the article contends, non-scientists become more willing to accept the claim that one is occurring — a dynamic the author labels with the phrase "Trust the experts." The guide's repeated cosmic-energy and anti-gravity messaging then supplies authority and repetition, illustrated with a quotation attributed to [[william-james|William James]]: "There is nothing so absurd that it cannot be believed as truth if repeated often enough."

What makes the illusion powerful, the author argues, is that the body then cooperates through involuntary physiological responses: the eyes report that a sloped surface is flat, the brain accepts the report, and the body fights to align with a false vertical. "Your eyes are deceiving you."

## Spatial disorientation and flight training

After the tour, General Kwast described flight-school training in which instructors deliberately induce conditions where a pilot's senses mislead him, so that he learns to trust his instruments. With an obscured horizon, the article says, a young pilot "must fight his human senses and instincts telling him which way is up," trusting instruments that oppose his instincts "to the point of certain death."

The article quotes the [[federal-aviation-administration|FAA]]'s *Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge* defining [[spatial-disorientation|spatial disorientation]] as "a condition in which a pilot's perception of direction, altitude, or speed is misleading or incorrect, usually due to conflicting sensory information," occurring when a pilot "cannot accurately interpret their aircraft's position relative to the Earth." It notes that the fatal crashes of [[jfk-jr|John F. Kennedy Jr.]] and of the helicopter carrying [[kobe-bryant|Kobe Bryant]] were attributed to spatial disorientation.

Applying this to Cosmos, the author writes that the attraction's illusions are "largely the product of an obstructed horizon combined with a deceptively designed physical environment and intentional disinformation," since without a horizon a person cannot reliably judge the slope of a surface. She cites a 2003 study in which scientists recreated Cosmos-style sites and concluded that "antigravity-hill effects follow from a misperception of the eye level relative to gravity, caused by the presence of either contextual inclines or a false horizon line."

## "Trust your instruments"

The closing section extends the analogy. Asked what the instruments are in the [[information-war|information war]], Kwast answered, according to the article, that they are "the trust between each of us, and our ability to use our individual skills and knowledge, working together, to triangulate what's true." The author endorses the phrase "triangulate what's true."

She argues that audiences are "bombarded with 'explosive' stories and brazen emotional pleas, all of Hegel's best shots," most of which, like Cosmos, combine "a tiny bit of truth with a whole lot of deception," and that such narrative deployments affect individuals differently depending on circumstance and disposition.

Diane is quoted at length comparing the experience to political communication: "When we were at Cosmos, I was thinking about how every time [[barack-obama|Obama]] spoke, all I heard was, 'these aren't the droids you're looking for.' He constantly told us the opposite of what our senses were saying. Same with the MSM. They tell you things that your eyes and ears tell you are not true, but some people don't trust their inner instruments that keep us flying safe. Our inner instruments include our instincts and intuition, discernment and intelligence, along with the guidance from our Creator. Using these inner instruments, along with the triangulation with others who are awake and resisting the narrative, we will begin to more easily break through the deception we see and hear."

The article concludes that working together to separate facts from intended emotional reactions and "to triangulate the truth amid the noise" is a critical toolset for navigating and winning the information war, and notes that Badlands would return to Deadwood the following summer.
