# Brandenburg v. Ohio

**Brandenburg v. Ohio** (395 U.S. 444) is one of two seminal [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] cases of the latter half of the twentieth century cited in [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary on the [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|Colorado ballot disqualification case]], the other being [[naacp-v-claiborne-hardware|NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware]].[1] The decision is invoked for the proposition that political advocacy — including inflammatory advocacy — is constitutionally protected speech unless it crosses a narrow line into direct incitement.[1]

## Relevance to the January 6 speech

Writing on January 1, 2024, [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that in order to use the pretext that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] incited violence with his speech on January 6, 2021, a court "would have to completely trample the precedents of both Brandenburg and Claiborne."[1] He maintained that regardless of one's opinion of the content of Trump's remarks at the Ellipse — including his statements about the postponement of the election result and his promotion of nonviolent protest marches to the Capitol — it is indisputable that those words fall squarely within the First Amendment's protections for free speech as the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] has primarily defined them in those two cases and subsequent precedents.[1]

DeLarme treated the Brandenburg line of authority as the legal reason why the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]]'s reliance on an "insurrection" characterization of Trump's speech was untenable: absent a charge and conviction under the federal rebellion-or-insurrection statute at [[18-usc-2383|18 U.S.C. § 2383]], the speech remains protected expression rather than criminal incitement.[1]
