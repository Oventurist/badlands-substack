# 2025 Supreme Court Ruling on Alien Enemies Act Deportations

On Monday, April 7, 2025, a divided [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] cleared the way for the Trump administration to use the wartime [[alien-enemies-act|Alien Enemies Act]] (AEA) to deport alleged members of a Venezuelan gang to El Salvador, while holding that detainees must first have an opportunity to challenge their removals.[1] The 5–4 ruling vacated temporary restraining orders that had blocked the removals and became one of the most consequential immigration decisions of [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]]'s second term.[1]

## Holding

The majority did not resolve the underlying legal questions about the government's use of the AEA.[1] Instead, it ruled that the five Venezuelan immigrants who challenged the policy had sued in the wrong court: "The detainees are confined in Texas, so venue is improper in the District of Columbia," the justices wrote, adding that "As a result, the Government is likely to succeed on the merits of this action."[1] The decision left open the possibility that those targeted could refile in Texas or other jurisdictions where they were detained.[1]

The Court further held that challenges to removal under the AEA — a statute that largely "preclude[s] judicial review" — must be brought in habeas corpus, because "their claims fall within the core of the writ of habeas corpus."[1] The detainees had sought equitable relief against implementation of the presidential Proclamation and against removal under the AEA, and had challenged the government's interpretation of the Act, but the majority wrote: "we do not reach those arguments."[1] Rejecting the dissenters' preferred course, the majority added that starting the process anew later would be "wasteful delay" in which the Court saw "no benefit."[1]

In a concurrence, Justice [[brett-kavanaugh|Brett Kavanaugh]] wrote that in the extradition context and with respect to transfers of Guantanamo and other wartime detainees, "habeas corpus proceedings have long been the appropriate vehicle," and that the same general rule holds for claims under the Alien Enemies Act.[1]

## Procedural posture

The plaintiffs had initially sought relief in habeas among other causes of action but, as the majority opinion noted, "they dismissed their habeas claims" — voluntarily abandoning them in order to join a provisionally certified class action brought in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia under the [[administrative-procedure-act|Administrative Procedure Act]].[1] Commentary in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] argued that these particular detainees likely waived their habeas claims by dismissing them to join the class, leaving them to be tried individually in the districts where they were held.[1]

## Dissent

Justice [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]] wrote a 17-page dissent joined by Justices [[elena-kagan|Elena Kagan]] and [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]]; Justice [[amy-coney-barrett|Amy Coney Barrett]] joined two parts of it.[1] Sotomayor stressed that "The plaintiffs in this case sued not to challenge their detention, but to protect themselves from summary deportation pursuant to the Proclamation," noting that because all plaintiffs were already in immigration detention under other statutes, "they do not seek release from custody" and were not contesting the validity or duration of their confinement.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the opinion as most damaging to Judge [[james-boasberg|James Boasberg]] and to plans for judicial obstruction of executive action, characterizing Sotomayor's dissent as "a temper tantrum" and arguing that the president's national-security and foreign-policy powers under the AEA are not subject to judicial review.[1] She noted that Boasberg had a hearing scheduled the following day amid online speculation that he would hold the administration in contempt, and questioned whether a judge the Supreme Court had just found to lack jurisdiction could impose sanctions at all.[1]

[[canncon|CannCon]] offered a second take, arguing that leftist NGOs had been able to judge-shop and obtain standing in Washington, D.C. for plaintiffs held in Texas while voters were denied local forums for election challenges, concluding that "Our judicial system is either utterly compromised or broken beyond repair."[1]
