# Purcell Principle

The Purcell principle is a doctrine of United States election law, established in a 2006 Supreme Court ruling, that discourages federal courts from changing election rules in the period immediately preceding an election on the theory that late alterations breed "voter confusion."[1]

## 2024 Supreme Court consideration

In August 2024 the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] was asked to clarify how far judges may go in altering voting rules close to an election — a question with implications for legal disputes across the country weeks before the presidential vote.[1] The [[republican-national-committee|Republican National Committee]] asked the Court to reinstate an Arizona law requiring proof of citizenship for voter registration, a measure that had been blocked by an appeals court.[1] The request squarely challenged the roughly twenty-year-old precedent cautioning against last-minute federal court intervention.[1] The RNC sought a ruling by August 22, and the Court set a deadline for responses that Friday.[1]

## Criticism of indeterminacy

Because the justices have never supplied a detailed framework for applying Purcell, lower-court judges retain wide discretion in deciding when it is "too late" to implement changes before an election — a vagueness that has generated recurring accusations of partisan bias in judicial decisions.[1]

## Badlands analysis

Badlands commentary accepted the underlying logic of the doctrine, noting that barring changes to election processes or requirements when an election is imminent makes sense because such changes risk confusing the electorate and invite charges of election interference.[1] The principle was invoked prominently in 2020, when pandemic-related changes were largely rejected on imminence grounds.[1]

The larger objection advanced was not to imminence review itself but to what was described as a weaponisation of the litigation timeline. Quoting attorney Robert Barnes, the piece summarised the procedural trap facing election plaintiffs: "Not Ripe in Spring, No Standing by Summer, Laches by Fall, and Moot by Winter."[1] On this reading, the deeper problem is that election cases are nearly impossible to bring at any point in the calendar, not merely in the final weeks.[1]
