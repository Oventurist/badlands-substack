# War on Drugs

The **War on Drugs** is the decades-long United States campaign, framed publicly as a crusade against narcotics trafficking at home and abroad, in which billions of dollars have been directed into law enforcement, military operations, and international partnerships aimed at cartels, traffickers, and kingpins.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argues that the campaign is better understood not as a failed policy but as a deliberately engineered operation whose real purpose was to harness the economic and destructive power of narcotics rather than to eliminate them.[1]

## Framing and critique

In DeLarme's account, the public rhetoric of a "noble crusade" conceals a paradox: the same agencies charged with prosecuting the war — described as an "unholy trinity" of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]], the [[state-department|State Department]], and the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] — have at various times exploited drug trafficking to finance covert agendas.[1] He stresses that the claims are not confined to fringe commentary but appear in congressional reports, declassified documents, and firsthand accounts, producing a record in which "the lines between enforcer and enabler blur."[1]

Because the campaign has run continuously for more than half a century, DeLarme notes that if treated as an actual war it would be the longest in American history — the ultimate "endless war," rumoured to have underwritten black operations, special access programs (SAPs), and paramilitary ventures.[1]

## Domestic dimension

Domestically, the war is associated with the growth of a [[prison-industrial-complex|prison industrial complex]] that became a multi-billion-dollar annual industry, with the article pointing readers to the published revenue reports of the private prison operators CoreCivic and the GEO Group.[1] The [[pentagon|Pentagon]]'s domestic surveillance and militarised interdiction efforts expanded steadily from the 1970s onward, yet critics observe that drug flows persisted despite the resources committed, which DeLarme reads as evidence of a perpetual "war" maintained to justify budgets and control.[1]

Generational familiarity with scheduled substances is, in the article's framing, itself a product of the campaign: Gen-Z and Millennial students were taught to identify narcotics in D.A.R.E. classes across the country.[1]

## Overseas pattern

The article traces a historical pattern of drug tolerance in service of geopolitical goals running from [[operation-underworld|Operation Underworld]] and the [[office-of-strategic-services|OSS]] during the Second World War, through the [[french-connection|French Connection]] and the [[golden-triangle|Golden Triangle]], to Laos, [[operation-cyclone|Operation Cyclone]] in [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]], and the Nicaraguan [[nicaraguan-contras|Contras]] in Central America.[1] DeLarme's conclusion is that the war was never lost: "The War on Drugs isn't lost—it's won," with coups and proxies abroad and prisons and gutted communities at home.[1]

## Nixon, Reagan and the British thesis

In the account given in [[dope-inc-book|*Dope, Inc.*]] and summarized by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]], the American drug epidemic of the late 1960s was produced deliberately: with an organized crime syndicate in place from [[prohibition|Prohibition]], British drug traffickers flooded America with illegal drugs through the 1960s.[2] [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]]'s response — declaring a war on drugs — is presented as the reason he was driven from office, since attacking the drug infrastructure meant taking on the British oligarchy, the [[eurodollar|Eurodollar]] market and the People's Republic of China.[2]

Carlson notes that [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]] likewise declared a war on drugs and likewise faced an attempt to remove him from office, in his case an assassination attempt by a shooter with close ties to the family of [[george-hw-bush|George Bush Sr.]], then Vice President and a former Director of the [[cia|CIA]].[2]
