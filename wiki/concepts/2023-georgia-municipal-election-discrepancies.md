# 2023 Georgia Municipal Election Discrepancies

The **2023 Georgia municipal election discrepancies** refers to a cluster of vote-count irregularities reported after the November 7, 2023 coordinated municipal elections in the U.S. state of Georgia, most prominently in [[spalding-county|Spalding County]] and [[fulton-county|Fulton County]]. In both counties the reported anomalies were confined to *advance* (early) voting ballots rather than to Election Day or absentee tabulations, a pattern that critics argued undercut official assurances that Georgia's voting machines had performed without error.[1]

## Spalding County

Spalding County, one of several Georgia counties that require a post-election hand count, conducted the mandated hand count of the November 7 municipal results in the week following the election. According to sources observing the count, the hand-count totals failed to match the machine totals in every race across every precinct.[1] Anecdotal reports from observers during the advance-vote hand count described hundreds or more apparent ballot inaccuracies.[1]

The certified outcome was far less dramatic than the initial reports: the final results reflected a variance of nine votes, netting out to a five-vote effect on the outcomes.[1] On the Tuesday following the count the county election board voted 2–1 to certify, over a push to delay certification until the root cause of the mismatches could be identified. The lone vote against certification was cast by Board of Elections Secretary [[roy-mcclain|Roy McClain]].[1] Commentators observed that in most races — the ballot measure excepted — the machine count *added* votes relative to the hand count, and that although the variances were immaterial to the outcomes, they did nothing to resolve the machine-manipulation concerns that had prompted the hand count in the first place.[1]

## Fulton County

Fulton County reported multiple problems with the November 7 election, including voters in East Point and Atlanta being issued incorrect ballots, and difficulties in East Point and South Fulton with uploading voter information into the state's electronic registration system.[1] The county announced a recount, stating that it "will be held to confirm the accuracy of the election results before election certification."[1]

On the Tuesday morning scheduled for certification, the county delayed the vote after three successive counts produced irreconcilable totals. In an email to Fulton County Commissioners, [[patrise-perkins-hooker|Patrise Perkins-Hooker]] of the county's "Administrative Partner" law firm [[johnson-and-freeman|Johnson & Freeman, LLC]] wrote that a discrepancy had been discovered between the number of advance voting ballots initially counted and the number reported during the recount, that staff suspected a box of advance voting ballots had not been scanned, and that a retabulation of all advance voting ballots would prevent the Board of Registration and Elections from certifying at 11 a.m.[1]

Sources witnessing the recounts, speaking anonymously out of fear of retaliation, said the successive counts *decreased* rather than increased: 13,705 on the first count, 13,690 on the second, and 13,330 on the Tuesday morning count that triggered the delay — a loss of 375 votes from the first total and 360 from the second.[1] Critics noted the internal contradiction: if a missed box of ballots had been located and scanned, totals would be expected to rise, not fall.[1] [[just-the-news|Just the News]] reported the story that afternoon, quoting Perkins-Hooker as saying the recount would not delay certification, that the discrepancy "was only 300 ballots off," and that the error resulted from a group of ballots in one box not being scanned.[1] Fulton County ultimately certified late that Tuesday without publishing final numbers to the county elections website.[1]

## Official response and criticism

Georgia officials and allied groups attributed the variances to human error. Kristin Nabers, Georgia state director of [[all-voting-is-local|All Voting Is Local]], who observed the counting, told the [[atlanta-journal-constitution|Atlanta Journal-Constitution]] that she saw hand counts "as part of a push by bad-faith actors to spread lies and conspiracy theories," adding that discrepancies were to be expected from human error but that any discrepancy could be used to cast doubt on the counting process.[1] The AJC had separately reported that there were "no signs of miscounts or voting machine errors in Spalding County," an assertion published the same day the outlet reported that all Fulton elections were headed for a recount.[1]

Critics drew the opposite conclusion. Holly Kesler, Georgia state director for [[citizens-defending-freedom|Citizens Defending Freedom]], said the 2023 results proved that "Georgia can no longer afford to use voting machines."[1] Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that three counts producing three different totals would not be dismissed in any other industry or audit practice, and that transparency, not dismissive speculation, is what sustains public trust — citing polling showing more than 68% of voters most concerned about public corruption and more than 57% distrusting U.S. elections.[1]

## Related litigation and context

The tabulation devices at issue are used statewide, including in Fulton and Spalding counties, and are the subject of [[curling-v-raffensperger|*Curling et al v. Raffensperger et al*]], the long-running federal case in which Judge [[amy-totenberg|Amy Totenberg]] set trial for January 9, 2024.[1] Vulnerability findings by [[j-alex-halderman|J. Alex Halderman]], released in June 2023 after being sealed since July 2021, had not been patched ahead of the 2024 election.[1] The discrepancies also unfolded in the county where District Attorney [[fani-willis|Fani Willis]] was prosecuting [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and co-defendants over their refusal to accept the 2020 Georgia result.[1]
