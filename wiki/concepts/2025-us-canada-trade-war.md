# 2025 US–Canada Trade War

The 2025 US–Canada trade war refers to the escalating exchange of tariffs and threats between the second [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration and Canadian federal and provincial governments during early 2025.[1]

## Escalation

Trump pledged to "just get it all back" with stiffer reciprocal tariffs the following month as Ontario Premier [[doug-ford|Doug Ford]] threatened to cut off electricity to American states.[1] Ford imposed a 25% tariff on Canadian electricity exported to Michigan, New York and Minnesota, and said he was prepared to "shut the electricity off completely" if the United States continued to "escalate."[1]

Trump mocked the surcharge on Truth Social, writing that "Canada is charging the USA from 250% to 390% Tariffs on many of our farm products" while "Ontario just announced a 25% surcharge on 'electricity,' of all things, and you're not even allowed to do that."[1] He said the United States would recover everything on April 2, when the administration's reciprocal tariff plan was scheduled to take effect, and declared that "Canada is a Tariff abuser, and always has been, but the United States is not going to be subsidizing Canada any longer."[1] Ford told Fox Business he would relent only when Trump stopped threatening his allies.[1]

## Political context

The dispute unfolded alongside a leadership transition in Ottawa, where [[mark-carney|Mark Carney]] won the Liberal Party leadership to succeed [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]] as prime minister amid what the New York Times described as a generational crisis in Canada's relationship with the United States.[1] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] observed that a week after Trump asked why Trudeau was still speaking for the Canadian people, Carney — "a literal central banker being forced to adopt nationalist rhetoric" — was succeeding him, concluding: "Trump plays the tunes. They dance."[1]

## Contrast with Mexico

The Canadian confrontation contrasted with the Mexican response: tens of thousands of people packed Mexico City's main plaza to celebrate with President [[claudia-sheinbaum|Claudia Sheinbaum]] after Washington postponed 25% tariffs on many Mexican goods for a month, citing progress on drug smuggling and illegal immigration.[1] Sheinbaum, who had planned to announce retaliatory measures, instead held a mass celebration, telling the crowd that "dialogue and respect have prevailed" and saying she would continue to act with a "cool head."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that she was "old enough to remember when the expert class told us that tariffs would only hurt America," yet in the midst of a trade war the people of Mexico threw a party over a tariff delay.[1] She dismissed Canada's posture as "an unserious flex, executed through emotionally blackmailing 47 for continued subsidies," argued that Americans were largely oblivious to the trade war because the United States does the subsidizing and can source domestic lumber and electricity, and said Ford's complaint about threatening allies only reinforced Trump's view of allies as "entitled freeloaders that hate us and enable our enemies."[1] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] added that "Trump is like a cat owner with a laser pen and the global Regime the cat."[1]

## March 2025 tariff onset

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] enacted sweeping 25 percent tariffs on Mexico and Canada in the first days of March 2025, and defended them the same week in his [[2025-trump-address-to-joint-session-of-congress|address to a joint session of Congress]], saying he was "fighting every day" to lower prices despite the onset of a trade war and promising to tariff other countries at whatever rate they tariff the United States.[2] Reporting at the time framed the tariffs as a stress test for two economies that had bet heavily on free trade under the [[usmca|US–Mexico–Canada Agreement]], with more than 80% of both countries' exports going to the United States.[2]

## March 2025 tariff implementation and the Trudeau call

After previously delaying the 25 percent tariffs on Canada and Mexico by a month as the result of negotiations with Prime Minister [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]] and Mexican President [[claudia-sheinbaum|Claudia Sheinbaum]] — during which both countries agreed to implement additional border security measures — President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said on Monday, March 3, 2025, that there was no more room for dealmaking, and the tariffs took effect at midnight.[3] On Wednesday, March 5, Trudeau called Trump to ask what could be done about the tariffs; Trump responded that many people had died from fentanyl that came through the borders of Canada and Mexico and that nothing had convinced him it had stopped, dismissing Trudeau's claim that it had "gotten better" as "not good enough."[3]

Trump said afterward on Truth Social that the call ended in a "somewhat" friendly manner but that Trudeau's inability to say when the Canadian election would take place led him to conclude the prime minister was "trying to use this issue to stay in power."[3] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode as part of "the inversion of the globalist west," arguing Trump's refusal to deal reflected a judgment that Trudeau was an illegitimate leader rather than a political rivalry.[3]

## March 11, 2025: steel tariff threat and market turmoil

On March 11, 2025, global stock markets were plunged into chaos after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] threatened to double tariffs on imports of steel and aluminium from Canada, with America's S&P 500 falling by as much as 1.5 percent before rebounding later in the day.[4] Markets whipsawed after a string of surprise tariff announcements from both Washington and Ottawa, as investors responded to Ontario's decision to backtrack on its surcharge on power sales to the United States.[4] Trump had vowed to impose 50 percent tariffs on Canadian metals from the Wednesday, but the White House abandoned the plan hours before it was due to be implemented.[4]

The erratic announcements drove the "fear index" of market volatility to highs not seen since the previous summer's recession scare, while the UK's FTSE 100 fell a further 1.2 percent — down 4.2 percent since the start of the prior week — and the dollar fell to its lowest level since Trump's November election victory.[4] Trump called Ontario's proposed electricity surcharge an "abusive threat," marking an escalation in the tit-for-tat spat with a country traditionally among America's closest allies; the episode was reported by [[the-telegraph|The Telegraph]].[4]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that after Trump raised 50 percent steel tariffs in retaliation, Ontario Premier [[doug-ford|Doug Ford]] "immediately changed course," arguing that the United States subsidizes Canada and is "holding all the cards," and that Mexico had proven "much smarter than Canada."[4] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described a broader "Capitulation pattern" among collectivists worldwide, contending that in order to combat Trump's rhetoric opponents were being forced to adopt it — donning "nationalist plumage" to fight nationalism and disguising globalism as sovereignty — an untenable narrative position as the actuals diverged from the rhetoric.[4]
