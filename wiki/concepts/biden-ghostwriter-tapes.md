# Biden Ghostwriter Tapes

The Biden ghostwriter tapes are more than 70 hours of recorded 2016 and 2017 conversations between [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and memoir ghostwriter [[mark-zwonitzer|Mark Zwonitzer]], obtained during special counsel [[robert-hur|Robert Hur]]'s classified documents probe and published in July 2026 by the [[oversight-project|Oversight Project]] after a lengthy legal fight.[1]

## Content

On the recordings Biden warns at one point that "some of this may be classified" and later states flatly, "this is classified."[1] One exchange has Biden telling Zwonitzer: "Some of this may be classified … So, be careful. I'm not sure … It's not marked classified but —"; at another point he says, "The next thing I have here is, um, this is classified …"[1] The tapes also show Biden frequently pausing, speaking haltingly, and struggling to recall events — details that align with Hur's earlier description of Biden's memory.[1]

## Litigation and reaction

Biden's attorneys spent months attempting to block release before abandoning their lawsuit, and a judge accepted the dismissal on a Monday.[1] Oversight Project Vice President of Litigation [[jeff-clark|Jeff Clark]] said the recordings should force a full reexamination of the decision to give Biden a pass for keeping and disclosing classified information.[1]

## Biden's May 2026 suit against DOJ

On May 27, 2026, Joe Biden sued the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] to block the release of audio recordings and transcripts from the private interviews with Zwonitzer, arguing that the Trump DOJ had reversed earlier FOIA privacy protections for political purposes.[2] The materials, recorded in 2016 and 2017 for Biden's memoir *Promise Me, Dad*, had been obtained during Hur's classified-documents investigation, and DOJ planned to provide redacted versions to the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] and the [[heritage-foundation|Heritage Foundation]] by June 15.[2]

Hur had declined to charge Biden in 2024, saying the evidence did not meet the standard for prosecution, though his report stated that Biden had retained classified materials and raised questions about his memory.[2] Biden's suit argued that release would expose sensitive personal law-enforcement material, contending that "every American, including a sitting or former Vice President, has a right to privacy in the personal conversations he has within his own home," and that when DOJ obtains such information through a criminal investigation it "bears a particular responsibility to protect it from disclosure."[2] DOJ countered that the public should be able to hear the recordings and draw its own conclusions.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described herself as of two minds: the tapes would be entertaining and the Hur findings point to intentional cover-ups of the decline of the "so-called ruler of the United States" at a time when American rights were being undermined, giving the public a claim to hear the audio; yet the privacy principle invoked in the lawsuit is important, and the test of whether one truly believes in rights is whether one is principled about them when applied to enemies.[2]
