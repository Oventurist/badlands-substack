# Swatting

Swatting refers to the practice of making false emergency calls targeting a person's residence, typically in order to provoke an armed law-enforcement response against an unsuspecting occupant.[1] The term entered mainstream U.S. political coverage as a form of intimidation directed at public figures.

## Use against Trump administration nominees (November 2024)

In the week of Thanksgiving 2024, multiple nominees and appointees tapped to serve in President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s incoming administration were targeted with threats, with at least four officials reporting bomb threats at their homes.[1] [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]], designated [[white-house|White House]] press secretary, said "several" of Trump's Cabinet picks were the subject of bomb threats and swatting.[1]

"In response, law enforcement and other authorities acted quickly to ensure the safety of those who were targeted," Leavitt said in a statement online, adding that Trump and the entire transition team were "grateful for their swift action" and that "dangerous acts of intimidation and violence will not deter us."[1]

The [[fbi|FBI]] later confirmed it was aware of "numerous bomb threats and swatting incidents targeting incoming administration nominees and appointees" and said it was "working with our law enforcement partners," encouraging the public to report anything suspicious.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], argued that the episode should not be described as mere "threats of political violence" but as terror — an effort to frighten Trump's staff and the broader public into believing that their leaders would be killed.[1] She observed that the long "war on terror" had left Americans largely apathetic to such threats, and noted that the incidents were first reported by the Trump Transition Team, calling them "a sobering reminder that rabid animals are most dangerous when they're cornered."[1]

She further contended that it is not credible that law enforcement, the Secret Service or related agencies cannot trace such threats, since every communication leaves a digital footprint: "If they don't identify who is making threats against incoming cabinet members, it's because they don't want to — not because they can't."[1] Whether the perpetrators are identified, she suggested, would indicate whether the country remained in the pattern set by the unsolved January 6 pipe bombs or had entered a phase of justice and transparency.[1]
