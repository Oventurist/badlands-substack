# Stablecoin

A **stablecoin** is a digital token designed to hold a fixed value — typically one U.S. dollar — by holding reserve assets against each coin issued. Stablecoins became the subject of a dedicated U.S. regulatory framework under the [[genius-act|GENIUS Act]], which requires issuers to back their coins with secure, short-term assets such as U.S. Treasuries.[1]

## Regulatory treatment

Under the framework described in coverage of the legislation, the permissible reserve set is deliberately narrow and issuers are barred from paying interest to holders.[1] Supporters expect regulatory clarity to legitimize the sector and widen adoption; critics counter that the ban on interest payments leaves stablecoins at a competitive disadvantage against bank deposits and money market funds, which can pay yield.[1]

## Strategic interpretations

Independent commentators in the Badlands orbit have framed regulated U.S. stablecoins as a structural threat to the commercial banking model rather than a niche crypto product. Burning Bright argued that dollar stablecoins would displace bank intermediation, serve as a backstop for a "new" dollar, and accelerate capital flows into bitcoin.[1] The Treasury-backed reserve requirement is read in this analysis as a mechanism that channels stablecoin growth directly into demand for U.S. government debt.[1]

## Congressional debate (May 2025)

A U.S. bill to regulate stablecoin issuers, described as formally introducing crypto into the established financial system, was scheduled for congressional debate in the week of May 19, 2025, with some observers predicting it would pass the Senate.[2] Its progress coincided with a [[bitcoin|bitcoin]] rally to $106,000.[2]

## Stablecoins and US debt markets

By late 2025 stablecoins had become central to a paradox in global finance: the US government, in trying to stabilize its debt markets, legitimized the very free-market forces eroding its monopoly over money.[3] The [[genius-act|GENIUS Act]], enacted in mid-2025, gave official legitimacy to an already booming industry of privately issued, fully backed digital dollars moving across open blockchain networks rather than through the regulated banking system, and in doing so turned stablecoin issuers into a new and dependable class of buyers of US government debt.[3] With foreign central banks cutting their Treasury holdings and the American fiscal burden swelling, this private demand became a genuine lifeline.[3]

Analysis from the Mises Institute carried by [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] noted that both principal Federal Reserve tools — setting reserves and setting interest rates — rely on the assumption that most dollars circulate inside the regulated banking system, and that stablecoins quietly break that link: each token represents real assets, Treasuries or cash, but moves on networks operating outside the Fed's direct reach.[3] Badlands contributor [[gmoney|GMONEY]] summarized the development by writing that "fractional reserve parasites are panicking."[3]
