# Three Ordeals

The "Three Ordeals" are, according to "The Men Behind the Curtain - Part 8," the criteria beyond family ties by which candidates are assessed for initiation into Yale's [[skull-and-bones|Skull and Bones]] society.[1] They are said to be intended to measure a prospective Bonesman's ability to "make it" in the world beyond the university campus, and to mark the beginning of his psychological conditioning.[1]

## The ordeals

The first is **boarding school**: removal from the family unit and placement in a loveless environment where the candidate is conditioned into obedience through fear of physical abuse in older generations, or of social repercussion in more recent ones.[1]

The second is **trial by nature**: in earlier days, Bonesmen who had taken an eye to a potential initiate would abduct him and leave him in the wilderness outside New Haven to test his will and survival ability, adding trauma while gauging resolve; more affluent initiates could satisfy the requirement with simple hunting excursions.[1]

The third is **war**. Combat experience is considered of special significance for Bonesmen, who have traditionally seen themselves as the elite of the New England WASP warrior caste.[1] Many Yale Bonesmen of [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]]'s generation went directly from prep school into military service before entering Yale because of the outbreak of the Second World War, with the U.S. Navy — and during that war the naval air corps in particular — the preferred branch.[1] In peacetime, participation in officers' training at Yale is desirable but not essential, and a commitment to enter some branch of the military upon graduation is viewed with favor.[1] The article observes that death and war are recurring themes in the society's culture.[1]

## Tapping and initiation

Selection proper is conducted through a ritual called "tapping," carried out by the fifteen senior classmen who make up the current membership; they choose fifteen juniors to be the following year's Bonesmen.[1] A group of Bonesmen goes to the tappee's dormitory room and pounds loudly on the door; when he opens it, a member taps him on the shoulder and shouts, "Skull and Bones: Do you accept?"[1] If the candidate accepts, he is handed a message wrapped with a black ribbon and sealed with black wax bearing the skull and crossbones emblem and the number 322, appointing a time and place to appear on initiation night, with instructions to wear no metal objects or clothing.[1]

A 1940 society document describes the ceremony itself: "New man placed in coffin—carried into central part of building. New man chanted over and reborn into society. Removed from coffin and given robes with symbols on it. A bone with his name on it is tossed into the bone heap at the start of every meeting."[1]

## Purpose

Ryan DeLarme argues that the apparent frivolity of these rites conceals a serious psychological purpose: the same psychology that keeps a cult in line is employed in the fraternal organizations that mold deep state players.[1] He contends that elite circles have long studied what can be done to the human psyche to elicit particular results, applying such tactics to condition the neophyte to place the machinations of the Order above even family and God, making him colder, more calculating and better able to compartmentalize natural emotions.[1] The author recommends James Randall Noblit's *Cult and Ritual Abuse* and [[alison-miller|Alison Miller]]'s *Healing the Unimaginable* for further reading on the conditioning involved.[1]
