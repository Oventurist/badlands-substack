# Stockholm Syndrome (political usage)

Stockholm Syndrome — the phenomenon in which a captive comes to empathize with and defend those holding them — is used by Erik Carlson in "America's Greatest Threat" as the organizing metaphor for the relationship between the American public and the American government.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) He argues that Americans in general suffer from the condition, defending the very apparatus that constrains them.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

## The abused-spouse analogy

Carlson opens the argument with an image of an abused wife who fights the police as they attempt to remove her abusive husband — throwing punches with her uninjured arm while the other remains broken from the abuse — and who afterward makes excuses for the treatment she received and scrapes together bail money.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) Americans, in his account, behave the same way toward their government.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

## Voting for control

The essay contends that Americans vote for the people who want more control over them, regarding that control as both necessary and good for the nation.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) Those who question the controllers, the level of control, or the means of control are ostracized, ridiculed, fined, jailed, killed, or censored — removed from society and from positions of power and influence — because dissent cannot be tolerated and accountability cannot be permitted.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) Carlson cites the fact that the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] has never once been audited in more than a century of existence as an example of this immunity from scrutiny.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

## Captives who inform on each other

Extending the analogy, Carlson notes that in genuine Stockholm Syndrome the prisoners still fear their captors but nonetheless empathize with them and inform on fellow prisoners planning escape.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) He applies this to the [[covid-19-lockdowns|2020 lockdown period]]: business owners fired loyal and productive employees over vaccination status, Americans administered the injections, and many insisted the unvaccinated should be excluded from society altogether — the government having brainwashed Americans into doing its work for it.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)

## Cultural framing

Alongside the clinical metaphor, the essay argues that discernment and self-awareness among Americans have reached unprecedented lows while self-importance and entitlement have never been higher, despite universal access to information.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat) Carlson also invokes the saying that the greatest lie the Devil ever told was that he did not exist, asserting that the American government has surpassed even that deception by persuading citizens to surrender most of their income for protection against foreign enemies — many of which, he claims, the American government and its agencies created.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/americas-greatest-threat)
