# California Proposition 50

California Proposition 50 was a legislatively approved constitutional amendment placed before voters at the November 4, 2025 statewide special election, proposing temporary redistricting of California's congressional districts.[1]

## Provisions

Proposition 50 would allow temporary congressional district maps in California starting in 2026.[1] According to the measure's text, the temporary maps were intended to respond to partisan redistricting efforts in Republican-led states, including Texas, Florida and Ohio, with the goal of ensuring fair representation for California communities.[1]

A "Yes" vote would implement the new legislative maps for the next election cycle; a "No" vote would maintain the current maps drawn by the [[citizens-redistricting-commission|Citizens Redistricting Commission]] until the 2030 Census prompts new lines.[1] Voters across the state had until Monday, October 20, 2025 to register for the special election.[1]

## Supporters

Supporters, including Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]], described the measure as a way to counter [[mid-decade-redistricting|mid-decade redistricting]] efforts elsewhere that could expand Republican control in Congress.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] used the measure to raise a broader question — "If elections are real, why would the powers allow them?" — arguing on [[devolution-power-hour|Devolution Power Hour]] Ep. 397 that there is no reason to believe voting machines are credible or produce accurate results, that a machine-printed barcode is not a paper ballot, that it is possible elections stop altogether as the country recognizes it is something other than what it has been told, and that great powers do not hand control of the most powerful nation on earth to their enemies because people voted for it.[1] He allowed that technology such as blockchain or Bitcoin verification might be part of the solution and should not be rejected merely because machines have been abused.[1]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the measure as a potential "inverted rerun" of [[texas-v-pennsylvania|Texas v. Pennsylvania]], in which the Supreme Court held that Texas had not demonstrated a judicially cognizable interest in the manner in which another state conducts its elections.[1] She argued California was demonstrating precisely such a cognizable situation by openly admitting questionable intentions for redrawing congressional districts before the midterms — and just after large numbers of migrants were removed from the state — and suggested Texas, along with voters, candidates and citizens, should sue.[1] Noting that California holds the most electoral votes in the country and that Newsom "said the manipulation bit publicly," she asked what a plan to restore real elections would look like, and what it would look like if Newsom lost the ability to manipulate elections.[1]
