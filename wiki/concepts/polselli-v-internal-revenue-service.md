# Polselli v. Internal Revenue Service

Polselli v. Internal Revenue Service is a 2023 decision of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|United States Supreme Court]] holding that the [[internal-revenue-service|Internal Revenue Service]] need not notify innocent bank account holders when it issues summonses for their records in aid of collecting a delinquent taxpayer's assessment.[1] The ruling resolved a split among the federal circuits.[1]

## Legal question

Under Internal Revenue Code § 7609, the IRS must generally give notice to third parties when issuing summonses for their records, but the statute contains exceptions on the theory that notice can defeat a summons by giving a taxpayer the chance to move assets beyond reach.[1] The exception at issue, § 7609(c)(2)(D)(i), permits the IRS to withhold notice when three conditions are met: the third-party summons must be issued "in aid of collection"; an assessment or judgment against the delinquent taxpayer must exist; and both the assessment or judgment and the collection activity must pertain to the same taxpayer.[1]

## Decision

The Court ruled unanimously in an opinion written by Chief Justice [[john-roberts|John Roberts]], concluding that the notice requirement does not apply in these circumstances.[1] Although the outcome was a win for the IRS, the Court expressed apprehension about the scope of the agency's summons power and declined to define what "in aid of the collection" of an assessment or judgment means — a limitation that could constrain the IRS in future cases.[1] The case was reported in the banking trade press by the [[american-bankers-association|American Bankers Association]]'s ABA Banking Journal.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] characterized the ruling as overreach and an assault on due process and the reasonable expectation of privacy, arguing that a 9–0 decision authored by the Chief Justice hands the IRS a loophole that will fall hardest on small business owners and the self-employed while wealthy individuals and corporations that bend or violate tax law escape comparable scrutiny.[1] The commentary questioned the practical value of a conservative majority on the Court and framed the outcome within a broader complaint that federal taxation has never been properly apportioned.[1]
