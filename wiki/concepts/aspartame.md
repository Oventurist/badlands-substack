# Aspartame

Aspartame is an artificial sweetener that Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] identified as the most prevalent and "possibly the most damaging" of at least five artificial sweeteners described as neurotoxic, carcinogenic, and/or allergenic — a group that also includes sucralose, acesulfame, saccharin, and neotame.[1] The article presented aspartame as a case study in how corporate money, regulatory capture, and search-engine curation combine to produce "two separate realities" around a single controversial ingredient: one in which large corporations, medical institutes, and the outlets they fund dismiss all negative claims, and another in which practicing and former physicians together with former [[environmental-protection-agency|EPA]] scientists attempt to raise alarms.[1]

## Chemistry and exposure

The article classifies aspartame as an excitotoxin — a substance that overexcites cells to the point of damage or death — placing it in the same category as [[monosodium-glutamate|MSG]].[1] It is described as commonly present in diet and zero-sugar sodas, gelatin desserts, sugar-free gum, drink mixes, sugar-free sports drinks, baking goods, tabletop sweeteners such as NutraSweet, Kool-Aid, iced tea, chewable vitamins, breath mints, toothpaste, and mouthwash.[1] The article states that aspartame was listed as a potential biochemical weapon by the [[pentagon|Pentagon]].[1]

Methanol exposure is a central concern. In the peer-reviewed paper *Aspartame: Methanol and the Public Health*, Dr. Woodrow Monte wrote that when aspartame-sweetened diet sodas and soft drinks are used to replace fluid loss during exercise and exertion in hot climates, methanol intake "can exceed 250 mg/day, or 32 times the Environmental Protection Agency's recommended limit of consumption for this cumulative poison."[1]

## Adverse-event record

The article reports that the effects of aspartame are documented in the [[food-and-drug-administration|FDA]]'s own data: in 1995 the agency was compelled under the Freedom of Information Act to release a list of aspartame symptoms reported by thousands of consumers.[1] From roughly 10,000 consumer complaints, the FDA compiled a list of 92 symptoms, including death.[1]

## Approval history and the Searle controversy

A 1980 FDA Board of Inquiry composed of three independent scientists concluded that aspartame "might induce brain tumors," and the FDA had previously banned the sweetener on that basis.[1] According to the article, that posture changed after [[donald-rumsfeld|Donald Rumsfeld]] — described as an establishment Republican and American businessman — vowed while serving as president of [[gd-searle|G.D. Searle]], the company that originally held the aspartame patent, that he would "call in his markers" to secure approval.[1] [[monsanto|Monsanto]] subsequently bought out Searle and, in the article's assessment, was untroubled by aspartame's clouded regulatory history.[1]

DeLarme characterized the material presented as barely scratching the surface of the dangers and history associated with the sweetener, and urged readers to cross-reference claims independently rather than relying on well-funded fact-checking outlets.[1]
