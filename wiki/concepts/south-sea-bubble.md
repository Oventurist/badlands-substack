# South Sea Bubble

The South Sea Bubble is presented in the Badlands Media essay "In Defense of Benjamin Franklin" as the financial expression of [[bernard-mandeville|Bernard Mandeville]]'s deregulatory philosophy in early eighteenth-century England.[1]

Mandeville's thesis, the essay argues, was applied to deregulate the English economy, which soon came to be driven by the South Sea Bubble — an episode premised upon rampant speculation the author says is not qualitatively different from the bubbles that transformed modern economies from industrial powerhouses into speculative globalized systems.[1]

When the Bubble burst in 1720, it resulted in a vast wealth transfer and a consolidation of power into the hands of an elite dominated by the [[hellfire-club|Hell-Fire Club]], which the essay describes as intent on turning England into the throne of a new global Venetian Empire.[1] Quoting [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]], the article states that 1720 was "the year of the Bubble and an unrestrained Venetian rampage against Britain," in which the Hell-Fire clubs played a major part in the intended shock treatment.[1] The most prominent such club was founded that year by the new Lord Wharton, later elevated to duke, whose dining menu included "Hell Fire Punch," "Holy Ghost Pie," "Devil's Loins," and "Breast of Venus."[1] Even in London such open Satan-worship was ahead of its time, and in 1721 a King's Order-in-Council banned the Hell-Fire clubs, at least in such public forms.[1]
