# Horizontal Shareholding

Horizontal shareholding is the practice, arising largely as a consequence of [[passive-investing|passive investing]], by which a small number of very large asset managers hold simultaneous "cross-holdings" in all of the leading firms within a single industry.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] described it as the leading contributor to the American economy's monopoly problem, arguing that [[blackrock|BlackRock, Inc.]] and [[vanguard-group|the Vanguard Group]] are its principal vectors.[1]

## Mechanism

In a healthy competition-based economy, the largest companies in a single industry should still compete head-to-head on the price and quality of their products.[1] Where the same institutional shareholders own commanding stakes in every major competitor, that incentive erodes: the common owners profit regardless of which firm wins market share, so aggressive price competition merely transfers value between portfolio companies rather than creating it.[1] The article characterizes the resulting effect as the erosion of competition itself, citing legal and academic literature on how horizontal shareholding harms the economy and why antitrust law might fix it.[1]

## The airline case study

The airline industry is offered as the clearest documented example.[1] When two airlines genuinely compete, they tend to lower prices on contested routes to draw in more customers; once horizontal shareholding becomes the norm, there is no need to lower ticket prices, because the shareholders win whichever carrier the customer chooses.[1] DeLarme argues the consequences extend beyond fares to the whole character of the firm, most notably service quality — so lost bags, shoddy service and high fares can all be traced back in part to the common-ownership structure.[1]

## Consumer-facing illustration

The article illustrates the reach of the pattern with an everyday shopping example: a consumer in Western Michigan choosing between Minute Maid orange juice, owned by [[coca-cola|The Coca-Cola Company]], and Tropicana, largely owned by [[pepsico|PepsiCo]], is choosing between two firms whose top institutional holders are the same two asset managers — as is the supermarket chain FamilyFare, owned by [[spartannash|SpartanNash]].[1] DeLarme extends the point to the great consumer rivalries of the late twentieth century — Coke versus Pepsi, Rite Aid versus Walgreens, CNN versus Fox, Ford versus Chevrolet — arguing that each is now essentially a facade because BlackRock and Vanguard win no matter which company ends up on top.[1]

## Recursive ownership

Asked who owns the largest shares of BlackRock and Vanguard themselves, the article answers that the largest shareholders of each are BlackRock and Vanguard, closing the ownership loop.[1] It further notes that only three other asset managers — [[fidelity-investments|Fidelity]], Vanguard and [[state-street|State Street]] — come close to competing with BlackRock in size, and that BlackRock is itself a major shareholder in the others, making the sector intensely oligopolistic.[1]
