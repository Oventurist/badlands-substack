# National Defense Authorization Act

The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) is the annual United States legislation that authorizes funding levels and sets policy for the [[pentagon|Department of Defense]]. Because it is considered must-pass legislation, the NDAA frequently serves as a vehicle for broader policy provisions attached by members of [[congress|Congress]].[1]

## Fiscal Year 2027 strategic investment fund provision

The [[united-states-senate|Senate]]'s Fiscal Year 2027 NDAA includes a provision that would authorize the Department of Defense to establish a strategic investment fund capable of taking equity stakes in private companies deemed critical to national security.[1] The proposal is intended to strengthen domestic defense and technology supply chains by allowing the government to invest directly in firms developing strategically important capabilities, particularly in advanced manufacturing, artificial intelligence, semiconductors and critical minerals.[1]

The legislation would centralize the Pentagon's direct equity investment authority, establish congressional reporting requirements and oversight mechanisms, and require regular disclosures regarding investment decisions.[1] Supporters argue the fund would help preserve critical industrial capabilities that might otherwise fail to attract private capital; critics contend it would expand the federal government's role as an investor in private enterprise and expose taxpayers to investment risk.[1] The provision remains part of the Senate version and must survive negotiations with the House before becoming law.[1]

## Analysis

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] situated the provision within a broader trend beginning with the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration's 2025 equity stakes in Intel and MP Materials, arguing that codifying the authority would remove the need for future administrations to rely on temporary executive orders or creative workarounds to take strategic public-market stakes.[1] He characterized the approach as a form of fifth-generation warfare in which the administration pressures the American corporate oligarchy to reinvest domestically rather than accumulate wealth abroad, and noted the irony that the policy parallels positions long held by [[bernie-sanders|Bernie Sanders]].[1]

## FY2027 US–Israel defense technology provision

The draft Fiscal Year 2027 NDAA produced by the [[house-armed-services-committee|House Armed Services Committee]] contained a Section 224 establishing a "[[united-states-israel-defense-technology-cooperation-initiative|United States-Israel Defense Technology Cooperation Initiative]]," which would expand defense cooperation between the United States and [[israel|Israel]] across research and development, weapons co-production, joint ventures, licensing agreements, and emerging technologies including artificial intelligence, quantum technologies, autonomous systems, directed energy, cyber capabilities, and biotechnology, with references to "network integration" and "data fusion."[2] Critics said the language amounted to a quiet integration of the two countries' militaries, while [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] assessed the accusation as overblown after reviewing the text.[2] A [[quincy-institute-for-responsible-statecraft|Quincy Institute]] brief by [[steven-simon|Steven Simon]] argued that such integration would shift the relationship out of annual aid votes in [[congress|Congress]] and into less visible defense acquisition channels.[2]
