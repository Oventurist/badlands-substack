# Battle of Quebec (1759)

The Battle of Quebec, fought at the Plains of Abraham on September 13, 1759 during the Seven Years' War, ended in French defeat and the death of the young British general James Wolfe. It is the subject of [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]]'s 1770 painting *The Death of General Wolfe* and is analyzed at length in the Badlands Media essay [[a-new-american-renaissance|A New American Renaissance]].

## Strategic significance in the source's reading

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] calls the battle the decisive moment at which the French empire lost its stranglehold on North America, thereby giving the networks of [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] room to organize what became the American Revolution. Citing [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]]'s *How the Nation Was Won: Volume 1 (1630–1757)*, the article contends that these networks had been preparing the conditions for independence for decades and expected that Quebec, once fallen to Britain, would side with the republican cause: French Canadians almost unanimously regarded the British as tyrants while getting along well with the thirteen colonies, and Quebec was understood to be the prospective fourteenth signatory of the Declaration of Independence. Franklin established Canada's first postal service in 1759 to secure lines of communication and elevate Canadian culture, and travelled to Quebec in 1774 to organize elites and population. The article says "certain games were played and certain bribes were made" that prevented this outcome.

The battle also turned, in the source's account, on the allegiance of the [[iroquois-confederacy|Mohawks and Iroquois]]. The [[jesuits|Jesuits]] controlling French North America had built extensive native alliances, and had the Six Nations sided with them the result could have differed; the efforts of [[william-johnson|William Johnson]] brought them to fight alongside the English and "tipped the scales of battle entirely."

## West's painting

West's composition is explicitly non-documentary: in reality Wolfe was shot and died behind a bush with only two people in attendance. West instead assembled a group including Johnson in native stockings and moccasins, pointing toward a soldier running up with a captured fleur-de-lis, and placed a Native American warrior at the center bearing more visual weight than the dying general — a contrast the source reads as an image of the weakness of the aristocratic British Empire itself. The essay emphasizes the painting's Platonic ambiguity, by which an American republican and a British aristocrat would each read an opposite message.

The work was also a technical break: until this 1770–1771 painting, virtually no history painting anywhere in Europe depicted its subjects in contemporary costume rather than Greco-Roman dress. West was warned he would never receive another commission, but persisted, writing that the event "took place... in a region of the world unknown to the Greeks and Romans" and that "if, instead of the facts of the transaction, I represent classical fictions, how shall I be understood by posterity?" [[king-george-iii|King George III]], impressed, bought the painting and settled the debate.
