# Overtime Pay Tax Deduction

The overtime pay tax deduction is a provision of the Republican 2025 tax cuts, signed into law in July 2025, that allows workers to deduct a portion of overtime earnings from taxable income. It became one of the most widely claimed new tax breaks of the 2026 filing season and the focus of an emerging anti-tax bidding war between the parties.[1]

## Mechanics

The provision allows eligible couples to deduct up to $25,000 on the extra "half" of time-and-a-half overtime pay earned for weeks worked beyond 40 hours.[1] It carries complex eligibility rules and temporary reporting waivers that have raised questions about potential abuse once full requirements take effect the following year, and it is scheduled to expire at the end of 2028.[1]

## Uptake

According to internal [[us-department-of-the-treasury|Treasury Department]] data, nearly 20 million Americans claimed the deduction, appearing on roughly 23 percent of returns filed during the season and outpacing other new breaks such as those for tipped income, seniors, and car-loan interest.[1]

## Political effects

Republicans have touted the deduction as a political win that helps reclaim blue-collar support ahead of the [[2026-midterm-elections|2026 midterm elections]].[1] Democrats are divided in response: Reps. [[brendan-boyle|Brendan Boyle]] of Pennsylvania and [[mike-thompson|Mike Thompson]] of California have voiced support for extending or enhancing the break to ease burdens on wage workers, while Sen. [[chris-van-hollen|Chris Van Hollen]] of Maryland has proposed alternative relief such as exempting those earning under $46,000 from income taxes entirely.[1] Rep. [[lloyd-doggett|Lloyd Doggett]] of Texas and other skeptics have called for more data on claims and warned of possible abuse, noting the policy's origins in a broader GOP package they opposed.[1] The dynamic has been described as a political anti-tax arms race.[1]
