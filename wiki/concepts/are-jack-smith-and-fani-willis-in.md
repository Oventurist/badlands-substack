# Are Jack Smith and Fani Willis in on the Plan?

"Are Jack Smith and Fani Willis in on the Plan? The Consequences of the Indictment of Donald Trump" is an opinion essay published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on September 20, 2023 and written by the pseudonymous contributor [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]]. The piece argues that the 2023 criminal indictments of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] brought by Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] and Fulton County District Attorney [[fani-willis|Fani Willis]] are so legally weak that they may, intentionally or otherwise, hand Trump the courtroom platform he needs to place evidence of fraud in the [[2020-united-states-presidential-election|2020 presidential election]] onto the public record. The article carries Badlands Media's standard disclaimer that contributions represent the opinions of their authors.

## Central thesis

The essay opens with a rhetorical question — "What is the best way to present evidence of election theft in the current environment?" — and answers it with the claim that the best way is "by indicting Trump for, essentially, lying about the 2020 election." The author contends that both indictments rest fundamentally on the allegation that Trump lied about the election result, and that this alleged lie is the predicate "crime" from which every other charged offense flows. On this reading, the prosecutions amount to the criminalization of "the very act of disagreeing with the mainstream."

The prosecution's problem, according to the source, is the possibility "that Trump has been telling the truth this whole time." As a criminal defendant, Trump possesses powers of discovery and, the author argues, must as a matter of due process be given the opportunity to prove the truth of his statements. The article states plainly, "To be quite frank, this looks like a set-up to me."

## Basics of court procedure

A substantial portion of the article is devoted to a lay explanation of legal procedure. It explains that every case begins with *the cause* — the unlawful act or acts alleged — and that what matters is whether a prosecutor or plaintiff can present a sufficient amount and type of admissible evidence to support that cause. A cause must satisfy its *elements* in order to be heard. The author uses defamation as an illustration: a plaintiff must prove that the defendant intentionally lied, did so maliciously, and that damages resulted. If a person says something untrue but sincerely believed it to be true, no defamation has occurred. The essay adds that "until recently, it was considered common sense that a person ought not be punished for speaking the truth in good faith to the best of their ability."

Applying the analogy, the article notes that Trump does not face defamation charges, but that the same logic applies: being wrong that the 2020 election was stolen is not a crime. What makes the charged acts criminal under the indictments is the assertion that Trump secretly knew he had lost. The author concludes that "Fani Willis and Jack Smith have both indicted Trump based on the belief that they can somehow read his mind."

## Standing and the dismissed election cases

The essay then discusses jurisdiction and [[legal-standing|standing]], dividing jurisdiction into territorial, personal, and subject-matter components. A court must confirm that the acts occurred within the proper territory, that they were committed by an entity over which the court has jurisdiction (a question of legal personality), and that the acts are cognizable by that court — one would not, the author writes, bring a trillion-dollar suit to a small claims court.

From this the article draws its key polemical point: standing must be established before evidence or testimony can be heard. It asserts that of the 55 cases filed in connection with the 2020 election, 50 were thrown out on standing, meaning "a LOT of evidence that never got the chance to be heard." Anyone who claims "there's no evidence because the courts threw out the cases" is, in the author's phrase, betraying their own illiteracy — "they may as well comment on a chess tournament while blindfolded." The author speculates that many of those dismissals reflected "some form of corruption or compromise operating in the background."

By contrast, the Smith and Willis cases are underway. Since Trump is effectively on trial for lying about the theft of the election, his defense rests on proving he was telling the truth. The article asks whether it is a crime to be incorrect, to disagree with one's advisors, or to attempt to stop a theft, and answers that none of these are crimes. It maintains that Trump "has never contradicted himself on the 2020 election" and has been "100% consistent since the get-go," so the prosecution will struggle to prove him a liar while Trump gains discovery powers to put his own evidence on the record "almost all of it for the very first time."

## Analysis of the indictments

The article examines the two charging documents directly. Willis's Fulton County indictment, it notes, contains 41 counts, of which only a handful are unrelated to "false statements" or to acts arising from them; the solicitation-of-violation-of-oath counts are described as depending on the prior existence of those alleged false statements. Count 1 is the RICO charge, listing 161 separate "acts" said to prove an organized criminal enterprise — a significant portion of which, the author argues, amount to tweets or ordinary conversations that officials investigating election fraud might have. What makes such acts criminal, the essay says, is only the presumption that they were carried out fraudulently by people intending to overthrow the election.

Turning to Smith's federal indictment, the article quotes paragraph 2: "Despite having lost, the Defendant [Trump] was determined to remain in power… the Defendant spread lies that there had been outcome-determinative fraud in the election… these claims were false, and the Defendant knew they were false." The author responds, "How does Jack Smith know that? Does he have psychic powers or something?" The indictment's four counts, the essay argues, all require that Trump acted in bad faith; if he acted in good faith throughout, the necessary elements cannot be proven and there is no case.

## Speculation about coordination

Under the heading "Were Fani Willis and Jack Smith in on It?", the article argues that the last thing the [[deep-state|Deep State]] would want is viable evidence of election fraud entering a court record, yet the indictments make exactly that outcome likely. Had no indictments been filed, the author reasons, the establishment could simply have continued discrediting Trump through friendly media. The essay offers three possible explanations: that they cannot help themselves, that they loathe the idea of Trump being free, or that "it's all part of the plan." The author explicitly hedges — "I'm only speculating here—I'm on the fence myself" — while insisting the indictments are "downright pitiful" and invoke "the flimsiest of premises in order to make the most far-fetched claims imaginable."

Beyond the legal dimension, the article claims the indictments shift the optics in Trump's favor: despite being a billionaire, he now has a mugshot and faces "a broken and corrupt system that's hurting everybody," making him appear to be one of the people. The essay states that if "Trump and the military are going to take back the country, there needs to be a necessary threshold of support amongst the people," and suggests mass arrests may have been avoided so far to prevent sparking a civil war. Either the prosecutors are desperate and have "served up the best they've got," or the indictments were "crafted to give Trump precisely the ammo he needs to proceed with the next phase of the 5G war" — a reference to [[5th-generation-warfare|fifth-generation warfare]].

## White Squall as narrative metaphor

The final section revisits the author's earlier essay on the 1996 film [[white-squall|White Squall]], which he interprets as a narrative metaphor for the journey of Trump, [[q|Q]], and anons since Q first appeared in October 2017. The article emphasizes that the last scene of the film places the ship's captain, Skipper, on trial "for doing the best he can with his ship and crew while facing enormous, destructive natural forces," and argues the correspondences with Trump's court cases are too numerous to be coincidence. Most importantly, Skipper is redeemed by his surviving crew, who stand by him at trial; in the same manner, the author writes, "loyal Anons are stepping into their role as the New News ('you are the news now' – Q)," shaping the informational landscape so that Trump receives a fair hearing. The essay adds the aside that in highly politicized proceedings, "what happens in a courtroom is often downstream from what happens in the court of public opinion."

The piece closes by noting the irony that Trump and his co-defendants stand accused of a massive conspiracy — "Perhaps they are …" — and signs off with the slogan [[wwg1wga|WWG1WGA]]. A closing note directs readers to the author's own Substack, American Hypnotist.
