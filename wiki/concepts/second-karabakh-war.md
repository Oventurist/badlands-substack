# Second Karabakh War

The **Second Karabakh War** was the 44-day conflict fought in late 2020 between [[azerbaijan|Azerbaijan]] and [[armenia|Armenia]] over the [[nagorno-karabakh|Nagorno-Karabakh]] territory and its adjacent districts. In the Badlands Media analysis by Joe Lange, the war is presented not as a peripheral post-Soviet territorial dispute but as one of the most consequential events of the past decade — a conflict that, in the author's framing, stripped the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and shadow government of control over a strategically vital narcotics corridor while receiving almost no coverage in the mainstream press.[1]

## Background

Relations between the former Soviet republics of Armenia and Azerbaijan had been tense since 1991, when the Armenian military occupied Nagorno-Karabakh — also known as Upper Karabakh, a territory internationally recognized as part of Azerbaijan — along with seven adjacent regions.[1] The article frames this First Karabakh War seizure as having occurred during the presidency of [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]], and argues it was not coincidental: the international community recognized the territory as Azerbaijani, and yet Bush "allowed the Armenian aggression to stand," in contrast to his forceful response to Saddam Hussein's invasion of Kuwait.[1] The author asks whether Bush's passivity reflected indifference or active encouragement, given the land's importance to the heroin supply flowing out of [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]].[1]

## The 2020 conflict

New clashes erupted on September 27, 2020, which the cited Anadolu Agency reporting describes as Armenia launching attacks on civilians and Azerbaijani forces and violating several humanitarian cease-fire agreements.[1] During the 44-day conflict, Azerbaijan liberated several cities and nearly 300 settlements and villages that had been occupied for nearly three decades.[1] A cease-fire deal mediated by Russia on November 10, 2020, ended the fighting and included provisions for future efforts toward a comprehensive resolution of the dispute.[1]

## The 81-mile border strip

The article's central claim is that the war was fought over an 81-mile (130-kilometer) section of the state border with [[iran|Iran]] that had been under Armenian control for roughly thirty years.[1] This strip physically divides Azerbaijan and constitutes, in the author's description, the "doorway to Europe" — the single main road through the region and a vital artery for trade from Iran into Europe.[1] Its recapture is presented as the true significance of the war: not territory for its own sake, but the severing of a heroin trade route running from Afghanistan through Iran and Armenia into European markets.[1]

Azerbaijani president [[ilham-aliyev|Ilham Aliyev]] stated that after Azerbaijan regained control of the border section, the volume of heroin seized on other sections of the Azerbaijan–Iran border doubled compared with the same period in previous years — evidence, he argued, that for about thirty years Armenia, in collusion with Iran, had used the occupied territories to carry out drug trafficking to Europe.[1] Aliyev added that he had repeatedly said during the years of occupation that the occupied territories were being used "for drug trafficking and training of international terrorists," and declared, "It is a proven fact today."[1]

## Foreign fighters

The article cites Daily Sabah reporting that Azerbaijan possessed concrete evidence of the Armenian military's use of terrorist fighters recruited from different parts of the Middle East, with an Azerbaijani envoy specifying that Lebanese, Syrian and other fighters had fought against Azerbaijan on behalf of Armenia.[1] The author reads the Lebanese and Syrian composition as pointing to [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]] — a proxy of Iran which, in this framing, is itself controlled by the CIA — and asks why such fighters would have any interest in a small strip of Caucasus land absent a shared narcotics interest.[1]

## Interpretive significance

Within the article's overall thesis, the Second Karabakh War is contrasted directly with the war in Gaza. The Gaza conflict, the author argues, is a perpetual and deliberately unresolved war used to divide people along religious lines and generate war spending, and therefore receives saturation coverage; the Karabakh war, which actually removed the shadow government's control of a critical logistics corridor, received almost none.[1] The author treats this asymmetry of coverage as itself evidence of purpose.[1]
