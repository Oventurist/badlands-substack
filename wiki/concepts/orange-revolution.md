# Orange Revolution

The Orange Revolution was the wave of protests and political events in Ukraine during 2004–05, treated by Badlands Media as perhaps the most relevant 21st-century example of [[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]] and as a precursor — together with its sequel, the 2013 [[euromaidan|Maidan uprising]] — to the ongoing Russian invasion of Ukraine.[1]

## Surface account

On the surface, the Orange Revolution followed the run-off of the 2004 Ukrainian presidential election between [[viktor-yushchenko|Viktor Yushchenko]] and [[viktor-yanukovych|Viktor Yanukovych]].[1] Yushchenko represented pro-Western democratic reforms and closer ties to Europe and [[nato|NATO]], while Yanukovych had a good working relationship with Vladimir Putin.[1] Official results declared Yanukovych the winner with 49.5% to Yushchenko's 46.6%, but widespread allegations of fraud — which the article compares to the 2020 U.S. election — ignited mass protests in Kyiv's Independence Square.[1] Hundreds of thousands of demonstrators in orange scarves and flags occupied the square for weeks in freezing conditions demanding a revote; the Supreme Court annulled the results and a December 26 rerun gave Yushchenko 52%.[1] He was inaugurated in January 2005, marking a temporary shift toward EU and NATO integration.[1]

## Badlands reading

DeLarme argues the episode was in fact the opening salvo in a broader U.S. strategy to pry Ukraine away from Russian influence, install compliant pro-Western leaders, and weaponize NGOs, media and civil society as tools of soft-power intervention.[1] He situates it, along with [[otpor|Otpor!]] in Yugoslavia, within the Western foreign policy establishment's undeclared second Cold War against Russia, tying the same lineage to events as varied as the ousting of Imran Khan in Pakistan and the 2015 Iran Nuclear Deal.[1]

Though celebrated in Western media as a triumph of people power, the revolution echoed the template used for Otpor! and other [[color-revolution|color revolutions]] — Georgia's 2003 Rose Revolution, Kyrgyzstan's Tulip Revolution, Lebanon's Cedar Revolution, Tunisia's Jasmine Revolution and Armenia's Velvet Revolution.[1] Skeptics at the time ranged from presidents Vladimir Putin, Alexander Lukashenko and Hugo Chavez to Republican Rep. Ron Paul, columnist Patrick Buchanan, and left-wing writers in the Nation and the Guardian.[1]

## Funding and training

[[usaid|USAID]], the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] and other foundations sponsored U.S. organizations including [[freedom-house|Freedom House]], the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]], the [[national-democratic-institute|National Democratic Institute]], the [[solidarity-center|Solidarity Center]], the [[eurasia-foundation|Eurasia Foundation]] and [[internews|Internews]].[1] These "democracy promotion" outfits poured millions into Ukrainian civil society, training activists in protest tactics, media manipulation and election monitoring.[1] The youth movement [[pora|Pora]], central to the protests, received direct funding including an initial $30,000 seed from the Canadian embassy.[1]

Because the revolution was modeled on earlier successes, Serbian activists from Otpor and Georgians from Kmara were brought to Ukraine to coach organizers.[1] [[mark-r-beissinger|Mark R. Beissinger]], writing in Dissent, described how overthrowing governments had become "an international business," with Otpor activists functioning as "a modern type of mercenary" and Pora leaders trained in Serbia at the Center for Nonviolent Resistance; Pora activists even joked about creating a new Comintern for democratic revolution.[1] Further Western support came through the NDI and Serbia's [[canvas|Center for Applied Nonviolent Action and Strategies]].[1]

## Aftermath

The article calls the revolution's success pyrrhic: Yushchenko's government fractured through infighting, paving the way for Yanukovych's 2010 return and the 2014 Maidan events, which DeLarme describes as another U.S.-orchestrated coup using the same tactics and funding mechanisms.[1]

## Internews and the media groundwork

Badlands Media's "The Information Imperium" argues that the 2004 uprising was preceded by years of Western media investment in Ukraine: by 2001 [[internews|Internews]] had reportedly trained 1,900 journalists and supported 203 television stations and 126 radio outlets reaching 50 million viewers and 18 million listeners, though the article concedes these figures are hard to verify.[2] This activity is described as synchronised with the [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]]'s push to orient Ukraine westward, with the [[national-democratic-institute|NDI]] and [[international-republican-institute|IRI]] coaching the "spontaneous" uprising while Internews amplified pro-U.S. voices.[2]
