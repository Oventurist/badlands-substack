# Nationwide Injunctions

A nationwide (or universal) injunction is an order by a single federal district court that blocks enforcement of a government policy against all persons everywhere, not merely the parties before the court. The device became a central flashpoint in the second term of Donald Trump, as district judges repeatedly enjoined executive branch immigration and administrative actions on a nationwide basis.[1]

## The June 2025 Supreme Court case

The case argued as a challenge to Trump's changes to [[birthright-citizenship|birthright citizenship]] was, in substance, a case about whether district courts may issue such universal relief, and its decision was scheduled for release on June 27, 2025, the final day of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] term.[1] Media outlets nonetheless framed it as the "birthright citizenship" case; Badlands commentary argued the label was misleading and that the real question was presidential authority and whether district courts — which derive their authority and limited jurisdiction from [[us-congress|Congress]] — can halt a president's domestic and foreign agenda.[1]

NBC News reported that what the court said about nationwide injunctions "could have wide-ranging impacts, with judges frequently ruling against Trump on his broad use of executive power," and noted the court retained the option of sidestepping the injunction question and instead reaching the merits of the birthright citizenship plan.[1] Badlands host Ashe in America criticized that framing, questioning whether the Court could assume authority over immigration and naturalization policy without lower-court factfinding or full briefing by the parties.[1]

## Related dispute over the Maryland bench

The nationwide-injunction fight overlapped with the Justice Department's June 2025 suit against all 15 judges of the federal district bench in Maryland, filed in response to a standing order automatically granting temporary injunctions in immigration habeas cases — an action the administration characterized as an "egregious example of judicial overreach."[1]
