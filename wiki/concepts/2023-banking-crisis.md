# 2023 Banking Crisis

The 2023 banking crisis was a wave of United States bank failures that began in the spring of 2023 and prompted extensive comparison — and equally extensive official denial of comparison — with the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 financial crisis]].[1] A May 31, 2023 Badlands essay by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the media sector was "pushing hard for Americans to dismiss comparisons to 2008 – while continually making comparisons to 2008," and that the acknowledged similarities were real: banks were failing, regulators were absorbing criticism and issuing promises, and investor uncertainty was disrupting markets.[1]

## The three-difference talking point

The essay identified a common set of talking points, circulated through outlets including [[cnn|CNN]], [[axios|Axios]] and Fortune, asserting three primary differences between 2008 and 2023: scale, root cause, and overall impact on the banking system.[1]

On scale, 25 banks collapsed in the initial phase of 2008 and nearly 400 failed over the following three years, whereas only three — [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]], [[signature-bank|Signature Bank]] and [[first-republic-bank|First Republic Bank]] — appeared on the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]] failed bank list in 2023.[1] The essay credited the [[new-york-times|New York Times]] and [[new-york-post|New York Post]] for reporting that asset volume matters more than institution count: the three 2023 failures held $532 billion in assets, which the Times noted was "more than the $526 billion, when adjusted for inflation, held by the 25 banks that collapsed in 2008 at the height of the global financial crisis."[1]

On root cause, analysts attributed the 2008 failures chiefly to risky lending and faulty loans "packaged as safe investments," and the 2023 failures to defective business models and an inability to adapt to the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s rapidly rising interest rates.[1] Ashe in America rejected this as a genuine distinction, tracing both episodes to the domestic policy of the [[us-congress|United States Congress]] and the monetary policy of the Federal Reserve, which she characterised as a private central bank.[1] In the years before 2008, she wrote, loosened monetary policy and lowered interest rates combined with government policies promoting homeownership and expanded credit access to inflate the housing bubble that burst when borrowers stopped paying.[1]

On overall impact, the argument was that the 2023 failures involved less interconnected institutions and fewer secondary markets tied to risky assets than the domino effect of 2008.[1] The essay dismissed this as "hopium," holding that it was too early to assess the overall impact and that those downplaying the failures "may be in for a rude awakening."[1]

## JPMorgan Chase's role

A further parallel identified with 2008 was the position of [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]], the largest U.S. bank and third largest in the world, which had acquired [[washington-mutual|Washington Mutual]] and [[bear-stearns|Bear Stearns]] during the earlier crisis and acquired First Republic Bank in May 2023.[1] Chief executive [[jamie-dimon|Jamie Dimon]] told Bloomberg on May 11, 2023 that the crisis "will eventually sort itself out" and that "it's not like the crisis in 2008 or 2009," while stating that the firm would not buy further failed banks because of the integration workload.[1]

## Enforcement turn against short sellers

The crisis also produced a regulatory and prosecutorial turn against [[short-selling|short sellers]] of bank securities. Dimon called on the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|SEC]] to pursue traders in options, derivatives and short sales, and on May 24, 2023 Reuters reported a [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] official saying action against short sellers was likely "in the next few months."[1] Ashe in America framed this as part of a broader offensive by what she termed the [[banking-cartel|Banking Cartel]], citing a report by [[sean-morgan|Sean Morgan]] and [[kirk-elliott|Kirk Elliott]] on the [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] Economy Update that a London bank had begun requiring customers to justify in writing any withdrawal above £2,000, and warning that a comparable regime in the United States was not far off.[1]
