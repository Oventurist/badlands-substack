# Medicaid

Medicaid is a federal health-care program jointly funded by the federal government and the states that provides health coverage to tens of millions of Americans.[1] Because eligibility determination, enrollment, and payment administration are handled at the state level while a large share of the money is federal, the program has become a recurring focus of congressional oversight into fraud, waste, and improper payments.[1]

## 2026 congressional fraud probe

In March 2026 the [[house-energy-and-commerce-committee|House Energy and Commerce Committee]] announced it was expanding an investigation into potential Medicaid fraud and improper payments to ten states: New York, California, [[colorado|Colorado]], Massachusetts, Maine, Nebraska, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Vermont and Washington State.[1] Committee members sent letters to state Medicaid agencies requesting records on eligibility verification, enrollment procedures, and potential improper payments, with the stated aim of determining whether states are adequately preventing fraud, waste, and abuse.[1]

The expansion followed earlier fraud revelations in [[minnesota|Minnesota]], which Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized as only the beginning of a wider federal examination of blue-state program books.[1] She argued that where state-level prosecution of such fraud has stalled, the cause is less a lack of authority than the exercise of prosecutorial discretion to avoid cases with political ramifications.[1]

## Minnesota payment suspension (February 2026)

In February 2026 the Trump administration suspended Medicaid payments to [[minnesota|Minnesota]] over fraud concerns in the state's program, withholding approximately $259 million while a federal review proceeded.[2] The action was the first move of the [[war-on-fraud|War on Fraud]] led by Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]], who announced it alongside [[centers-for-medicare-and-medicaid-services|CMS]] Administrator [[mehmet-oz|Mehmet Oz]], with Minnesota given 60 days to implement corrective measures.[2] The administration described the suspension as part of a broader effort to combat fraud in federal benefit programs, while Minnesota Democratic leaders called it politically motivated and warned of harm to beneficiaries.[2]

## Cuts in the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill

Medicaid reductions were among the most contested provisions of the Senate-passed [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill]] in July 2025.[3] Representative [[chip-roy|Chip Roy]] (R-Texas) said on July 2 that the cuts in the Senate version were "a bit better" than he had originally anticipated after receiving more information, though he added that he still had concerns and was continuing to review the text.[3] Roy had voted against the rule in committee, contributing to uncertainty over whether the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]] could pass the measure before its self-imposed July 4 deadline.[3]

## Medicaid politics and the 2026 midterms

By mid-July 2025, Democrats were banking on backlash to Republican Medicaid cuts to boost them in the 2026 midterms, but Politico reported a complication: the cuts had not yet taken effect, with work requirements delayed until 2027 and financing changes until 2028, while the [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill Act]] frontloaded tax breaks voters were likely to see sooner.[4] California-based Democratic strategist Doug Herman said that "if we say 'they took it from you,' but it hasn't happened yet, it just complicates it," though Democrats pressed ahead with Medicaid-focused messaging as a central midterm theme.[4]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that Democratic voters believed Trump was kicking poor people off Medicaid while Republican voters believed the cuts targeted waste, fraud, and abuse.[4] Because Medicaid is treated as a third rail in American politics, she argued, the fact that the cuts happened at all — and that Politico and Democratic strategists were cautioning that the issue was no silver bullet — signaled a massive shift in public sentiment, which she credited to [[doge|DOGE]]'s early efforts to expose systemic waste, fraud, and abuse and thereby build public support for reducing the size of government.[4]

## Medical debt pressures (November 2025)

Charities that help patients cover medical bills reported an alarming increase in requests for assistance in late 2025 — before scheduled cuts to Medicaid under President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill Act]] took effect and before the potential year-end expiration of [[affordable-care-act|Obamacare]] subsidies.[5] The charities warned of exploding medical debt and lower survival rates for diseases such as cancer if [[congress|Congress]] failed to act.[5] Michael Sapienza, chief executive of the Colorectal Cancer Alliance, said his organization could not "really handle much more demand," while the HealthWell Foundation reported financial assistance already 23 percent higher than in all of the previous year.[5]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the surge reflected the end of federal subsidies for care recipients could not previously afford without taxpayer support, and contended that charity rather than the state is the appropriate vehicle for such aid — arguing that when the state performs charity, charity becomes an entitlement and dependence follows.[5] She also cautioned that health-branded financial products such as insurance, cost-sharing cooperatives and federal subsidies are not themselves healthcare.[5]
