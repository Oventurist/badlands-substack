# DOJ Lawsuit Against the Maryland Federal Bench

In June 2025 the [[department-of-justice|U.S. Department of Justice]] filed a complaint naming all 15 judges of the federal district court in Maryland as defendants, an escalation of the running conflict between the executive and judicial branches during Donald Trump's second term.[1]

## Background

The suit responded to a standing order issued by Chief Judge [[george-l-russell-iii|George L. Russell III]] concerning habeas corpus petitions filed in immigration cases.[1] The order required that every apparent illegal immigration case receive a temporary injunction "upon its filing," with terms remaining in effect until 4 p.m. on the second business day after filing unless extended by the presiding judge.[1] The Justice Department called the order an "egregious example of judicial overreach," arguing that district judges had "used and abused their equitable powers" to undermine enforcement of the administration's immigration policy.[1]

## Procedural posture and commentary

Because every judge in the district was a defendant, the case was referred to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, as reported by legal commentator Margot Cleveland.[1] Badlands host Ashe in America argued that headlines describing the suit as Trump escalating a "war on the judiciary" inverted the sequence of events, contending that the district courts had moved against Trump first, that federal district courts derive their authority from [[us-congress|Congress]], and that the dispute was likely to reach the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]].[1] The suit ran parallel to the Court's pending consideration of [[nationwide-injunctions|nationwide injunctions]].[1]

## June 2025 filing

The Department of Justice filed suit against the U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland, challenging a standing order that automatically blocks federal immigration enforcement actions.[2] The court's order requires clerks to immediately issue injunctions halting the removal or legal status challenges of any alien detained in Maryland who files a habeas petition.[2] DOJ officials argued that the order bypasses essential procedural and legal requirements for preliminary injunctions, ignores congressional intent, and violates Supreme Court precedent.[2]
