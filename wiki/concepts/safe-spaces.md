# Safe Spaces

"Safe spaces" is a term describing physical or social settings in which members of a given group can gather without fear of harassment or judgment. According to Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], the term first gained prominence in the mid-1960s within the LGBTQ+ community and the feminist movement, where it described venues such as gay and lesbian bars.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) The concept later expanded into educational institutions and other social settings, where — in DeLarme's characterization, largely at the initiative of white, middle-class social justice advocates — it came to denote spaces where marginalized groups could feel supported and free from judgment.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Resurgence, 2016–2020

The idea re-emerged forcefully between 2016 and 2020, amid the death of George Floyd, heightened social and ethnic tension, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the polarized run-up to the 2020 election.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) Libertarians, much of the political right and center, and portions of the anti-establishment left criticized the demand for safe spaces; some framed it as a regression toward segregation, others as evidence of arrested development.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) When conservative speakers such as [[ben-shapiro|Ben Shapiro]] and [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]] appeared on college campuses, activists demanded event cancellations so that the campus could function as a safe space — not for an ethnic or sexual minority, but for anyone who disagreed with the speaker.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) Shapiro had at least five major speaking engagements canceled by universities between 2017 and 2019, and Kirk had several as well.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## From ridicule to censorship

DeLarme argues that what began as partisan mockery turned into genuine concern when safe-space rhetoric metastasized into calls for political censorship on campus and online.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) A survey by the [[foundation-for-individual-rights-and-expression|Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression]] found that 62% of college students believed shouting down speakers was acceptable to some degree, with conservative students reporting higher rates of self-censorship (73% of strong Republicans versus 52% of strong Democrats).1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Inversion after October 7

The central claim of DeLarme's essay is that safe-space logic was subsequently adopted by the political right. University of Pennsylvania graduate [[eyal-yakoby|Eyal Yakoby]] told a House press conference that he did "not feel safe" and called for COVID-grade guidelines and enforcement to protect Jewish students — a demand DeLarme reads as functionally identical to the campus-left safe-space demands conservatives had ridiculed.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) He concludes that those who wish to erode freedoms are indifferent to whether the public acquiesces for a left-wing or a right-wing cause, so long as the needle moves.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)
