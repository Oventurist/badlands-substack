# Snowden Disclosures

The Snowden disclosures were the series of leaks of classified U.S. surveillance documents made by [[edward-snowden|Edward Snowden]] beginning in 2013, which Badlands Media describes as unfolding with their consequences over roughly the 2013–2015 period.[1]

## Consequences

The leaks led to multiple Pulitzer Prizes for the news organizations and journalists who worked with Snowden and produced surveillance reforms around the world.[1] Within the United States they prompted the passage of the [[usa-freedom-act|USA Freedom Act]] in 2015, which ended the [[national-security-agency|NSA]]'s bulk collection of Americans' phone records.[1] Overseas, the European Court of Justice ruled against the U.S.–EU Safe Harbor Agreement in 2015 on privacy grounds, ending a framework that had permitted transatlantic data transfers.[1]

Technology companies, particularly U.S.-based firms, faced a backlash from international customers wary of American surveillance, prompting broader deployment of encryption and a push toward privacy-focused services; Google, Apple and others strengthened encryption in their products in what became known as the "Snowden effect."[1]

## Contested narratives

Badlands Media notes that during and after the disclosures there were extensive misrepresentations and outright falsehoods about Snowden, but that over time enough evidence emerged to debunk them — to the point that the U.S. government, while still seeking his arrest, renounced claims that he was a Russian spy or that he shopped his material to adversarial governments.[1] The publication argues that commentators and senators nonetheless recycled these debunked claims a decade later, using them to argue against the confirmation of [[tulsi-gabbard|Tulsi Gabbard]] as [[director-of-national-intelligence|Director of National Intelligence]].[1]

It was also Snowden who revealed that [[james-clapper|James Clapper]] and [[john-brennan|John Brennan]] had lied before the [[senate-intelligence-committee|Senate Intelligence Committee]], a fact the article treats as central irony given Brennan's leading role in attacking Gabbard.[1] The author separately acknowledged, without endorsing, arguments that Snowden was part of a [[cia|CIA]] operation to undermine the NSA or that he leaked selectively, holding such claims to be unprovable and immaterial to the confirmation question.[1]
