# America for Sale — Part 3

"America for Sale - Part 3," subtitled "Sanctions Are a Scam," is a Badlands Media essay published on March 15, 2025 by [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]]. It is the third installment of the [[america-for-sale-part-1|America for Sale]] series, following [[america-for-sale-part-2|Part 2]]. The article argues that hostage crises function as cover stories for covert dealmaking, that economic [[sanctions|sanctions]] are deliberately engineered to create closed financial systems useful to the [[cia|CIA]] and what the author calls the shadow government, and that this machinery links [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]]'s 1994 nuclear agreement with [[north-korea|North Korea]], the pardon of commodities trader Marc Rich, and the Uranium One transaction approved during [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s tenure at the [[state-department|State Department]].

## Hostages as cover story

The essay opens with the claim that hostages "have been a template for the CIA/shadow government for many years," used as a cover story because captives reliably generate public sympathy. Its central example is Bill Clinton's August 2009 trip to North Korea to secure the release of two American journalists held by [[kim-jong-il|Kim Jong Il]] on spying accusations.

Citing [[buzzfeed|BuzzFeed News]], the article reproduces a memo attached to a hacked email reporting that the visit "went so well" that Kim Jong Il "asked that President Clinton return often, officially and unofficially," suggesting he visit again "to tour someday when there were no problems in bilateral relations." Clinton, the memo says, replied that "he would like someday to visit the beautiful seaside location depicted in the painting in the entry hall of the Paekhwanwon Guest House," to which "Chairman Kim said he would show him a much more beautiful place, and that President Clinton should come back to the DPRK on holiday."

The memo, BuzzFeed reported, was attached to an email sent to [[john-podesta|John Podesta]], who accompanied Clinton on the mission and was later chair of Hillary Clinton's presidential campaign; the emails were published by WikiLeaks after what researchers described as a Russian hack. The author reads the exchange as evidence that "we have been lied to forever" — that the adversarial framing of North Korea was "a charade" and that the hostages were never the real purpose of the trip.

The article notes Q drop #227 (November 24, 2017), which posted a photograph of the negotiation without comment. In the image, the author writes, Clinton aide [[doug-band|Doug Band]] — then working for the [[clinton-foundation|Clinton Foundation]] and the [[clinton-global-initiative|Clinton Global Initiative]] — stands behind Kim Jong Il, while Podesta stands behind Bill Clinton.

## The 1994 Agreed Framework

To explain why Kim Jong Il specifically requested Clinton, the essay argues the two men "ALREADY had a working relationship" dating to the Agreed Framework of 1994. Quoting the History Channel, it recalls Clinton taking the podium on October 18, 1994 to announce the deal — "This agreement is good for the United States, good for our allies, and good for the safety of the entire world" — which was designed to halt North Korea's nuclear program.

The four-page agreement, the source notes, required North Korea to shut down its main reactor at Yongbyon, abandon two others, and seal fuel usable for weapons; in exchange the United States would supply oil to replace lost generating capacity and build two new "light fuel" plants. The article asks why the United States would help North Korea build nuclear plants if the aim was to prevent weapons development, and answers that the deal "was never meant to prevent North Korea from getting nuclear weapons." North Korea, it says, broke the agreement almost immediately and resumed weapons work at an underground facility.

The author identifies oil as the key to the arrangement: by lifting sanctions and supplying fuel, the United States allowed Pyongyang to reallocate cash to its weapons program, thereby accelerating rather than slowing it.

## Iran sanctions and the sanctions thesis

Five months after the North Korean deal, the article notes, Clinton moved in the opposite direction toward Iran. Quoting Wikipedia, it records that Clinton imposed "some of the toughest sanctions against Iran in March 1995," during the presidency of Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, in response to the Iranian nuclear program and support for Hezbollah, Hamas and Palestine Islamic Jihad, through Executive Order 12957, which prohibited U.S. trading in Iran's oil industry; Executive Order 12959 followed in May 1995, ending trade with Iran altogether.

The essay treats the contrast as a revealing contradiction: both Iran and North Korea were pursuing nuclear weapons and sponsoring terrorism, yet one had sanctions lifted while the other was embargoed. Its answer is that "sanctions have always been part of the game." Cutting a country off from the world banking system, the article argues, does not merely punish it; it creates a "closed financial system" in which money-laundering law and congressional oversight do not reach. "Sanctions promote corruption," the author writes, and closed systems like Iran and North Korea "allow the CIA/shadow government to launder taxpayers money much easier," beyond the tracking of [[congress|Congress]].

## Marc Rich and Pardongate

The article then turns to Pardongate — Clinton's 140 pardons and 36 commutations issued on his final day in office, January 20, 2001, which the author characterises as deliberate cover for one controversial act: the pardon of Marc Rich.

Quoting The American Liberty Report, the essay recalls that Rich, born Marcel Reich, was "a notorious commodities trader and international financier who was convicted of tax fraud in the U.S. in the wake of oil purchases from Iran during the 1979 American hostage crisis," and that his career included deals for Muammar Gaddafi's Libya, Kim Il Sung's North Korea, Slobodan Milosevic's Yugoslavia and Ferdinand Marcos's Philippines. Rich had been indicted in 1983 for tax evasion, racketeering and wire fraud and was, at the time, the largest tax evader in American history, owing nearly $50 million.

Sanctions, the author argues, were the source of Rich's fortune: they eliminated competition and forced desperate producers into lucrative terms, allowing him to buy Iranian oil far below market price and resell it above market to other sanctioned states such as apartheid-era South Africa. Rich, the article says, was an "untouchable" because of deep connections to the CIA and [[mossad|Mossad]].

The Washington Post is quoted at length on Rich's base in Zug, Switzerland — a town of "stringent privacy laws, low tax rates and guarantees of corporate anonymity" boasting "10,000 international companies, or roughly one corporation for every two residents" — where after "a lifetime of deal-making and sanctions-breaking" Rich became "the uncrowned king of Zug." The same report lists his trading partners as "a compendium of rogue states: Iran during the hostage crisis, apartheid-era South Africa, Slobodan Milosevic's Yugoslavia, North Korea, Moammar Gaddafi's Libya, the Soviet Union under Leonid Brezhnev," and quotes former U.S. marshal Howard Safir, who staked out Rich's Swiss residence in 1985: "He sees himself as a citizen of the world, unencumbered by the laws of sovereign nations... His view is that everything and everyone can be bought and sold, and government is irrelevant." The article asserts that repeated arrest attempts failed because someone in the State Department tipped Rich off.

Citing Foreign Policy, the essay adds that Rich was "an inveterate sanctions-buster" who "pioneered the practice of commodity swaps, like the uranium-for-oil deals he brokered in the 1980s between apartheid South Africa and Islamic Iran and Soviet Russia." From this the author infers that the CIA stood behind the nuclear programs of the "rogue states," and speculates that Rich helped the Clintons sell Iranian oil to North Korea for profit — the real motive, in the author's view, for the pardon.

## The Russian Reset and Uranium One

The essay next links the 2009 North Korea trip to the [[russian-reset|Russian Reset]]. Quoting The Week, it recalls that in March 2009 Secretary of State Hillary Clinton presented Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov with a mistranslated "Reset" button, symbolising Obama's outreach; relations with President Dmitri Medvedev subsequently produced an arms control treaty, Afghan transit rights, Russian support for U.N. sanctions on Iran, and Russian entry into the World Trade Organization.

Q drop #48 (November 2, 2017) is quoted: "(May 2010) BO 'Russia should be viewed as a friendly partner under Section 123 the Atomic Energy Act of 1954'... What did the Russia reset really provide? Clearance/pathway to complete the U1 deal? Why is the Canadian PM so important?" The author reads the reset as narrative preparation clearing a pathway for the Uranium One sale, and connects the Canadian question to figures around the deal and to [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]]'s alleged panic.

Q drop #46, also from November 2, 2017, asked why information about [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] mattered regarding "U1 and export approval to Canada to EU," and whether the administration falsely claimed uranium would never be exported. The article answers with reporting from The Hill: after the sale of a Canadian mining company with significant U.S. uranium reserves to a Russian state firm, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission assured Congress in a November 2010 press release that "No uranium produced at either facility may be exported," a claim repeated by then-Chairman Gregory Jaczko in a letter to Senator John Barrasso of Wyoming.

NRC memos reviewed by The Hill, however, showed the agency did approve shipments of yellowcake from Russian-owned U.S. mines to Canada in 2012 "through a third party," with some ultimately reaching Europe. Rather than issue Rosatom a direct export license, which "would have raised red flags inside a Congress already suspicious of the deal," the NRC in 2012 amended an existing export license held by a Paducah, Kentucky trucking firm, RSB Logistics Services Inc, adding Uranium One to its client list. The license, dated March 16, 2012, raised the volume RSB could ship to the Cameco Corp. plant in Ontario from 7,500,000 to 12,000,000 kilograms. "The move escaped notice in Congress."

Uranium One controlled roughly 20 percent of U.S. uranium capacity; Rosatom, Russia's state nuclear agency, acquired it in 2010 through the subsidiary ARMZ. Hillary Clinton, the article stresses, denied involvement even though State Department sign-off was required.

## Frank Giustra, Kazakhstan and the Clinton Foundation

Drawing on the New York Times investigation of April 2015, the essay traces the deal's origins to "leaders of the Canadian mining industry" who were "major donors" to the Clintons. The Times reported that beyond mines in Kazakhstan "among the most lucrative in the world," the sale gave Russia one-fifth of U.S. uranium production capacity, and that the State Department under Hillary Clinton was among the agencies signing off.

The path began in 2005 in Kazakhstan, where Canadian mining financier Frank Giustra "orchestrated his first big uranium deal, with Mr. Clinton at his side." The two flew aboard Giustra's private jet to Almaty and dined with authoritarian president Nursultan Nazarbayev, whose bid to head an international elections monitoring group Clinton endorsed — undercutting American criticism of Kazakhstan's human rights record, including by his wife, then a senator. Within days, Giustra's company UrAsia Energy Ltd signed a preliminary deal for stakes in three uranium mines controlled by the state agency Kazatomprom. Several months later Giustra donated $31.3 million to the Clinton Foundation, which the author labels quid pro quo.

In 2007 UrAsia merged with Uranium One, described by the Times as "a South African company with assets in Africa and Australia," in a $3.5 billion transaction; the combined firm kept the Uranium One name and was controlled by UrAsia investors including Ian Telfer, who became chairman. Giustra's personal stake was estimated at about $45 million, and he said he sold out in 2007.

Publicly disclosed records showed Telfer as the only Uranium One official to give to the Clinton Foundation, at no more than $250,000 in 2007. But Canadian tax records for Telfer's family charity, the Fernwood Foundation, showed he "donated millions of dollars more" during and after the period when the foreign investment committee reviewed the Russian deal: $1 million reported in 2009 — the year his company appealed to the American Embassy to help keep its Kazakh mines — $250,000 in 2010, $600,000 in 2011 and $500,000 in 2012. These came on top of between $1.3 million and $5.6 million in reported contributions from people tied to Uranium One or UrAsia. The Times also reported that Bill Clinton received $500,000 for a Moscow speech from a Russian investment bank promoting Uranium One stock. The author observes the irony that undisclosed giving amounted to the same offence — tax evasion — for which Marc Rich was indicted and pardoned.

The article treats Uranium One's South African origin as "a key data point," given Rich's South African commodity trading and expertise in uranium-for-oil swaps.

## Chagoury, Kurzin and the Rich network

Citing the New York Post, the essay argues that Rich's associates sustained the connection after his death in 2013. Nigerian businessman Gilbert Chagoury was "a close ally and business associate of Rich" — the Nigerian media in 1999 called the "Gilbert Chagoury-Marc Rich alliance... a formidable foe" — and in 2000 was convicted in Geneva of money laundering and aiding a criminal organization in connection with billions stolen from Nigeria under dictator Gen. Sani Abacha. The conviction was later expunged in a plea deal.

Chagoury organized a 2003 event at which Bill Clinton was paid $100,000 to speak, donated millions to the Clinton Foundation, and in 2009 pledged $1 billion to the Clinton Global Initiative. Relatives were active in Hillary Clinton's 2008 campaign, with Michel Chagoury serving as a bundler and staffer and others giving the $4,600 maximum. In 2009 CGI gave Chagoury's company a sustainable development award; in 2013 Bill Clinton appeared at a ceremony in Nigeria for one of his projects; Chagoury attended Clinton's 60th birthday party and Doug Band's wedding. The author notes that CGI was Band's brainchild and that "almost all of the money flowing through the Clinton Global Initiative went through Doug Band."

The second associate is Russian investor Sergei Kurzin, who worked for Rich in the 1990s seeking investments in the former Soviet Union. An engineer by training, Kurzin was involved in deals in Kazakhstan "including the lucrative Uranium One deal that involved Bill Clinton and Frank Giustra," and donated $1 million to the Clinton Foundation.
