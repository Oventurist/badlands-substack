# 2025 United States Credit Rating Downgrade

On Friday, May 16, 2025, [[moodys|Moody's]] downgraded the United States one notch from Aaa to Aa1, stripping the country of its last top-tier sovereign credit rating.[1]

## Assessment of the impact

The Financial Times, which had previewed the implications of a downgrade months earlier, framed the central question as whether it mattered at all.[1] It declined to predict the equity market reaction when trading opened the following Monday, recalling that [[standard-and-poors|S&P]]'s August 2011 downgrade had triggered the worst single-day fall in U.S. stock prices since the global financial crisis before a quick recovery, likely driven by fears over the financial plumbing rather than fundamentals.[1] Mechanically, the paper judged the 2025 downgrade would matter "almost certainly 'not at all'."[1]

## Administration response

Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]], appearing on Meet the Press, called Moody's "a lagging indicator" and said that is "what everyone thinks of credit agencies," invoking [[larry-summers|Larry Summers]]'s comparable view of the 2011 downgrade.[1] He blamed the prior administration's spending, cited an inherited 6.7% deficit-to-GDP ratio outside of recession or war, and promised to cut spending and grow the economy.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Chris Paul argued that the muted response exposed the AAA rating as a manufactured fear: the downgrade was "an info op that removes the fear of a credit downgrade," and its not mattering "is the point."[1] He placed it alongside what he called other existential risk hoaxes and argued that the underlying reason it does not matter is that the financial system is being reset.[1] He treated the post-2011 Justice Department investigation of S&P — the exit of CEO Deven Sharma and the subsequent $5 billion federal lawsuit that S&P called retaliation for its downgrade — as the more consequential precedent.[1]
