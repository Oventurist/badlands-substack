# Marbury v. Madison

Marbury v. Madison is the 1803 decision of the United States Supreme Court, authored by Chief Justice John Marshall, that established the principle of judicial review and the supremacy of the Constitution over ordinary legislation.[1] Its most frequently quoted formulation — "A Law repugnant to the Constitution is void" — has become a standard rhetorical anchor for constitutional-limits arguments.[1]

## Use in contemporary Second Amendment argument

The Marshall maxim was invoked in June 2023 by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] as a closing consolation to readers frustrated by the likely survival of the ATF [[stabilizing-brace-rule|stabilizing brace rule]].[1] Having predicted that Congress would not assemble the two-thirds majorities needed to override President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s promised veto of the House repeal resolution, she argued that legislative failure does not settle the constitutional question, since a measure repugnant to the Constitution is void regardless of whether it survives a veto fight.[1] The citation illustrates how the case functions in the Truth Community less as a technical precedent on judicial review than as a general assertion that unconstitutional government action lacks legitimate force.[1]

## Invoked against the New Mexico carry suspension

In September 2023, Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] invoked Marbury v. Madison (1803) against New Mexico Governor [[michelle-lujan-grisham|Michelle Lujan Grisham]]'s emergency order suspending public firearms carry, citing the decision's confirmation that all laws repugnant to the Constitution are null and void.[2] From that premise she argued that a governor cannot suspend the [[second-amendment|Second Amendment]], because states have no right to separate citizens from their constitutional rights.[2]
