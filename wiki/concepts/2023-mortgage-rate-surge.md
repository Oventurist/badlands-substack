# 2023 Mortgage Rate Surge

The 2023 mortgage rate surge refers to the rapid rise of United States mortgage borrowing costs in late February and early March 2023, which drove homebuyer mortgage applications to their lowest level in 28 years.[1] According to Bankrate, the average rate on the 30-year fixed mortgage jumped back above 7 percent, reaching 7.06 percent — 7.10 percent by Mortgage News Daily's measure — the highest level since early November 2022.[1]

## Market context

One month earlier, the CME's little-known housing price futures had been read as a signal that housing had bottomed, and the May 2023 Housing Price Futures contract continued to rise, indicating that the market remained optimistic about a bottom.[1] The renewed surge in mortgage rates threatened to spoil that bullish outlook.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] framed the episode as part of "the controlled demolition of the economy," attributing record [[inflation|inflation]] to the volume of money printed during the COVID-19 pandemic combined with a broad loss of confidence in the economy and in government.[1] He argued that the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]'s apparent incompetence in managing the problem "is a ruse," and that international bankers had used sophisticated economic warfare tactics to destabilize economies long before the Fed's creation as a United States institution.[1]

In his account, boom and bust cycles can be artificially generated through a combination of money printing, criminal activity, supply-line interruptions and destabilizing events such as plagues, disasters and fires, with a clear pattern visible in past cycles.[1] He pointed to a contemporary explosion of such destabilizing events — train derailments, a pandemic, lockdowns and cultural disintegration — noting that in 2020 and earlier the Fed flooded some markets with capital while depriving others of credit, producing artificial supply-and-demand differentials that magnified inflationary factors.[1] The other side of the operation, he wrote, was monetary constriction: rising interest rates make money hard to get and reduce the velocity of money, the rate at which money changes hands.[1] Unless the public developed effective parallel systems resting on "a wise and intelligent citizenry," he concluded, the economic warfare and its casualties would continue.[1]
