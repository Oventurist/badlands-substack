# The Enlightenment

The Enlightenment, dated in "Hijacked Consciousness - Sacred Origins" to roughly AD 1650–1800, is treated by [[observing-consciousness|Observing Consciousness]] as the hinge point at which learning began to be severed from the sacred.[1] The essay, the first installment of the [[hijacked-consciousness-series|Hijacked Consciousness]] series published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on July 8, 2025, concedes that the period "brought many gifts" but argues that it also "began the long separation of knowledge from knowing."[1]

## The first fracture

In the article's account, the first fracture "wasn't loud" — it arrived as refinement, as rationalism, as efficiency.[1] Reason was elevated and intuition downgraded; the symbol was demoted and the measurable promoted.[1] Disciplines that had previously "lived in communion" were split apart: mathematics from music, physics from theology, language from energy.[1] Knowledge, no longer sacred, became "content" — measurable, sortable, stackable — and ceased to touch the soul.[1]

## Renaming of time

The author gives particular weight to the calendrical shift of the same era, in which BC and AD — Before Christ and Anno Domini — gave way to BCE and CE.[1] This is described as "secular, sanitized, detached from divinity," a change that presumed the calendar could be neutral and that memory could be rewritten without consequence; the article calls it "a symbolic exorcism of God from time."[1]

## Consequences

The Enlightenment's separations are presented as the precondition for the [[industrial-model-of-schooling|industrial model of schooling]] that followed in the 18th and 19th centuries, and as the beginning of the loss of the initiatory practices described in the essay's account of [[ancient-sacred-learning-traditions|ancient sacred learning traditions]].[1] The author nonetheless holds that remnants survived the transition — old songs lingering in lullabies, the spiral appearing in architecture, and children carrying questions no textbook could answer.[1]
