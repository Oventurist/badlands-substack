# ADHD Medication Cascade

The "drug cascade" describes the pattern in which a child diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and prescribed a stimulant subsequently accumulates additional psychiatric prescriptions to manage the side effects of earlier ones.[1]

## The Gansky case

Danielle Gansky was seven years old when an administrator at her upscale private girls' school in suburban Philadelphia flagged problems with her academic performance; she was bubbly and creative but easily distracted and her schoolwork was sloppy.[1] The school told her mother the girl should see a psychiatrist, who diagnosed ADHD and prescribed a stimulant; fearing Danielle might be expelled if her focus did not improve, her mother agreed in tears.[1] The pills made Gansky agitated, moody and angry, so another doctor put her on Prozac, and more pills followed.[1]

Over the years Gansky was always on two and sometimes three or more psychiatric drugs at once, and by her late twenties had taken 14 different kinds of psychiatric pills — Concerta for inattention, Prozac for moodiness, Lorazepam for anxiety.[1] None of it ever felt right; the drugs dulled her mind and made her irritable or sleepy, and when she complained her doctors would raise the dose or try another medication.[1] "I was living in a body hijacked by the medication," said Gansky, 29, who is still struggling to wean herself off an antidepressant. "I didn't have the words or authority to challenge what I was being told."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that children's brains do not fully form until age 25 and that society warns kids away from drugs and alcohol precisely because such substances alter brain chemistry during that formation — yet "the science" puts seven-year-olds on powerful psychotropic drugs to make them sit still in what she called the Prussian indoctrination factory.[1] She asked why Americans allowed [[big-pharma|Big Pharma]] and the health industries to make "patients for life" a virtue, calling the practice mad science that history will judge harshly.[1]
