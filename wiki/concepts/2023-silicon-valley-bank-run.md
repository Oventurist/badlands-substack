# 2023 Silicon Valley Bank Run

The 2023 Silicon Valley Bank run began on March 9, 2023, when venture capital firms — including some of the biggest names in the industry — began advising their portfolio companies to move money out of [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]] after the bank announced that morning that it intended to sell shares in pursuit of additional capital.[1]

## Onset of the run

A number of investors feared a bank run: enough startups withdrawing capital at once could leave the institution upside-down in terms of deposits versus demand for those funds.[1] Contemporary coverage noted the irony that bank runs are often self-fulfilling prophecies.[1] Because founders who raise millions in venture financing commonly park that capital in an SVB account, even the appearance of risk to those balances could set off a domino effect driven more by fear than by underlying reality.[1]

Some investors publicly defended the bank. Venture investor Mark Suster said on Twitter that he believed SVB's chief executive "when he says they are solvent and not in violation of any banking ratios" and that the goal of the share sale was to raise and strengthen the balance sheet.[1] Rob Go of NextView and Bryce Roberts also spoke up in support of the bank.[1]

## Commentary

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[patriots-in-progress|Patriots in Progress]] likened the global banking system to a dam in which "some major holes are starting to form and gush water," noting that banks across the globe were encountering bank run scenarios and facing default.[1] The commentary attributed the fragility to massive currency creation during COVID, argued that banks were significantly more leveraged than in the 2008 crisis, and predicted worse ramifications.[1]

The piece emphasized that, as of March 15, 2020, the reserve requirement in the United States is 0.0%, so there is no guarantee a bank will have a depositor's currency on request — meaning that absent large injections from governments or central banks, at-risk banks would fail and losses would cascade to creditors whose debts those banks serviced.[1] It further argued that while such interventions can temporarily stop the pain, they exacerbate future inflation by increasing the currency supply.[1] The conclusion drawn was that virtually all currencies are at the end of their life-cycle, in the late phases of exponential growth, at which point the magnitude of debt and interest payments can no longer be concealed as it once was.[1]

## Scale of the withdrawal and insolvency

The [[california-department-of-financial-protection-and-innovation|California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation]] reported that, although the bank had been in sound financial condition prior to March 9, 2023, its announcement of an approximately $1.8 billion loss on a sale of investments — accompanied by a capital raise that ultimately failed — caused investors and depositors to withdraw $42 billion in deposits in a single day, roughly a quarter of the bank's deposits, drained within hours.[2] By the close of business on Thursday, March 9, the bank had a negative cash balance of approximately $958 million.[2] Despite attempts by the bank and regulators to transfer collateral from various sources, it did not meet its cash letter with the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and was declared insolvent.[2]

## Insider conduct and orchestration claims

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued the run may have been an intentional implosion designed to produce a loss of confidence and economic contagion, comparing its propagation to a viral agent.[2] He cited pre-collapse share sales by SVB chief executive [[gregory-becker|Gregory Becker]] and two other executives, bonuses paid before the government seized the bank, and a [[bloomberg|Bloomberg]] report that [[jpmorgan-chase|JP Morgan]] had warned some SVB customers to move their funds citing asset-safety concerns — which he read as a hat tip to [[jp-morgan|J.P. Morgan]], the financier associated with the 1907 bank run that preceded creation of the Federal Reserve system.[2] In that framing the episode was an industry-driven bank run mounted by Silicon Valley venture capitalists, part of a recurring pattern in which the international banking cartel inflates currency through predatory lending, engineers instability, and then arrives with a bailout — here potentially the introduction of [[central-bank-digital-currency|central bank digital currencies]].[2] Deschamps described insider self-dealing before the collapse as a breach of fiduciary duty and urged the development of parallel systems to replace conventional banks.[2]

[[peter-thiel|Peter Thiel]]'s [[founders-fund|Founders Fund]] withdrew millions from SVB and advised its portfolio companies to do the same, a move [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] described as creating an "inside money stampede" of blue-chip investors — while arguing it pointed away from, rather than toward, a planned demolition of the banking system.[2]

## Political and regulatory aftermath

On Sunday, March 12, Treasury Secretary [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] said on *Face the Nation* that the federal government would not bail out SVB's investors and owners as it had systemically large banks during the financial crisis, though it was focused on depositors' needs.[2] That same afternoon, Representative [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] joined a Zoom call with members of [[us-congress|Congress]], the Federal Reserve, the Treasury and the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|FDIC]], during which he reported a Democratic senator asked whether a program existed to censor free speech on social media — a question critics framed as an attempt to suppress information that might trigger further bank runs.[2] Days later [[signature-bank|Signature Bank]], which had closed [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s accounts after January 6, 2021, also failed.[2]
