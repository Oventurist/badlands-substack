# Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board

Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board is a 2010 decision of the Supreme Court of the United States that, among other holdings, addressed the constitutional meaning of the phrase "officer of the United States." In Badlands Media coverage the case appears not for its own subject matter but as the authority invoked against attempts to disqualify [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from presidential ballots under Section 3 of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]].[1]

## Use in the Colorado ballot dispute

In December 2023, following the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]]'s move to strip Trump from the state's ballot, [[vivek-ramaswamy|Vivek Ramaswamy]] published a legal argument on X that [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] quoted in full.[1] Ramaswamy wrote: "And there's another legal problem: Trump is not a former 'officer of the United States,' as that term is used in the Constitution, meaning Section 3 does not apply. As the Supreme Court explained in Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (2010), an 'officer of the United States' is someone appointed by the President to aid him in his duties under Article II, Section 2. The term does not apply to elected officials, and certainly not to the President himself."[1]

The argument turns on the textual structure of Section 3, which disqualifies persons who, having previously taken an oath as specified officeholders, engage in insurrection or rebellion. If the President is not an "officer of the United States" within the constitutional sense identified in Free Enterprise Fund — a person appointed by the President under the Appointments Clause of Article II, Section 2, rather than an official chosen by the electorate — then the disqualification clause does not reach a former President at all, independent of any factual question about January 6.[1]

## Deschamps's commentary

Deschamps endorsed Ramaswamy's reading, writing simply that "Vivek is right again," and paired it with Ramaswamy's separate historical argument that the Reconstruction Amendments were aimed at former Confederate leaders who had plainly waged war against the United States.[1] He cautioned, however, that in his view the [[deep-state|Cabal]] would not be constrained by the precedent, since "they do what they do best — redefine words, terms and ideas to suit their agenda," a practice he said had produced decades of revision to constitutional precepts in areas including gun control, abortion and immigration policy.[1] His conclusion was that there is no legal basis for removing Trump from the ballot, where "legal" means the rules established by the people through the legislative and judicial process, and that the lawful basis for the attempt was weaker still.[1]
