# Debt Ceiling

The debt ceiling is the statutory limit on the total amount the United States federal government may borrow. Congressional Republicans used the ceiling for years as a tool to pressure Democrats into accepting spending cuts, making it a recurring point of leverage in budget negotiations.[1]

## Trump's 2024 demand

During the [[2024-december-continuing-resolution-fight|December 2024 continuing resolution fight]], President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] broke with that Republican tradition by calling for the debt ceiling to be lifted or eliminated entirely before he took office. The demand, injected in the final hours before funding expired, complicated Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]]'s efforts and pushed the government close to a shutdown.[1]

The replacement bill that House leadership brought forward on December 19, 2024 would have lifted the debt ceiling until 2027 alongside a three-month funding extension, a farm bill extension, and $110 billion in disaster relief. It failed, with 38 Republicans voting against it.[1]

## Reception in the Truth Community

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote in [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] that she did not love the idea of raising the debt ceiling but was willing to give Trump the benefit of the doubt if he needed the room for his intended negotiations and actions in 2025 and beyond. She argued that if the transformational change program succeeded and the federal government were "put back in its constitutional box," the debt ceiling should be lowered in years to come as the country returned to sound monetary policy.[1]

## The April 2023 House bill

On 26 April 2023 House Republicans narrowly passed a bill to raise the debt ceiling while cutting spending and dismantling several items in President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s domestic agenda, by a margin of 217 to 215.[2] Democrats voted along party lines and four Republicans voted against it: [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]], [[andy-biggs|Andy Biggs]], [[ken-buck|Ken Buck]] and [[tim-burchett|Tim Burchett]].[2] The legislation would have raised the ceiling for a year in exchange for freezing spending at 2022 levels for a decade — a 14 per cent cut — and rolling back parts of Biden's health, climate and tax law.[2] The measure was considered dead on arrival, since even if it cleared the Democrat-controlled [[us-senate|Senate]] Biden had vowed to veto it, leaving the government facing a potentially catastrophic default within months absent action by [[us-congress|Congress]].[2] The vote followed a late-night Republican negotiation in which Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] agreed to jettison a provision rolling back ethanol tax credits.[2]

Badlands contributor [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] wrote that the showdown would have major implications for the financial collapse the team had been discussing for months, describing the parties as at a stalemate and warning that failure to agree would deal the "already shaky economy" a potential death blow.[2]

## The trillion-dollar coin workaround

Successive debt ceiling standoffs have generated proposals to bypass the limit altogether, the most prominent being the [[trillion-dollar-coin|trillion-dollar coin]] — the suggestion that the Treasury use its platinum coinage authority to mint a coin denominated at $1 trillion and deposit it with the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], funding government operations without incurring new debt.[3] The idea originated on finance blogs in the summer of 2011 as a way for Treasury Secretary Timothy Geithner to avert the ceiling, gained mainstream traction during the 2013 standoff, and was revived in May 2023 by economist [[paul-krugman|Paul Krugman]], who claimed it would not be inflationary.[3] A December 2012 [[business-insider|Business Insider]] endorsement argued it was "even crazier to think that the U.S. would default arbitrarily just because of a dumb law" — the "dumb law" in question being the debt ceiling itself.[3] Treasury Secretary [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] rejected the maneuver as a "gimmick" in January 2023, noting the Fed is under no obligation to accept such a coin.[3]
