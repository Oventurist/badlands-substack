# Rule of Law

The **rule of law** is the principle that a society is governed by known, general rules rather than by arbitrary power. In a May 16, 2023 Badlands Media essay published under the pen name [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]], the concept is examined critically: the author argues that the rule of law "does not exist when the citizens are unable to articulate the very laws under which they are required to operate."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the)

## The central contradiction

The essay opens with the question of why lawyers are needed at all. Citizens are told simultaneously that ordinary people lack the competence to interpret law — hence the necessity of qualified legal professionals — and that those same citizens are nevertheless bound to obey the law competently and voluntarily, on pain of punishment.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) The author contends that these two assumptions are mutually exclusive: if the public is competent to obey the law unaided, professional interpreters are unnecessary; if interpreters are genuinely required, the public cannot fairly be held liable for failing to know the law itself.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the)

From this the essay derives its core thesis: "You cannot have a free society if the very rules that govern that society are indecipherable by the citizens of that society."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) Lawyers and judges alike, it argues, have almost certainly violated some statute or code unknowingly, and no person can plausibly claim mastery of the whole body of enacted law. The example given is the federal revenue code — Title 26 of the United States Code — which agents of the [[internal-revenue-service|Internal Revenue Service]] are expected to enforce in real time without having memorised it, a situation the author likens to playing chess without knowing how the pieces move.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the)

## Law as Russian roulette

Until law becomes both *decipherable* and *accessible* to those obliged to follow it, the essay maintains, legal exposure resembles Russian roulette: virtually any act or omission might be construed as illegal by a sufficiently aggressive advocate.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) The author cites the estimate, drawn from Andrew Łobaczewski's *Political Ponerology*, that clinical psychopaths constitute roughly four percent of the population, while conceding that humane outcomes still occur in the legal system — the objection is that no guarantee of such outcomes exists.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the)

## Caveat regarding practitioners

The piece explicitly declines to condemn the legal profession wholesale, saluting those who entered it "for the right reasons, not knowing the true nature of the game."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) It argues that the system survives precisely because people of good conscience inside it make it appear to deliver order and justice, and that reform is impossible without them. The stated target is "the system itself—not necessarily the individuals 'inside' of it," excepting those who knowingly misuse it to cause harm.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the)

## Civic renaissance as remedy

The essay calls for "a renaissance in civic awareness," warning that when law is subverted to serve aims other than the defense of legitimate rights, it becomes "the greatest source of tyranny."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) Because no individual can track the tens of thousands of pages of new law enacted regularly, the author proposes a collective, crowd-sourced database of the legal system's "most blatant absurdities," using the metaphor of passengers on a leaking boat locating and plugging holes together.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) Widespread knowledge is framed as the safeguard, since — in the essay's formulation — the [[deep-state|Deep State]] "requires our collective ignorance to operate unopposed."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the) The article closes with the maxim that the law belongs to the people, but only knowledge and vigilance can keep it so.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-startling-contradiction-at-the)

## Deschamps: the deliberately distorted phrase

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] in March 2023, [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that the phrase "rule of law" has taken on many meanings over the years and that this is not an accident — its meaning has been intentionally distorted so that the power of the ideas behind it can be obscured.[4] The reason offered is that law in its best form is a blueprint for a *perfecting* civilization rather than a perfect one, the latter being the communist utopia; because all law comes from God and man is finite, man is forever reaching for the blueprint.[4]

On this reading the cabal, globalists or deep state have had to attack the concept of law for a very long time because it is the single biggest threat to their shallow and false claims to power, sustained by mass mind control so effective that most people would reject the ultimate solution if it were handed to them.[4] Deschamps offered a working definition: the rule of law simply means that the *law is clear, publicized, stable, and applied evenly* — and stated flatly that this is not what exists in the world today.[4]

He presented [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] as one of very few elected officials who grasps the point, citing Trump's statements that respect for the rule of law is at the country's core and that it is "the cornerstone of our Freedom," and distinguishing the rule of law from the man-made [[legal-system|legal system]], which a promised sequel would characterize as a [[color-of-law|color of law]] system.[4]

## "Rule of law" as a mechanism of control (DeLarme, 2025)

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] analyzed "the rule of law" as a phrase with a surface definition that disarms the public and an unspoken operational meaning.[2] He contrasted the conventional account — quoted from Google's Gemini, that the rule of law ensures all individuals, institutions and entities are accountable to laws publicly promulgated, equally enforced and independently adjudicated, with no one above the law — against what he called the real meaning: the use of judicial systems at home and abroad to retain control of the levers of power.[2]

His reasoning was that if law rather than a monarch or oligarchy governs, then the true rulers are those able to write, interpret or change the law — legislators, judges, prosecutors, and the financiers to whom they are indebted.[2] He pointed to [[george-soros|Soros]]-funded district attorneys and prosecutors including [[alvin-bragg|Alvin Bragg]] in Manhattan, George Gascón in Los Angeles and Kim Foxx in Cook County as illustrations, describing a race to control court systems, judges and prosecutors.[2]

Beyond prosecutors, DeLarme identified operatives who build legal frameworks, run "rule of law programs" and organize [[lawfare|lawfare]] campaigns.[2] He characterized these programs as control over a vast international network of judges and prosecutors, administered through [[usaid|USAID]], the [[state-department|U.S. State Department]] and the [[united-states-institute-of-peace|U.S. Institute of Peace]].[2] Figures he named as fitting the mold included elections lawyer [[marc-elias|Marc Elias]], Media Matters founder [[david-brock|David Brock]], ACLU executive director [[anthony-romero|Anthony Romero]], and above all [[norm-eisen|Norm Eisen]], whose "rule of law" work as U.S. Ambassador to the Czech Republic — including collaboration in Prague with Chief Justice [[john-roberts|John Roberts]] on "rule-of-law issues" — he presented as evidence of the term's operational sense.[2]

The article placed "rule of law" alongside [[democracy-promotion|democracy promotion]] and "democracy" itself as part of a whole-of-society system hidden in plain sight, urging readers confronted with the phrase to ask: whose law?[2]

## The "true rule of law" as God's will (Deschamps, 2023)

In the April 10, 2023 feature "Trump Did It," Badlands Media contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] advanced a theological account of the rule of law, defining it as "the method or technique by which man seeks to rule using God's Will—we try to run our civilization the way God would."[3] He asked readers to imagine that God had written a manual for how human beings should live on Earth, one that would produce "a perfecting society" if followed; the rule of law, he wrote, *is that manual*.[3] When societies embrace it, as he said the American founders did, freedom and prosperity follow; when it is eroded and replaced by the [[color-of-law|color of law]], those goods break down.[3]

Deschamps grounded the claim in the changelessness of God: if God is infinite and eternal, God's will must be unwavering, and the observable universe likewise shows endless change governed by an unchanging set of [[natural-law|natural]] and moral laws.[3] He used the image of a standing wave, in which the nodes remain fixed while the wave moves, to picture the relationship between a changeless God and a changing creation.[3] Physical laws such as Newtonian gravitation and Coulomb's law were offered as instances of that constancy, and the [fine-tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-tuned_universe) of the universe as evidence that atheistic appeals to inherent laws or to a multiverse fail to explain why such laws exist at all.[3]

Among the principles he identified as constitutive of the rule of law is that "the law applies to all equally and fairly," so that a king, a homeless person, a business mogul, a celebrity, a judge or the pope alike face the same due process, with penalties suited to the situation rather than lax for the powerful and harsh for the dissident.[3] The purpose of justice, on this account, is "to restore order and fair play in society once more," a function he compared to a referee penalizing a foul so that the offended team can set aside the urge for revenge and resume play.[3]

### Case-by-case application

Deschamps argued that although the principles of God's method are consistent — he cited the golden rule, "love thy neighbor as you love yourself" — their application must always be case by case, because "a rubber stamp process can't be used" when every person and situation differs.[3] True justice, he wrote, must both make victims whole and motivate the perpetrator toward redemption, mimicking the corrective methods of a loving father.[3] A boot-camp intervention that reforms one troubled fifteen-year-old, he noted, may leave another feeling abandoned and resentful.[3] Blind application of principled ideas — what he termed ideologies — "often results in tyrannical governments and totalitarian cultures."[3]

### Law, order and contract

Quoting his earlier feature "Trump Knows," Deschamps described the laws of contracts, agreements, trust, duty and stewardship as "the pillars upon which all social groups operate," and the legal system as man's instrument for bringing order and stability to civilization.[3] He illustrated the ordering function with a Chladni-style vibrating plate, on which scattered sand assumes coherent shapes once vibration is applied, and paralleled it with Genesis 1:1–3, in which God brings order to a formless creation.[3] A contract, he argued, is "a special link between man's will and God's Will": property transfers only by contract, all contracts carry terms and conditions, and violating them places a party "in a state of dishonor."[3] God's covenant with man is presented as the archetype of that structure.[3]

### The wise king

On this reasoning, a tyrant king who changes the rules to suit his ambitions and to advantage some citizens over others produces a corrupt society lacking the rule of law, since laws must be "clear, publicized, stable, and applied evenly."[3] A wise king instead mimics God's way, making laws that apply equally to all, in which no one is above justice or unfairly prosecuted, a coin of the realm buys the same goods everywhere, and every person enjoys equal protection of unalienable rights — the king serving as a humble steward of those rights rather than their master.[3] Deschamps applied the model directly to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], whom he cast as a wise king and patient steward preparing the world to rediscover the true rule of law, an effort he tied to accelerating the [[great-awakening|Great Awakening]].[3]

## Deschamps's formula (June 2023)

In "Trump's 2025 Vision," [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] stated his thesis as an equation: **Rule of Law + Righteous Population = Prosperity, Abundance, and Peace**.[5] He defined the rule of law as the method by which a society encourages individuals to honor the rights of others, and argued that this in turn produces everything true, beautiful, and good about civilization.[5] Where citizens follow a system of law aligned with actual truth, he wrote, criminal behavior disappears, hardship evaporates, and prosperity spills over to all people.[5]

Deschamps held that no nation on earth currently exhibits a fully expressed rule-of-law civilization; the early American Republic came closest, and even the 1950s "golden age" was a paltry example of what is possible, though paradisiacal relative to the communist states of the Cold War.[5] He argued that the [[globalism|globalist]] agenda wages war on the rule of law specifically, and that [[lawfare|lawfare]] is its inversion — a system designed to shield criminals, deepen dependency, and prosecute opponents.[5] He also pointed to the 1850s United States as an outlier in which the citizen was treated as the fundamental unit of freedom and rights were understood as God-given, in contrast to nations placing government and elites at the top of the rights hierarchy.[5]

## Political prosecution as a stress test

Badlands commentary treats the prosecution of political opponents as the clearest test of whether the rule of law is real. In an August 2023 retrospective on the [[mar-a-lago-raid|Mar-a-Lago raid]], [[alpha-warrior|Alpha Warrior]] wrote that in a democracy the rule of law should be an unwavering beacon of fairness and justice, free from the taint of political agendas, and that the specter of imprisoning citizens or presidential candidates because of political bias raises profound concerns about the integrity of the justice system and the essence of democratic values.[6]

He argued that such prosecutions endanger constitutional civil liberties — free speech, assembly and political participation — because citizens come to fear that dissenting views will make them targets, and that they chip away at public trust in the institutions meant to uphold law, fracturing societal cohesion when the system is perceived to bend toward one group.[6] The remedies he proposed centered on [[judicial-independence|judicial independence]], transparent independent investigations, law-enforcement reform, whistleblower protection, a free press, empowered civil society, statutory review of arrest and detention powers, and above all [[electoral-integrity|electoral integrity]].[6]
