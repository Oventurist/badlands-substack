# Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act

Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) is the statutory authority allowing US intelligence agencies — including the [[fbi|FBI]] and the National Security Agency — to conduct warrantless surveillance targeting foreign individuals abroad.[1] Because the program can also incidentally collect communications involving Americans, it is regarded as one of the most powerful and controversial post-9/11 surveillance authorities.[1]

## The 2026 renewal fight

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] urged [[us-congress|Congress]] to pass an extension of Section 702, calling on Republican lawmakers to unify behind what he described as a critical national security tool ahead of a looming expiration deadline.[1] Trump backed a "clean" extension of roughly eighteen months, arguing the program was essential to military and intelligence successes, including operations tied to countries such as [[iran|Iran]] and Venezuela.[1]

The push collided with bipartisan resistance in Congress, where civil liberties advocates and lawmakers from both parties demanded reforms such as requiring warrants before searching Americans' data and tightening oversight of intelligence agencies.[1] The debate reflected a sharp reversal from Trump's earlier criticism of FISA authorities, as the program faced a hard renewal deadline amid continuing concerns about past misuse, including improper surveillance of Americans and political figures.[1]

## 45-day extension (2026)

On May 1, 2026, the House and Senate approved a 45-day extension of Section 702 of the [[foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act|Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act]], preserving the federal government's warrantless surveillance authority just hours before it was set to expire.[2] The short-term fix followed the Senate's rejection of a House-passed three-year extension that had included a [[central-bank-digital-currency|central bank digital currency]] ban drawing bipartisan opposition.[2] The measure passed 261-111 and went to [[donald-trump|President Trump]] for signature.[2]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] used the episode to criticize the accompanying partial government shutdown drama as having lost its impact, noting that DHS sources had told her ICE and CBP were fully funded in the OBBB.[2] She questioned why a Republican-controlled House would attempt to attach a CBDC provision to a DHS funding package after Trump had said there would never be a central bank digital currency, suggesting lawmakers remained "owned and controlled by the invisible enemy" and acted against the will of their constituents.[2]

## Lapse and the DNI standoff (June 2026)

Section 702 lapsed in mid-June 2026, and efforts to reauthorize it became entangled with the fight over the directorship of national intelligence.[3] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said Republicans had agreed to remove [[bill-pulte|Bill Pulte]] as acting DNI in exchange for Democratic support on reauthorization, but claimed Democrats later indicated they would oppose the measure, writing on Truth Social that "The Republicans wound up having fulfilled their commitment, but Dumocrats broke the Deal."[3] He responded by canceling [[jay-clayton|Jay Clayton]]'s Senate confirmation hearing for DNI, further complicating reauthorization efforts.[3] Trump added that he would not approve reauthorization without the [[save-america-act|SAVE AMERICA Act]] attached, arguing that Republicans had "fallen into a trap."[3]

## Hasbajrami ruling (January 2025)

In a decision reported on January 23, 2025, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York ruled in [[united-states-v-hasbajrami|United States v. Hasbajrami]] that warrantless searches conducted under Section 702 violate the Fourth Amendment — the first time a court had made such a determination regarding the provision, which critics say enables warrantless surveillance of Americans including protesters, journalists, and members of Congress.[4] The case centered on [[fbi|FBI]] queries during the investigation of defendant Agron Hasbajrami, in which the government initially concealed its use of Section 702 surveillance, reversing course only after revelations about the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]]'s practice of hiding such surveillance in criminal cases.[4] The ruling built on a 2019 decision by the Second Circuit Court of Appeals holding that Section 702 queries involving individuals in the United States constitute searches requiring Fourth Amendment scrutiny.[4]

## The April 2024 House reauthorization

On Friday, April 12, 2024, the US House of Representatives voted 273 to 147 to reauthorize Section 702 through 2026, ahead of the authority's scheduled April 19 expiration.[5] The measure did not head to the Senate immediately: right after passage, Representative [[anna-paulina-luna|Anna Paulina Luna]] (R-Fla.) objected and requested a vote on the motion to reconsider the legislation, meaning the bill could not advance until the House voted to table that motion the following week.[5]

Contemporary reporting described Section 702 as allowing the US government to collect the electronic communications of non-Americans located outside the country without a warrant, and noted that it had come under scrutiny from lawmakers on both sides of the aisle and from civil liberties groups because it sometimes results in the collection of data on Americans who are in contact with surveilled individuals.[5] An amendment to add a warrant requirement for querying Americans' data narrowly failed on a tied 212 to 212 vote.[5]

## Short-term extension in the FY2024 NDAA

On Thursday, December 14, 2023, the U.S. House of Representatives passed the annual defense policy bill containing a measure extending Section 702 of the [[foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act|Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act]] until mid-April 2024, punting the question of structural reform into the new year.[6] The provision, folded into the [[national-defense-authorization-act|National Defense Authorization Act]], averted an end-of-year expiration of the surveillance authority while prolonging the dispute over whether the government should obtain a warrant before querying already-collected communications belonging to Americans.[6] Section 702 permits the government to target foreigners located abroad, but Americans who communicate with those targets have their information swept up in the searches — a dynamic that produced broad agreement that reauthorization should be paired with limits on documented abuse of the tool by the [[fbi|FBI]].[6] Several conservative lawmakers objected to the authority's inclusion in a must-pass bill.[6]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that "this is how liberty dies," noting the new extension ran until at least April 19 and arguing that [[congress|Congress]] had decided "the need to surveil you outweighs your constitutional rights."[6] The commentary observed that 118 House Republicans opposed the measure and were eager to advertise that fact for fundraising purposes, describing the episode as an illustration of the [[uniparty|uniparty]]: "Democracy dies in darkness, but liberty dies under the bright lights of a Congressional floor vote."[6]
