# Language of Control

"Language of control" is the term used by Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] for the vocabulary of the Western political establishment — the network of institutions and operatives who maintain the status quo — in which ordinary English words carry a steganographic or dual meaning.[1] On the surface the language looks like ordinary English, but certain words and phrases mean one thing to the general public and something quite different to those using them.[1]

## "Democracy"

The primary example is the word "democracy," and the derived phrases [[democracy-promotion|"democracy promotion"]] and "dangerous to *our* democracy."[1] To the uninitiated, democracy denotes a form of government in which political power is vested in the people; for the ruling class, DeLarme argued, it refers specifically to their own rule.[1] He called this "the perfect cover," because hearing "rule by the people" fills ordinary listeners with warm feelings and lulls them into believing they are actively shaping their nation's fate.[1]

DeLarme dated the popularization of "danger to our democracy" to the spread of populist sentiment across the West, particularly after [[brexit|BREXIT]] in the United Kingdom and the rise of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in the United States.[1] The "our" in the phrase, on his reading, is not collective but proprietary — the establishment's democracy, secured by its ability to steer the public.[1] He added that one need not admire [[nigel-farage|Nigel Farage]] or Trump to observe that establishment institutions are working to impede an anti-establishment, anti-globalist populist tide over which they are losing control.[1]

He also allowed a counterfactual: if the population were educated about and resilient to the emotional and psychological manipulation directed at it, democracy would probably function far more effectively and fairly than it does.[1]

## "Rule of law" and other terms

The second major example is [[rule-of-law|"the rule of law"]], whose surface definition — universal accountability to publicly promulgated, equally enforced and independently adjudicated law — disarms the public when figures such as [[norm-eisen|Norm Eisen]] discuss "rule-of-law programs" on [[msnbc|MSNBC]].[1] Its unspoken definition, DeLarme argued, is the use of judicial systems at home and abroad to retain control of the levers of power.[1]

These terms function within what he called the [[whole-of-society-approach|whole-of-society]] system, in which the machinery of influence is largely hidden in plain sight, obscured by vague jargon rather than by secrecy.[1]

## Conclusion

DeLarme concluded that words trusted to represent collective power are being weaponized to preserve the dominance of a few, and that operatives backed by think tanks, NGOs and government agencies use them to entrench a global corporatocracy — from [[color-revolution|color revolutions]] abroad to [[lawfare|lawfare]] at home.[1] The antidote he proposed is awareness and resistance: demanding transparency, rejecting manipulative narratives, holding institutions accountable in everyday discourse as well as at the ballot box, and, on encountering the phrases "democracy promotion" or "rule of law," asking "Whose democracy? Whose law?"[1]
