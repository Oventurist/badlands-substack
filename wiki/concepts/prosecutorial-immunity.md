# Prosecutorial Immunity

Prosecutorial immunity is the legal doctrine shielding prosecutors from civil liability for actions taken in their official capacity. It became a live political issue in April 2023 when House Republicans began rallying around proposals to strip that protection from local district attorneys they accused of political prosecutions.[1]

## The 2023 House Republican proposal

For months prior, House Republicans had criticized leftist local prosecutors for releasing violent felons while pursuing political opponents such as [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[1] Representative [[austin-scott|Austin Scott]] of Georgia, a member of the House Intelligence Committee, said Republicans were considering "prosecutorial misconduct and whether or not prosecutors in this country should be exempt from liability," explicitly framing the effort as reversing the debate liberals had begun years earlier when they sought to abolish the qualified immunity protecting police officers from lawsuits.[1] House Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] said Congress would take action against Manhattan District Attorney [[alvin-bragg|Alvin Bragg]] after Bragg charged Trump following months of downgrading violent felonies in other cases.[1]

## Critique from Badlands

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] offered an extended historical critique, arguing that the legal system was hijacked long ago and that the most recent sweeping change came in the mid-1800s with a slow transition from common law to equity law, both resting on a presumption of the people's incompetence.[1] That presumption, he wrote, produced a "fast food style" justice system and the modern culture of frivolous litigation, while allowing certain actors to operate without legal pushback for harms inflicted on the public.[1]

Deschamps cited the Ford Pinto case, in which the manufacturer allegedly declined a costly recall for a defect that could cause fires in rear-end collisions and instead paid claims from those who filed suit correctly, leaving many injured parties without justice because the system had no proactive teeth.[1] He also described slip-and-fall suits capable of bankrupting a named business owner, arguing that the open capacity for lawsuits is precisely why immunity clauses were written into the incorporation documents used by judges, district attorneys, and elected officials.[1]

On the GOP proposal itself, Deschamps predicted that removing immunity from politicized prosecutors would produce a tidal wave of suits and questioned whether the emotional satisfaction would outweigh the resulting problems.[1] He judged the change unlikely because the two-tiered justice system depends on certain actors being able to cheat the system through immunity the general public does not enjoy.[1] Immunity, he argued, is by definition anti–rule of law, since justice is supposed to apply to all equally and fairly, and genuine reform would require a citizen-led branch ensuring that citizens are properly educated and competent at law and capable of identifying unlawful policies such as immunity and providing effective pushback.[1]
