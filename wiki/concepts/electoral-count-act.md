# Electoral Count Act

The Electoral Count Act is an 1887 federal law that spells out how presidential electoral votes are to be cast and counted.[1] Enacted in the aftermath of the disputed presidential election of 1876, the statute remained largely untouched for more than a century before becoming a central point of contention in litigation arising from the 2020 presidential election.[1]

## Role in the Arizona electors prosecution

The Act became the pivot of a May 2025 ruling by Maricopa County Superior Court Judge Sam J. Myers, who ordered Arizona prosecutors to return to a grand jury the case charging Republicans over their alleged roles in attempting to overturn the 2020 election.[1] Myers found that prosecutors had failed to provide the grand jury with the text of the 1887 law, which was central to the defendants' defense.[1] Attorney [[stephen-binhak|Stephen Binhak]], who spearheaded the effort to send the case back to a grand jury, said, "We are extremely pleased with the court's ruling, and we think the judge got it exactly right."[1] The ruling required Arizona Attorney General [[kris-mayes|Kris Mayes]] to assemble a new grand jury and present the case along with a copy of the Electoral Count Act.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the omission meant prosecutors had misled the grand jury, contending that the pre-reform text of the law rendered the alternate elector slates "entirely lawful" and, in her view, duty-bound.[1] She noted that Congress subsequently "fixed the loophole" in the statute, but that the grand jury was never told the original law authorized the conduct being charged, characterizing the omission as an abuse of the grand jury for political purposes and asking why prosecutors were not sanctioned.[1] She further observed that five states — Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada and Wisconsin — initiated criminal prosecutions related to the 2020 alternate electors, and suggested defendants in those states should investigate whether their grand juries were similarly deprived of the statutory text.[1]
