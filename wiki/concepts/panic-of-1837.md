# Panic of 1837

The Panic of 1837 was a severe American financial crisis that Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presents as the direct consequence of a transatlantic struggle over gold and silver reserves between [[andrew-jackson|President Andrew Jackson]] and the British banking establishment.[1]

## Catalyst

Jackson had dissolved the [[second-bank-of-the-united-states|Second Bank of the United States]], paid off the entire national debt in 1835 using import duties and public land sales, and then acted to disperse the resulting surplus away from the financial centre.[1] The [[deposit-act-of-1836|Deposit Act of 1836]] ordered distribution of the federal surplus to state banks around the country, transferring precious metals from the coast to the interior; monetary reserves in New York City deposit banks fell from $7.2 million in September 1836 to $1.5 million in May 1837.[1] The [[specie-circular|Specie Circular]], an executive order issued the same year, required all public land sales over 320 acres to be paid in specie, similarly diverting precious metals from east to west.[1]

British financiers began expressing alarm in 1836.[1] When the directors of the [[bank-of-england|Bank of England]] noticed that their specie reserves had dwindled to just four million pounds they adopted restrictive measures, blaming the high value of American securities purchased in London.[1] From August 1836 the Bank of England raised its discount rate from three to five percent and stopped discounting commercial paper from the merchant banking houses financing Anglo-American trade; those houses in turn ceased accepting the foreign bills of exchange American importers had used to pay for British goods, so that American merchants could send only specie to Britain.[1] Lange characterizes this as a trade war over real money and control, and as the catalyst of the panic.[1]

## Trigger

In May 1837 New York banks suspended specie payments, prompting banks nationwide to do the same; suspension generally meant refusing to redeem credit instruments at full face value rather than permanent closure.[1] Lange identifies [[citibank|Citibank]] — then the largest bank in New York — as the trigger, arguing that its suspension of full-face-value redemption in gold and silver caused a currency crisis and a banking run.[1] Citibank and its peers were over-leveraged and lacked sufficient specie to cover their liabilities, leaving the bank in need of what Lange calls its first bailout.[1]

## Consequences

[[john-jacob-astor|John Jacob Astor]], the wealthiest man in the country, deposited substantial funds in the bank and thereby took control of it, installing [[moses-taylor|Moses Taylor]] on its board of directors.[1] The panic was among the worst years ever for New York City property values: many residents lost homes to foreclosure and the state granted a one-year grace period on mortgage payments.[1] The economist Milton Friedman reportedly judged the five-year crisis comparable in severity to the Great Depression.[1] Astor bought $224,000 in Manhattan real estate during the freefall and later foreclosed on scores of properties when rates climbed to seven percent.[1] Lange generalizes from this that financial elites cause panics, recessions, and depressions and profit by buying assets at fire-sale prices while the majority suffer.[1]
