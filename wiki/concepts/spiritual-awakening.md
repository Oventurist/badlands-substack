# Spiritual Awakening

**Spiritual awakening**, as described in the March 1, 2025 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay "The Bridge: Awakening, Healing, and Ascension" by [[observing-consciousness|Observing Consciousness]], is a process in which a person begins to perceive that the world "does not feel the same anymore" — hearing things that call previously settled knowledge into question and noticing patterns that were always present but never fully recognised.[1] The essay presents it as simultaneously disorienting, painful and deeply personal, yet also "the beginning of something extraordinary."[1]

## Reported symptoms

The article catalogues experiences reported by the author and by "dozens more" people: unease while watching the nightly news, a sense that ordinary conversations now carry a different weight, and the feeling that trusted things are slightly off.[1] Physically and emotionally, people describe heightened sensitivity to noise, lights or certain foods, sudden dizziness, ringing in the ears, unexplained restlessness, and changing preferences in music, food and social interaction.[1] Anger, embarrassment, shame, sadness and grief are named as common accompaniments, which the essay frames not as pathology but as markers of a deep internal shift in awareness.[1]

Alongside the disturbance the author reports glimpses of hope, "like a soft hum in the background of your awareness," and says that as the brain shifts into more relaxed, open states a person may notice a newfound clarity, "as if the fog is beginning to lift."[1]

## The mind–body disconnect

The essay argues that awakening is not only about seeing through external illusions but about healing internal fractures, holding that society conditions people to separate from their bodies, suppress their emotions and disconnect from their own consciousness.[1] It asserts that governments, corporations and "bad actors" have worked to keep people fragmented and in fear, feeding cycles of stress and control, and that when people feel unsafe they seek external authority to protect them, further deepening those control structures.[1]

This disconnection is described as accumulating gradually rather than all at once: people are conditioned to distrust their instincts, numbed by distraction and kept in perpetual stress so that they do not question authority, while shifting rules and self-contradicting narratives train them to dismiss their own unease as paranoia.[1] The essay calls this "a slow erosion of self-trust, designed to keep individuals dependent, disconnected, and compliant," and states that the more fragmented people become, the easier it is for external forces to dictate what they think, feel and believe.[1]

## Scientific framing

The author insists that healing and ascension "are not just spiritual ideas" but are supported by psychology, neurobiology and an understanding of the mind–body connection, offering language for what many intuitively feel but struggle to articulate.[1] [[bessel-van-der-kolk|Bessel van der Kolk]]'s *The Body Keeps the Score* is cited for the claim that trauma reorganises how the mind and brain manage perceptions, so that a brain under constant fear rewires itself to anticipate danger even in safety.[1] [[peter-levine|Peter Levine]]'s *Waking the Tiger* supplies the account of trauma as something held inside when there is no empathetic witness, lingering in the nervous system and keeping a person locked in survival mode, with a suppressed vagus nerve preventing the body from feeling safe.[1]

[[joe-dispenza|Joe Dispenza]]'s *Breaking the Habit of Being Yourself* is used for the proposition that the brain begins to change the moment a person decides to think, act or feel differently, and adapts to whatever it is repeatedly exposed to.[1] [[bruce-lipton|Bruce Lipton]]'s *The Biology of Belief* supplies the claim that changing perception rewrites the body's chemistry, and [[david-hawkins|David Hawkins]]'s *Power vs. Force* the definition of wisdom as "the discernment of truth from falsehood."[1]

## Practices and progression

Reintegration is described as beginning with small shifts: taking a walk, listening to resonant music, cooking with full attention, drawing, writing, playing an instrument, learning a skill or visiting somewhere new.[1] Deeper practices follow — breathwork to stimulate the vagus nerve, time in nature to sync the nervous system with the earth's rhythms, and cold therapy and fasting said to reset the body's communication systems at a cellular level — alongside [[somatic-experiencing|Somatic Experiencing]].[1]

As the nervous system stabilises, the essay says, emotions become more manageable, intuition sharpens, discernment strengthens, and a person becomes able to pause and respond rather than react.[1] Outwardly, relationships may shift as people outgrow people-pleasing and codependency, and appetite for fear-driven narratives diminishes.[1]

## Collective dimension

The article extends the individual process to a collective one, arguing that trauma is what keeps society in cycles of war, division and external control, and that healing is what allows people to reclaim power, sovereignty and intuitive wisdom.[1] On this view, the more people step into such awareness, the less power fear-based systems hold — which the author offers as the reason oppressive systems work so hard to keep populations afraid, "because fear is control."[1]

The closing section, "Walking Across the Bridge," addresses the reader standing with "one foot in the world you have always known and the other stepping into new awareness," insisting there is no right or wrong way to navigate it, no finish line, and that discomfort is a sign that something is shifting rather than that something is wrong.[1]

## The liminal stage

A later stage of the same process is treated in the April 5, 2025 Badlands Media feature "The Liminal," which describes what follows once the initial awakening and its emotional unraveling have passed.[2] The essay tells readers that they are by then "no longer asleep, but not fully awake in the way you imagined," and that the quiet which sets in is "not peaceful, necessarily, but eerily still."[2] It names this interval [[liminality|the liminal]] — "a space between old and new, between letting go and arriving."[2]

In this stage, the article says, titles, identities, routines and even spiritual practices lose their charge, dreams turn vivid or symbolic, social withdrawal becomes common, and emotion swings between hope and inexplicable grief — all read as "echoes of identity shedding" rather than mood disorder.[2] Relationships strain because the person is "no longer broadcasting from fear, need, or ego," so that some connections fade or break as a matter of alignment.[2] The essay's counsel is to stop demanding proof of progress: "your mind might scream, 'Do something!' But your soul whispers, 'Just be.'"[2]
