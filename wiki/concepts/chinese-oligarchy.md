# Chinese Oligarchy

The **Chinese oligarchy** is the term used by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] for the entrenched ruling class of the People's Republic of China, described in his ongoing China series as a "dragon-blooded aristocracy" whose insidious and daunting origins and power structures trace back to the Ming Dynasty and beyond.[1]

## Characterization

In the December 2022 feature "CHIPS and Dips," Burning Bright argued that [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] has seemingly waged war on the "tigers and flies" of this oligarchy rather than serving as its heir, contrary to the portrait of Xi offered by both liberal and conservative figures in the west.[1] He held that rooting out institutionalized corruption in China would be an especially formidable task for sovereigns, even by comparison with the corruption infesting the west, and that moves to destabilize such power structures would need to be as impactful and far-reaching as they are carefully managed.[1]

## Continuity with western oligarchies

A central claim of the essay is that the Chinese oligarchy shares more than common interests with the American, Russian and European oligarchies — that they are "one in the same."[1] Where family bloodlines and political legacies dominate the ruling class and culture of the far east, Burning Bright wrote, corporations, logos and stock ticker acronyms perform the same function in the far west, with no meaningful difference between [[apple|Apple]], [[amazon|Amazon]], [[google|Google]] and other U.S. Fortune 5 companies or between them and "foreign" corporations such as [[samsung-electronics|Samsung]] and Volkswagen.[1]

Whatever their original intent and function, he argued, subversive globalist policies allowing these firms to benefit from engineered wars, front-run emerging crises and use slave labor and cheap communist distribution to serve comparatively rich consumer bases had converted them into "kingdoms unto themselves."[1] While American citizens saw purchasing power suppressed over decades by these practices, he wrote, the harms visited on the Chinese people were "sobering to behold" — a dynamic he tied to the accelerating unrest in China, including riots at the plant in the city "named after the iPhone itself" and pushback against draconian lockdown measures.[1]
