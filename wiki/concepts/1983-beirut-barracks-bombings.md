# 1983 Beirut Barracks Bombings

Early on the morning of Sunday, October 23, 1983, two truck bombs struck buildings in Beirut, Lebanon housing American and French service members of the Multinational Force in Lebanon, a peacekeeping operation during the Lebanese Civil War. The attack killed 307 people: 241 U.S. and 58 French military personnel, six civilians, and the two attackers.[1] The last time the Marines had lost that many men in a single day was the storming of the beaches of Iwo Jima.[1]

## Context

Lebanon was in a civil war for control of the country, and the United States had sent Marines as part of a multinational "peace-keeping force." [[hezbollah]] was among the factions fighting for control, and the Marines were nominally there to help prevent bloodshed.[1] A suicide bombing of the U.S. embassy in Beirut had already occurred in April of the same year.[1]

## Rules of engagement

To avoid being perceived as an occupying force, the Marines had been ordered to shelter in place on the south side of the Beirut airport, building makeshift bunkers out of sandbags that cast long shadows across an open expanse of dirt and asphalt, providing easy marks for gunmen.[1] Their rules of engagement, handed down from the [[pentagon]], required a noncombat presence: no heavily fortified bunkers, nothing more than concertina wire to mark the compound, and — in what struck many of the men as sheer madness — no loaded weapons, including for the guards posted to protect the outpost.[1]

## Missed and withheld warnings

Later investigations showed there were many clues that went unnoticed and dots the intelligence agencies and the White House never connected. Since May, U.S. intelligence agencies had received more than one hundred warnings of car bombs in Lebanon, and the military chain of command was regularly briefed about the widening threat to the Marines. The Pentagon never allowed the Marines to take more defensive positions and had essentially turned them into "sitting ducks."[1]

After the spring embassy attack, [[fbi|FBI]] forensic investigators discovered that the bombers had laden their explosives with ordinary pressurized gas bottles, which magnified the force of the blast. The fact that terrorists were enhancing conventional explosives with everyday materials was never made known to the military commanders in Beirut, because the FBI never disseminated its report; it stayed locked within the [[cia|CIA]] and the [[state-department|State Department]].[1]

The most fateful signal came in late September, when the [[national-security-agency|National Security Agency]] intercepted a message from the Iranian Ministry of Information and Security to the Iranian ambassador in Syria. The ministry ordered the ambassador to contact [[hussein-musawi|Hussein Musawi]], head of the Islamic group [[amal-movement|Amal]], instructing him to turn his sights on the multinational forces in Lebanon and mount a "spectacular action against the United States Marines."[1] The Beirut airport was the only place to launch such an attack, making the intercept the clearest indication yet that the Marines sat in the crosshairs; but owing to a cumbersome military chain of command and an inexplicable failure to grasp the urgency, the message was not delivered to senior military officials until two days after the bombing.[1]

## Aftermath: reorganization of crisis management

In reaction to the Beirut attack, and a subsequent bombing of the new U.S. embassy nearly a year later, [[john-poindexter|John Poindexter]] led a massive reorganization of the government's crisis management teams. He formed an alliance with his friend [[william-casey|Bill Casey]], the CIA Director, with whom he had developed an honest rapport and to whom he felt he could speak frankly.[1] The CIA set up a secure hotline connecting the State Department, the NSA, the Defense Intelligence Agency and the [[national-security-council|NSC]] staff with the CIA's photographic intelligence center — the government's primary imagery-analysis resource, which had previously had no data links in or out.[1]

Poindexter also took over an existing NSC outfit, the Crisis-Pre Planning Group, and turned it into the engine of the government's antiterrorism campaign, powered for the first time by modern information technologies designed to capture information and route it to those who needed it.[1] In retrospect he was planting the seeds of a much broader, pervasive and ultimately global system of information gathering; two decades later, after the [[september-11-attacks|9/11 attacks]], he would help lead the Defense Department's [[total-information-awareness|Total Information Awareness]] program, which bore a striking resemblance to modern NSA global surveillance.[1]

## Interpretation in the article

Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues the bombing was the catalyst the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] needed to move from CIA control of the [[presidents-daily-brief|Presidential Daily Brief]] to CIA control over information flowing to the entire executive branch.[1] He asks why, if the United States knew Iran was behind the killing of 241 Marines, it would negotiate to sell arms to Iran only a year later — concluding that protecting the cocaine trade through Nicaragua and consolidating information control mattered more than protecting the Marines, who in his account were pawns meant to be sacrificed.[1] The episode, in this reading, also supplied the fear needed to launch a never-ending [[war-on-terror|war on terror]] with Iran and its proxies as the new enemy.[1]
