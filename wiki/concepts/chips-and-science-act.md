# CHIPS and Science Act

The CHIPS and Science Act is U.S. legislation providing federal funding and lending authority to strengthen domestic semiconductor and advanced-technology supply chains. By 2026 its loan authority was being used well beyond chip fabrication, including to underwrite domestic rare-earth mining and processing.[1]

## Use in the USA Rare Earth package

In January 2026 the [[department-of-commerce|U.S. Department of Commerce]] issued a non-binding letter of intent to support [[usa-rare-earth|USA Rare Earth]] with a $1.6 billion funding package consisting of $277 million in proposed federal funding and a $1.3 billion senior secured loan extended under the CHIPS and Science Act.[1] In exchange, the federal government is expected to take a roughly 10% equity stake in the company through 16.1 million shares and about 17.6 million warrants.[1]

The transaction illustrates the statute's role in the broader effort to reduce U.S. reliance on [[china|China]] for the critical minerals used in communications, weapons, and healthcare systems.[1]

## Continuity between the Trump and Biden administrations

Badlands commentary has cited the CHIPS Act as a leading example of policy continuity across administrations, describing it as a Trump-proposed measure — "the most lucrative on-shoring of domestic technology production, and thus job creation in decades" — that was ultimately signed into law by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[2] Writing in January 2024, [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] placed the Act alongside the retention of tariffs on [[china|China]] and other trade partners, the resumption of U.S. drilling in the Gulf of Mexico, and the curtailment of LNG shipments overseas as evidence that the Biden administration had preserved the "Trump-era Actuals" while surrounding them with hostile narratives.[2]

## Criticism of offshore outcomes (2024)

Badlands commentary in March 2024 argued that the roughly $280 billion CHIPS and Science Act had functioned as a subsidy for corporate expansion outside the United States.[3] The [[white-house|White House]] fact sheet of August 9, 2022 had touted nearly $50 billion in newly announced semiconductor investments — including a $40 billion Micron memory-chip commitment projected to create up to 40,000 construction and manufacturing jobs, and a $4.2 billion Qualcomm–GlobalFoundries partnership to expand a facility in upstate New York — bringing total business investment to nearly $150 billion since [[joe-biden|Biden]] took office.[3] A year later, however, [[globalfoundries|GlobalFoundries]] opened a $4 billion expanded fabrication plant in Singapore, a 23,000-square-metre site expected to create 1,000 jobs there.[3] [[micron-technology|Micron]]'s announced $16 billion Syracuse project fell behind schedule in 2023, and the company said it was awaiting additional federal funding before fulfilling its 2022 commitment, while separately announcing an expansion of its investment in [[china|China]] in early 2024.[3] The commentary contrasted this record with Beijing's simultaneous drive to replace foreign technology with homegrown alternatives, suggesting those substitutes might end up funded by American taxes.[3]

## Trump proposal to repeal (2025)

In his [[2025-trump-address-to-joint-session-of-congress|March 4, 2025 address to a joint session of Congress]], President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] suggested getting rid of the CHIPS Act and using the savings to reduce the deficit, arguing that the law had been a giveaway that actually harmed the US chip manufacturing industry.[4] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] cheered the proposal, describing it as a rebuke to Democrats who she said had used the legislative vehicle for personal gain.[4] The proposal came the same week that [[tsmc|TSMC]] pledged $100 billion in new US investment under the pressure of tariff threats rather than subsidies.[4]

## Fab delays (December 2023)

In December 2023, [[samsung-electronics|Samsung Electronics]] was reported to have pushed mass production at its $17 billion fab in Taylor, Texas from the second half of 2024 to 2025, following an earlier decision by [[tsmc|TSMC]] to delay production at its Arizona plant to 2025 over shortages of experienced construction workers and machine-installation technicians.[5] Delays at the US sites of the world's two leading contract chipmakers were characterized as a setback to President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s plan to expand domestic semiconductor output and avoid a repeat of the 2021 shortage, which cost companies hundreds of billions in revenue.[5]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the CHIPS Act as one of the main economic hints of the [[devolution|Devolution]] operation, noting that the law's original structure was assembled by the Trump administration before Biden signed it.[5] Under his "Switch" theory, the initiative's seismic long-term effects — tens of thousands of high-paying jobs and the erosion of the China–Taiwan near-monopoly on semiconductors — would only be realized once Trump returned to office.[5]
