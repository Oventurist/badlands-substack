# American Revolutionary War

The American Revolutionary War appears in Badlands Media commentary chiefly as a test case for the claim that the [[british-cabal|British Cabal]] retained control of the historical narrative even after losing the war militarily.[1]

## The narrative-control argument

In "The British Cabal" (August 7, 2023), [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] asked whether readers really believe the American forefathers risked life and limb over a tea tax, or whether the British were in fact far more sinister, cruel and controlling — possibly viewed by the colonists as a globalist, Satanic cult.[1] Invoking the maxim that the victors of war write history, he noted that in both world wars the Allies came off "smelling like roses" while the Germans were rendered as less than human.[1] By that logic, he argued, if America won complete independence, American history books should portray England as evil; instead they offer "the expensive tea narrative," which he took as evidence that the British Cabal never lost control of the story.[1]

## Colonial manufacturing ban

Carlson asserted that before the war England did not allow Americans to manufacture anything at all — large or small, for domestic use or export, no finished goods of any kind.[1] Colonists, particularly in the South, were permitted to produce raw commodities such as cotton, which were shipped to England, manufactured into finished items such as clothing, and sold to the world including back to America.[1] He remarked that he had never learned this in high school American history.[1]

## Post-war economic attack and tariffs

After the war, on Carlson's account, the British placed manufactured products on the open market at less than the cost of production for the sole purpose of destroying America's economy, which as a newly created system could not afford to compete.[1] Early American leaders answered with tariffs on England.[1]

Carlson drew two parallels. The first is the post-WWI settlement in which Britain and France imposed surrender terms preventing Germany from rebuilding its economy or exporting its products, leaving much of the country in poverty — noting pointedly that the Kaiser was King George's first cousin.[1] The second is China's emergence as an economic superpower in the 1990s through cheap goods priced below what American producers could match, causing a mass exodus of American manufacturing, with Trump's China tariffs as the modern echo of the early republic's response.[1]
