# 2023 Debt Ceiling Agreement

The 2023 debt limit agreement is the legislative deal, struck under Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]], that raised the federal borrowing limit with what Badlands Media commentary characterized as "virtually no spending cuts."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america) It functions in [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]]'s June 5, 2023 article as the event that destabilized his prior reading of the speakership.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

## Interpretive fallout

Carlson writes that the agreement forced him to "rethink why Trump endorsed McCarthy to be the Speaker of the House," and poses a series of unresolved questions: whether an increased debt limit without cuts is part of [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s plan; whether more evidence of a [[uniparty|Uniparty]] is needed to wake people up; whether the economy must worsen before Trump takes over again; and whether the episode serves the destruction of the Cabal banking system said to control the country and the world.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

He also raises the possibility that the deal was the intended trigger for McCarthy's removal — that Trump endorsed him foreseeing he would side with the RINOs, [[neoconservatives|Neocons]] and Democrats, and that the [[house-freedom-caucus-holdouts|America First members]] had already installed the mechanism to act, having pushed for and obtained measures making it easier to impeach the Speaker.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

## Trump's silence

Carlson notes that as of the morning before Sean Hannity's town hall he had not heard Trump mention the "monumentally-big debt ceiling deal" even once, despite it being the biggest issue in the news cycle for weeks; Trump was instead making playful fun of [[ron-desantis|Ron DeSantis]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america) Carlson reads that silence as an indication that the deal needed to pass as written, while allowing that Trump might yet attack it.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-communist-plot-to-take-over-america)

## Fiscal substance of the deal

Contemporaneous analysis relayed by [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] argued that the bottom line of the "so-called 'debt ceiling deal'" amounted to a cut of just 0.2% of GDP in nominal spending — the entirety of "the ridiculous melodrama gripping Washington."[2] That cut, ZeroHedge wrote, would still push total debt to $35 trillion by the end of [[joe-biden|Biden]]'s term and would not dent the long-term US debt trajectory that even the [[congressional-budget-office|CBO]] displays "in its full, hyperinflationary glory."[2]

[[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]]'s post-mortem concluded that "the spending deal looks likely to reduce spending by 0.1-0.2% of GDP yoy in 2024 and 2025, compared with a baseline in which funding grows with inflation," but added that the FY23 funding boost Congress approved in late 2022 was so large — nearly 10% year over year — that overall discretionary spending would likely be slightly higher in real terms the following year despite the new caps.[2] ZeroHedge's translation: adjusted for inflation, 2024 spending would rise again, making the arrangement a "total farce."[2] Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]], who negotiated on behalf of conservatives, nonetheless promoted the reduction repeatedly on his Twitter feed.[2]

## Secret concessions to House Democrats

Hours after the House passed the debt ceiling bill on May 31, 2023, [[axios|Axios]] reported that Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] had given Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] secret concessions to boost spending in Democratic districts through "community project funding" in exchange for Democratic votes, according to two senior lawmakers.[3] One lawmaker said the deal raised Democratic earmarks "closer to parity" with what Republicans receive in the GOP-led House.[3] McCarthy told reporters that he had cut no deals to obtain the Democratic votes, while Jeffries said only that "House Democrats to the rescue to avoid a dangerous default and help House Republicans get legislation over the finish line that they negotiated themselves."[3] The backdrop was a prior warning from Appropriations ranking member [[rosa-delauro|Rosa DeLauro]] that Democrats would receive significantly reduced district project funding that year, and Axios noted the side deal risked further inflaming far-right lawmakers already angry that the compromise conceded too much to Democrats and cut too little spending.[3]

Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] described the episode as "this year's political UFC match," in which the needs and interests of the American people were set aside for private interests and political ambitions, and said the degree to which politicians conspire behind closed doors for political gain was sobering.[3] Writing as [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], another contributor argued that the debacle takes on a more positive cast when read as part of a controlled exposure of the Deep State and of a fiat dollar in need of replacement, with hyper-inflationary debt increases greasing the rails toward the monetary system's demise and the Great Awakening.[3]

Writing as [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], one Badlands contributor explained his low reactivity to the negotiations through the maxim that "Bad news is good news in the Mind War," distinguishing analysis of the Actual — observable political and kinetic events — from the Potential, meaning the narratives spun out of them.[2] He held to a foundational belief that continued "irrational and accelerationist spending" on the US dollar forms part of a deliberate transition toward "a new economy built on the Real rather than the unReal," and said the debt ceiling fiasco — the latest in a long public line of economic failures pegged to the Biden administration and to RINOs in the Senate and House — had not shaken that belief.[2]

## Aftermath: the June 6 rule-vote revolt

Signed into law by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] over the objections of conservative Republicans, the package prompted immediate retaliation inside the House.[4] On Tuesday, June 6, 2023, eleven Republicans led by [[house-freedom-caucus|House Freedom Caucus]] members blocked an otherwise routine procedural rule vote — the first such failure in nearly two decades — throwing the week's schedule into disarray and leaving Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] facing demands for a meeting under the shadow of a threatened motion to vacate.[4] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the bill had "eliminated any possibility of fiscal responsibility" and that the holdouts' leverage was weak because McCarthy had already shown he could assemble a coalition to pass whatever he wanted.[4]

## Student loan repayment restart provision

Analysis published at the end of May 2023 highlighted a provision of the agreement announced between [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] — formally titled the Fiscal Responsibility Act — that prohibited the Biden administration from further extending the pause on federal student loan repayments that had been in place since March 2020.[5] The pause postponed roughly $5 billion per month in repayments, and had most recently been extended until 60 days after the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruled on the separate $400 billion loan forgiveness plan.[5] The agreement left the forgiveness plan itself untouched but barred any further extension of the moratorium "except as expressly authorized by an act of Congress," which analysts at [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] read as guaranteeing a restart of payments in September 2023.[5]

Economists warned the restart would act as a fiscal drag. Jefferies compared the return of monthly payments to the 2013 fiscal cliff, when tax increases cut consumer spending, and [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan]] chief U.S. economist Michael Feroli estimated the end of the moratorium would reduce annual disposable personal income by roughly $38 billion.[5] [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] framed the change as the end of a "nonpayment boondoggle" that would precipitate a collapse in household spending.[5]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the episode was less a genuine fiscal crisis than a narrative trap for what he called the Uniparty establishment, stripping cultural progressives of one of the Biden era's signature inducements while pushing the real economy toward a spending contraction — an outcome he characterised as ultimately useful to the "Truth Community" because it accelerated public awakening to fiscal mismanagement.[5]
