# California Ballot Disqualification Case

The **California ballot disqualification case** was a federal lawsuit seeking to keep former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] off California's Republican primary ballot for the 2024 election, dismissed with prejudice in early January 2024.[1] It was one of a nationwide wave of ballot-access challenges that also produced the [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|Colorado]], [[illinois-ballot-disqualification-case|Illinois]] and [[maine-ballot-disqualification|Maine]] proceedings.[1]

## The claim

The plaintiff argued that they had suffered "emotional injury" as a result of the breach of the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021, which they watched unfold on television, on the radio and in various publications.[1] They contended that the incident caused them "severe emotional distress," and on that basis filed suit to bar Trump from California's ballots.[1]

## Dismissal

On Wednesday, January 3, 2024, U.S. District Judge [[david-carter|David O. Carter]], a [[bill-clinton|Clinton]] appointee sitting in California, granted a motion to dismiss the lawsuit "with prejudice," barring its resubmission to the same court.[1] Carter held that because the events at issue had occurred "more than two years before the plaintiff" filed suit, the claim fell outside the applicable two-year statute of limitations.[1] Former Republican National Committee for California chairwoman [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]] posted the decision on X, writing that "the remnants of the last California case to keep President Trump off the ballot here were dismissed today by Judge David O. Carter!!"[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[paul-fleuret|Paul Fleuret]] greeted the dismissal with "annnnnnd another one bites the dust," noting with relish that the dismissing judge was a Clinton appointee.[1] He argued that the run of failed disqualification suits demonstrated "the strength of the Bill Of Rights," that opponents knew there was no precedent or legal standing for removing Trump from ballots, and that the attempts amounted to "temper tantrums being thrown by a side that knows they are already beaten."[1] Fleuret framed the effort as self-refuting: if Trump's polling numbers were as weak as claimed, there would be no need to remove him from ballots, indict him, or send him to trial — prompting his closing question, "Who is really in control?"[1]
