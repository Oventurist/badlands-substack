# Trump-class Battleship

The **Trump-class battleship** is a United States Navy surface-combatant program announced in late 2025, whose implementation drew shock and criticism in Western specialized defense publications on the grounds that it responded to the [[us-navy|U.S. Navy]]'s real challenges with essentially the opposite of the decisions those challenges dictated.[1]

## Announcement at Mar-a-Lago

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] publicly unveiled the program on Monday, December 22, 2025, speaking from Mar-a-Lago during what the [[white-house|White House]] billed as a "major announcement," and describing the new battleships as the biggest and most powerful ever built for the [[us-navy|US Navy]]'s "Golden Fleet."[2] He said he had approved two ships to start, with plans for up to 25, and was joined by Secretary of War [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]], Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]], and Secretary of the Navy [[john-phelan|John Phelan]].[2] Larger than the WWII-era Iowa class, the vessels were said to carry hypersonic missiles, rail guns, and lasers, with the Wall Street Journal — which first reported the plans — noting the Navy intended to procure the first ship in 2030.[2] "As you know, we're desperately in need of ships," Trump said, adding they would be "the fastest, the biggest, and by far, 100 times more powerful than any battleship ever built."[2] The plan formed part of a wider naval expansion of manned and unmanned ships, prompted in part by official warnings that the US trails [[china|China]] in shipbuilding capacity and output; asked whether the warships were aimed at Beijing, Trump replied, "It's a counter to everybody."[2]

## Historical debate over the battleship

Badlands host [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] noted that Trump first floated reviving the battleship in his September 2025 address to assembled generals and admirals at Quantico, and that the media mocked him for suggesting a heavily armored gun-armed warship could again serve as a fleet's capital ship.[2] He observed that fleets of battleships met in full-scale conflict only twice: the Battle of Tsushima in 1905, the final naval battle of the Russo-Japanese War and the first engagement between modern steel fleets, where four Japanese battleships and more than 100 other ships faced eleven Russian battleships and some 30 others; and the Battle of Jutland in 1916, where 28 British battleships and 120 other ships met 22 German battleships and 75 others in the North Sea.[2] Only Tsushima was decisive — Russia lost all its battleships while Japan lost none — while both sides claimed victory at Jutland.[2]

The Battle of Leyte Gulf in 1944, often cited as a third case and considered the largest naval battle in history by personnel, involved twelve American and seven Japanese battleships plus two hybrid battleship-carriers, but with 35 American carriers, four Japanese carriers, over 1,800 aircraft and nearly 400 warships engaged, the battleships were hardly decisive.[2] Leyte Gulf comprised four separate engagements over three days, of which only the Battle of Surigao Strait featured a battleship-versus-battleship encounter — with Japan fielding just one battleship — and it is universally considered the last such duel.[2] The only other battleship-versus-battleship instance of the Second World War came during the Guadalcanal campaign, when the USS Washington sank the Kirishima, regarded as the clearest example of the type.[2] Under the broadest definition of combat, no more than eight instances exist in history of opposing battleships directly meeting.[2]

The battleship was the ultimate weapon of war in the 1890s and the early twentieth century with the advent of the Dreadnought class, reaching its apex in the First World War — even as the Americans launched aircraft from a cruiser deck in 1910 and landed one on a modified cruiser a year later, and the Allies converted warships to launch seaplanes and balloons during the war.[2] Flat-top carrier construction was well underway by the 1920s, rendering the battleship obsolete by the start of the Second World War.[2] The last American battleship, the USS Missouri ("Mighty Mo"), was ordered in June 1940 — eighteen months before Pearl Harbor, where much of the US battleship fleet was destroyed — and commissioned in June 1944; her quarterdeck hosted the Japanese surrender in 1945, she was reactivated in 1984 with missiles and served in the Persian Gulf during Desert Storm, and today is a museum at Pearl Harbor.[2]

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry argued that the aircraft carrier now faces its own obsolescence, since long-range hypersonic missiles threaten the roughly 5,000 sailors aboard one and next-generation fighters will not require runways, making the flat-top profile unnecessary.[2] A heavily armored ship shaped to deflect or absorb missile impacts, able to operate small drones or manned vertical take-off aircraft and armed with rail guns, lasers, and hypersonic missiles, he contended, is the likely future of naval combat — making the Trump-class battleship less absurd than critics claim.[2]

## Cost and criticism

By published estimates, each Trump-class ship would cost between $10 billion and $15 billion, comparable to the $12.8 billion price of the newest nuclear aircraft carrier, the Gerald R. Ford.[1] Critics argued that while powerful, the design was an insufficient solution: for $20–30 billion, two unique ships would be built with relatively narrow strike capabilities but with a maximum construction-cost coefficient driven by small series production, size and the concentration of new technical solutions.[1] Analysts contrasted the program with the alternative of building roughly five times as many destroyers.[1]

## Strategic implications

Defense Express argued the decision could only please [[china|China]], which already fields more ships than the U.S. Navy and was projected to hold a 1.5-fold advantage in pennants by 2030.[1] [[russia|Russia]] would likewise welcome the diversion of resources into vulnerable, costly small-series ships, since Moscow's approach to countering the U.S. Navy has always relied on a submarine fleet intended to strike large formations such as carrier strike groups — formations of which Trump-class battleships would form part.[1]
