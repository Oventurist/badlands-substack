# Aldrich Plan

The Aldrich Plan, embodied in the "Aldrich bill," was the central banking proposal drafted in secret at the [[jekyll-island-meeting|Jekyll Island meeting]] and presented to Congress under the name of Senator [[nelson-aldrich|Nelson Aldrich]].[1]

## Reception in Congress

The Aldrich bill was very quickly identified as a bill to benefit the bankers, which rubbed a great many people the wrong way, including members of Congress.[1] In response, a congressional subcommittee known as the [[pujo-committee|Pujo Committee]] was created and tasked with investigating [[jp-morgan|J.P. Morgan]] along with several other Wall Street bankers.[1]

During debate, Republican [[charles-a-lindbergh-sr|Charles A. Lindbergh]] declared that "the Aldrich plan is the Wall Street Plan. It means another panic, if necessary, to intimidate the people. Aldrich, paid by the government to represent the people, proposes a plan for the trusts instead," and alleged that a large sum of money had been raised to educate the public in favor of the plan.[1]

## Defeat and reincarnation

As debate continued the bankers realized they lacked sufficient support, and Republican leadership never brought the Aldrich bill to a vote.[1] The bankers instead turned to the Democrats and heavily financed [[woodrow-wilson|Woodrow Wilson]]; after his election, Morgan, [[paul-warburg|Paul Warburg]], [[bernard-baruch|Bernard Baruch]] and others advanced the Glass-Owen Bill, which Democratic leaders hailed as totally different from the Aldrich bill though it was virtually identical.[1] Aldrich and [[frank-vanderlip|Frank Vanderlip]] publicly opposed the new bill precisely in order to make the public believe it was radically different — posturing the article says was enough to quell suspicion.[1] Vanderlip later admitted in the Saturday Evening Post that "although the Aldrich Federal Reserve Plan was defeated when it bore the name Aldrich, nevertheless its essential points were all contained in the plan that finally was adopted."[1]

Ohio attorney Alfred Crozier likewise noticed the similarity, stating that the bill granted "just what Wall Street and the big banks for twenty-five years have been striving for — private instead of public control of currency," and that both measures robbed the government and the people of effective control over the public's money.[1]
