# Astrotheology

Astrotheology is the term used in the Badlands Media essay "False Flags and the Occult" for the fusion of astronomical observation with religious meaning — the practice of assigning socio-religious significance to the movement of celestial bodies — which the article claims was secretly woven into all modern and mainstream religions.[1]

## Origins

The article argues that many occult groups of the deep past believed heralds and signs in the heavens were related to god-like beings and their communion with humanity.[1] Comets, solar and lunar eclipses and other celestial phenomena, if properly identified and predicted, could be used to negotiate with the gods and gain favor for the tribe or cult.[1] In a world where primitive minds attributed rain to a rain god and crops to a fertility god, whoever could broker a bargain with such beings — a witch doctor, shaman or high priest — became a figure of renown.[1] Later, kings and their progeny acted as appointed representatives of the gods, and both priest and king functioned as messiah-like figures whose word was law and whose will was unquestionable.[1]

According to Anthony Napoleon's book *Shadow Men: An Encyclopedia of Mind Control*, cited in the article, the [[shadow-men|shadow men]] — intelligent psychopaths — learned to track the motion of the heavens and eventually to predict celestial events with staggering accuracy, using those predictions plus carefully seeded narratives to persuade a superstitious citizenry that they were in communion with the gods.[1] The article reasons that people naturally follow those who can accurately predict events, so that elite priests and druids effectively became the secret rulers of men, cementing their grip by concretizing their systems into religious dogma and astrologically centered days of worship, many of which involved ritual sacrifice.[1]

## The zodiac as time system

The zodiac is described as a time-measurement system used by ancient cultures to track the movement of celestial bodies with the Earth as center point, applicable both to a single year and to a Great Year of roughly 26,000 years.[1] The constellation behind the rising Sun determines the house or sign; on March 19th or 20th the Sun rises in Aries the Ram, and on April 17th it enters Taurus, though modern astrologers largely retain the historical system despite precessional drift, with the true rising sign tracked in systems such as Vedic astrology.[1]

The equinoxes — the word means "equal night" — occur when the Sun's angle to the Earth's equator is zero degrees, placing those two zodiacal points on either side of a horizontal line.[1] The solstices are marked by a vertical line: the Sun's lowest point from a northerly perspective lies between Sagittarius and Capricorn, and its highest between Gemini and Cancer, corresponding to angles of 23.5° south and 23.5° north as marked on globes by the Tropics of Capricorn and Cancer.[1] The year further divides into a Season of Life, beginning with the Sun's emergence from the southern hemisphere at the spring equinox, and a Season of Death, beginning with its recession at the fall equinox; within these the zodiac tells an allegorical tale representing the evolution of consciousness for the individual and the planet.[1]

## Crosses and sabbats

The Minor Zodiacal Cross is formed by the four seasonal beginnings of March 21st, June 21st, September 21st and December 21st.[1] The Grand or Galactic Cross is formed by the seasonal midpoints, each historically marked by a Sabbat: May 1st (Walpurgisnacht), August 1st (Lammas), October 31st (Samhain) and February 2nd (Candlemas).[1] Combining the Minor Cross point of March 19th with the Grand Cross point of May 1st outlines the forty-day [[season-of-sacrifice|Season of Sacrifice]].[1] Researchers including [[mark-passio|Mark Passio]] and Santos Bonacci are cited for the conclusion that this astrotheological basis for shaping world events remains active today.[1]
