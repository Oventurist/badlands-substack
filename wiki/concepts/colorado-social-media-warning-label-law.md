# Colorado Social Media Warning Label Law

The Colorado social media warning label law, enacted as House Bill 1136 in 2024, required social media companies to display warning labels to minors about the potential impacts of heavy social media use on youths' brains and health.[1] In November 2025 a federal judge blocked the warning label provision on First Amendment grounds, holding that it likely constituted unconstitutional compelled speech.[1]

## The law

Under HB 1136, warning pop-ups would have had to be displayed to minors who had been on social media for more than an hour within a 24-hour period.[1] The warning label provision was scheduled to take effect on January 1 of the following year.[1]

## Litigation and injunction

A coalition of technology companies organized as [[netchoice|NetChoice]] sued in August 2025 to block the law's enactment, arguing that the mandated warnings amounted to compelled speech.[1] U.S. District Court Judge [[william-j-martinez|William J. Martinez]], sitting in Denver, granted a preliminary injunction in a ruling issued on a Thursday in early November 2025, preventing the warning label provision from taking effect while the broader lawsuit proceeds.[1]

Martinez wrote that the court "fully appreciates Colorado's legitimate effort to protect the children and adolescents of our state from the impacts of social media use on their health and wellbeing," but concluded that NetChoice was substantially likely to succeed on the merits of its claim that the state "may not pursue this laudable goal by compelling social media companies to speak (the state's) expressive messages."[1]

## Commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described [[colorado|Colorado]] as a primary battlefield for attempts to undermine the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]], citing the Masterpiece Cakeshop litigation — which compelled expression contrary to a baker's religious beliefs in the name of prohibiting discrimination and ran from 2012 to 2018 — as an example of a case in which "the process is the punishment," and noting that similar cases have advanced through Colorado courts before being overturned at the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]].[1]

She also cited [[counterman-v-colorado|Counterman v. Colorado]], decided in 2023, which established the prevailing legal precedent for determining "true threats" as a predicate for criminalizing speech, including a four-part test that in her view should have gutted plaintiffs' ability to engage in speech-related lawfare.[1] She recounted presenting a First Amendment defense in the federal District of Colorado, citing Counterman and its Colorado test, after the League of Women Voters recruited the [[naacp|NAACP]] to accuse her and her co-defendants of voter suppression, intimidation, coercion and threats under the Voting Rights Act and the Ku Klux Klan Act; she won, the plaintiffs appealed, and the case was scheduled for argument in the 10th Circuit Court of Appeals days after the brief was published.[1]

Ashe drew a distinction between punishing disagreeable speech and compelling speech, arguing that the two are opposites but equally unconstitutional, and noted that the Colorado legislature's attempt to compel speech in the name of online child safety had been checked by a Colorado court.[1] She observed that compelled speech on tobacco labels has been held lawful, that this case may go the other way, and that both rest on science-based arguments for government intervention — asking what would make them different.[1]
