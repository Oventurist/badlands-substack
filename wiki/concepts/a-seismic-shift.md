# A Seismic Shift

**A Seismic Shift: Transferring Power From the Banks … To the People** is a Badlands Media feature published January 12, 2024 and written by [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]]. It argues that the United States is on the verge of a fundamental restructuring of its banking system, in which a publicly owned banking option — delivered through the United States Postal Service and through Federal Reserve retail accounts known as FedAccounts — will displace the dominance of private Wall Street banks. The article presents this transformation as a deliberate plan executed quietly by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], and closes with the Q slogan "WWG1WGA."

## Continuity with the author's earlier work

The piece opens as an explicit sequel to Lange's earlier article "The Illusion of Democracy," which discussed the importance of constitutional **safeguards** for the future of America. That article used the example of the Costa Rican leader José "Pepe" Figueres, known as "Don Pepe," who according to Lange placed permanent safeguards in his country's constitution to prevent insurgency and protect popular rights. Two safeguards are singled out: permanently protecting elections with an identification document issued only to citizens, so that nobody else could vote; and creating a permanent "public banking option" that prevented private banks from controlling the government and protected the people's savings from being stolen or controlled by private bankers. Lange writes that these measures "gave the power back to the people" and have gone a long way toward preventing another government controlled by a global cabal.

The article also cross-references three other Lange features: "Master and Commander," on Trump's control of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]]; "Are CBDC's the Path To Fear or Freedom?", on the digital dollar; and "The Midas Touch," on a return to a redeemable gold standard.

## The Bank of North Dakota

The article's central American precedent is the [[bank-of-north-dakota|Bank of North Dakota]], established in 1919 and described as the only public bank of its kind in the country. Quoting a 2019 Vox article, Lange describes early-twentieth-century North Dakota as a **"banking desert"**: a sparsely settled farming state whose low population density prevented local financial institutions from taking root, leaving its economy vulnerable to corporate banks in Chicago and Minneapolis that could "inflate farmers' loan rates or undercut grain prices without fear of reprise." David Flynn, economics and finance department chair at the University of North Dakota, is quoted saying that "a lot of your economic livelihood would be, for lack of a better word, controlled by out-of-state interests who may not have the best interests of the local producers in mind." Lange adds that the same predicament faces poor Americans today who lack access or choice in financial institutions, and that the movement to found the bank was fundamentally about removing out-of-state banker control over the people.

Citing In These Times, the article notes that the bank opened in Bismarck on July 28, 1919, is owned by all the residents of the state, and remains the only publicly owned bank in the continental United States a century later. It survived the Great Depression, the Savings and Loan scandal of the 1980s and the 2008 credit crisis because its purpose was to serve the people of North Dakota rather than Wall Street or the federal government. In These Times is further quoted invoking Ellen Brown, chair of the Public Banking Institute, whose book *Banking on the People* argues that a public banking system "can fund the goods, services and infrastructure required to satisfy the needs of the people and the economy without unsustainable debt, taxation or environmental degradation."

Lange highlights the historical parallel drawn by In These Times between the conditions that produced the bank — "corporate domination, an economy hobbled by debt burdens, gaping inequalities, ineffective reformists, bought politicians" — and those of the present "new gilded age," which he restates as four points: corporations in bed with government, an economy strangled by massive debt, huge inequality between rich and poor, and a corrupt government in which nobody is trying to change anything.

Performance figures are given: between 1919 and 1933 the bank lent almost $41 million to more than 16,000 farmers; during the Great Depression it helped the state sustain itself and recover quickly, foreshadowing similar success in 2008–09; while teachers elsewhere were paid in IOUs typically redeemed at a 15 percent loss, the bank paid them in full; and in the 1940s foreclosed farmland was sold back, in many cases to the families that had lost it. The article also credits the bank with returning a portion of its profits to the state general fund — more than a billion dollars over its history — which lowers taxes and funds infrastructure, and with offering lower-cost student loans to state residents.

## A nationwide movement

Returning to Vox, the article reports that North Dakota's success has drawn the attention of California, where a public bank movement has gained prominence since 2018 amid rising income inequality and predatory practices by financial institutions. Efforts have been made in nearly two dozen other states; in the year covered by the Vox piece, four state legislatures besides California — New York, New Mexico, New Hampshire and Massachusetts — introduced bills to create or study public banks, and none passed. Lange characterises the movement as growing but stymied, and asks whether Trump can change that.

## The Postal Savings System

The article's second precedent is the [[postal-savings-system|Postal Savings System]], a nationwide public savings option that operated from 1911 to 1967 and predated the Federal Reserve. Quoting the Roosevelt Institute, Lange writes that for more than fifty years the United States operated "a hugely popular and secure public option through the Postal Service," relying on local banks to manage accounts and allowing all Americans to make deposits into no-cost, interest-bearing savings accounts at post offices; the original $500 deposit limit was raised to $2,500 by 1918. He treats the deposit cap imposed by [[congress|Congress]] as the system's only real drawback.

During the Great Depression, when the public "justifiably lost faith in private banks," the system was a "uniquely safe and reliable" alternative; by 1947 more than four million people held $3.4 billion in savings across more than 8,000 postal units. Lange contrasts this with the more than 30,000 post offices operating today, which unlike private banks serve every community in the country, and argues that this history has been "strategically hidden from the American people by the same private bankers who now control our media and government."

## The FDIC argument

The article attributes the end of postal savings to what it calls "a cabal trick called Federal Deposit Insurance." Quoting the Roosevelt Institute, it notes that during the post-war boom, as interest rates at private banks rose and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation offered depositors at private banks a guarantee, use of the Postal Savings System declined until the program was discontinued in 1967.

Lange's interpretation is that deposit insurance removed the safety advantage that public savings had enjoyed, allowing private banks to offer higher interest through riskier investments while their deposits were underwritten by taxpayers, thereby placing the public option at a competitive disadvantage. He argues that the claim that the FDIC is funded by banks themselves ignores "decades of back door bailouts for the banks using money from the Federal Reserve," which he characterises as taxpayer money handed to Wall Street banks so they can profit by pumping up the stock market. The result, in his account, is "a rigged system with no competition and no control by the people," in which one financial crisis follows another and the public pays for every bailout.

## Postal banking proposals

Citing Cheddar News, the article argues that postal banking would let consumers avoid predatory non-bank options such as payday lenders and check-cashing companies. It reproduces FDIC household survey data showing that 6.5 percent of U.S. households — 8.4 million — were **unbanked**, with no bank account, and 18.7 percent — 24.2 million — were **underbanked**, holding an account but still going outside the banking system for some services. The nationwide footprint of the Postal Service, which tracks population rather than commercial activity, is described as a way to reach this population quickly; a quoted advocate named Armstrong says the 30,000 post office locations are "an ideal way to provide core banking services and money payment services to many of the people who do not have bank accounts."

The article then turns to a white paper by the Postal Service Office of Inspector General, reported by Postal-Reporter.com, which declined to make an official recommendation but suggested that a first logical step could be expanding products likely permissible under current law: payroll check cashing, electronic domestic money transfers between post offices, international money transfers, walk-up bill pay, money orders, ATMs and gift cards, subject to approval by the Postal Regulatory Commission. Other products — small loans, reloadable prepaid cards, and checking or savings accounts — were described as likely outside the Postal Service's current legal authority, though expanded authority could generate significantly more revenue and greater financial inclusion. The OIG also examined the possibility of the Postal Service becoming a licensed bank, calling it the most time-consuming, challenging and risky approach, but also the one with the highest potential benefit for the financially underserved. Lange notes that Q has mentioned several times the importance of Inspector General offices operating in the background as part of "the plan."

He emphasises that unlike private banks the Post Office has, in his words, a constitutional public service obligation to all Americans, and that this makes it the natural vehicle for a public banking option comparable to what Figueres established in Costa Rica.

## Trump and the Postal Service Board of Governors

The article argues that Trump took control of the Postal Service in the same manner it says he took control of the Federal Reserve, where he appointed every member of the board of governors except one — a governor described as a supporter of the digital dollar, as are Jared Kushner and Trump himself, which Lange offers as the reason she was not forced to resign and the reason Trump has never criticised a Federal Reserve digital dollar.

Quoting Time magazine, the article recounts that by 2014 the USPS Board of Governors had fewer than six members and lacked a quorum; that [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] nominated seven people to fill the vacancies but, per a 2018 Congressional Research Service report, none received a Senate vote; that by 2016 the board was down to a single member, James Bilbray, whose term expired that December; and that by the time Trump took office in January 2017 the board was officially empty, "offering him a unique opportunity that he quickly seized." Of the six members serving at the time of the Time report, four had donated to Republican candidates including Trump and had served in prior Republican administrations or political organizations. Their confirmations by voice vote restored a quorum by August 2019, so that when Postmaster General Megan Brennan announced her retirement that October the board was able to elect her successor: in May it selected Louis DeJoy, the first person chosen for the position who had not risen through the organisation.

The Washington Post is quoted describing DeJoy as a North Carolina businessman and Trump campaign donor who arrived in June to make sweeping cuts to postal operations, appointed by a board controlled five to one by Trump loyalists, and quoting Trump's remark to reporters, "We just got the board." The same report describes Trump reshaping the "once-obscure" Board of Governors within three years into a "behind-the-scenes powerhouse" setting his priorities in motion, possibly for years to come, and notes that the traditionally nonpartisan board sat at the centre of a major controversy over election integrity. The lobbyist Arthur Sackler is quoted saying the president "had a unique opportunity to start from scratch" and that he could not recall another time when there was literally no one on the board.

Lange concludes that Trump was fixated on the Post Office from the beginning of his administration because the institution can serve the people in two ways: protecting elections and transforming the banking system.

## The blockchain voting patent

On elections, the article cites Business Insider's 2020 report that the Postal Service filed a patent for a "reliable voting system" using blockchain technology to improve the security and transparency of election results, under which voters would receive a unique code by mail to verify their identity and cast a vote online, with the information stored on a blockchain so votes cannot be tampered with. Lange argues that the real purpose is not mail-in voting but verifying voter identity as a citizen and securing the voter rolls on an untamperable ledger; he predicts a U.S.-owned voter ID law and a cleansing of the voter rolls, with the Post Office as a key player in election safeguards.

## FedAccounts

On banking, the article cites an opinion piece in The Hill by Mark Dimondstein, president of the American Postal Workers Union, written after the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank. Dimondstein argues that the banking system's very existence — deposit insurance, loan guarantees, interest-rate setting — depends entirely on the federal government, and that the Postal Savings System, created by an act of Congress in 1910 and lasting 55 years, became one of the largest depository institutions in the country, a safe harbour during the Great Depression and a provider of reliable basic banking especially in rural communities. Postal banking, he writes, is "an old idea whose time has come again."

Dimondstein also introduces the idea of extending Federal Reserve services — inter-bank payments, account management, interest on balances — to individuals and small businesses, "creating a 'FedAccount' for everyone, not just the banks." The Berkeley Economic Review is cited for the origin of the proposal: in 2018 three researchers, two of whom had worked in the [[treasury-department|Treasury Department]], proposed that the Federal Reserve offer all individuals and businesses the option of opening an account with the Fed itself as an alternative to private banks or credit unions. Lange notes that Trump was president in 2018 and asks whether this is coincidence.

The Berkeley Economic Review is further quoted arguing that FedAccounts would lessen, if not eliminate, the need for widespread deposit insurance, since deposits at a Federal Reserve Bank cannot be defaulted on: a private bank's debit card carries the liability of that bank, while a Fed-issued debit card would hold "pure sovereign money," the liability of the Federal Reserve itself, which can always meet withdrawals by issuing more money. Lange instructs the reader to re-read the passage substituting "Treasury" for "Federal Reserve," asserting that the Federal Reserve is unconstitutional and that the Treasury is properly in charge of the people's money. He ties this to a return to a redeemable [[gold-standard|gold standard]], arguing that Americans will no longer need federal deposit insurance because their accounts will hold sovereign money rather than fiat Federal Reserve Notes, and therefore will no longer need to bail out failed private banks.

Drawing on JustMoney.org's summary of work by Morgan Ricks, one of the Treasury-affiliated authors of the FedAccounts proposal, the article notes the suggestion that FedAccounts could offer the full functionality of ordinary transaction accounts — debit cards, ATM access, direct deposit, online bill payment, online and mobile access — without fees or minimum-balance requirements, and that the Fed could partner with the Postal Service to serve as a ubiquitous physical branch network. Merged with postal banking proposals, this would make the U.S. money-and-payments system "fully public infrastructure akin to roads, sidewalks, public libraries and the judicial system."

## Opposition from the banking industry

The article presents the American Bankers Association as the principal opponent. It quotes the ABA's issue update stating that members of Congress have proposed legislation requiring the Federal Reserve to offer government-subsidised retail bank accounts in conjunction with the Postal Service, requiring banks with more than $10 billion in assets to offer basic "pass through" accounts whose deposits rest at the Federal Reserve rather than on the bank's balance sheet — a proposal the ABA calls impractical, "highly disruptive" and unlikely to produce long-term wealth-building financial inclusion.

Lange reads the objection as an admission: the requirement would let customers move deposits into safe, publicly owned accounts, removing them from bank balance sheets, preventing Wall Street banks from using them in risky schemes, and ending bailouts. He disputes the ABA's description of the accounts as "subsidized," arguing that it is private banks that have been subsidised for generations through federal deposit insurance, and that the industry fears losing FDIC protection because without it most Americans would choose the public option.

He further quotes the ABA's claim that the United States has a healthy banking system of roughly 5,000 banks employing more than two million people, that FDIC-insured depositors have never lost a penny of an insured deposit, and that FedAccounts would draw deposits from banks of all sizes onto the Federal Reserve balance sheet. Lange rejects the "healthy banking system" characterisation given recent bank failures and insists that taxpayers have paid for all bank bailouts. He identifies the industry's "real fear" in the ABA's statement that banks "will nevertheless be unable to compete with a government agency offering deposit accounts with an unlimited balance sheet," and notes that a public option would remove the $250,000 cap on protected savings. Finally he quotes the ABA's warning that postal banking would extend well beyond the Postal Service's core competencies and raise regulatory and consumer-protection questions, and that "FedAccounts and Postal Banking would represent a seismic shift in our national banking infrastructure" — the phrase from which the article takes its title.

## Conclusion

Lange closes by listing four developments he says he has given evidence for across his articles as being driven by Trump: a move back to the gold standard; a behind-the-scenes push for a digital dollar; a postal banking public option; and the creation of FedAccounts. He asks whether readers trust that Trump is in more control than most people realise and will build a new banking system protecting constitutional rights and hard-earned money, states his belief that Trump "is a hero and deserves our trust," and ends with "WWG1WGA."
