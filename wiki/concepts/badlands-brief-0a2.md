# Badlands Brief: A Virtual Smash & Grab & Another Impeachment Hoax

"A Virtual Smash & Grab & Another Impeachment Hoax" is an edition of the [[badlands-brief|Badlands Brief]] published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on February 17, 2026. The Brief is described by its publishers as "your daily drop of Badlands' takes on the narratives dominating the info war," pairing summarized news items with commentary from named Badlands contributors. This edition carried five main segments and one bonus item, with commentary from [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], and GhostofBasedPatrickHenry. The masthead notes that opinions expressed are those of the tagged authors and do not necessarily reflect the positions of Badlands Media.

## NAACP motion over the Fulton County records seizure

The lead segment reports that the [[naacp|NAACP]] and other non-governmental organizations filed an emergency motion in the US District Court for the Northern District of Georgia seeking court-imposed restrictions on how the [[fbi|FBI]] and the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] may use election materials seized from [[fulton-county|Fulton County]] in January, an action covered elsewhere in the corpus as the [[fulton-county-election-raid|Fulton County election records seizure]]. The brief supporting the motion was posted by the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law.

According to the source, the filing was brought under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(g) and does not directly challenge the validity of the underlying search warrant. Instead, the plaintiffs argue that Fulton County voters hold protectable privacy and constitutional interests in their personal voter data — including Social Security numbers, dates of birth, driver's license numbers, and ballot-related information — and therefore qualify as "persons aggrieved" under the rule.

The motion asks the court to confine federal use of the seized materials strictly to the criminal investigation described in the warrant, and specifically to prohibit use of the data for national voter roll programs, immigration enforcement, civil enforcement unrelated to the warrant, data sharing with other agencies, compilation of voter databases, and public disclosure. The plaintiffs further request access controls restricting review to personnel assigned to the investigation, a detailed inventory of the materials seized, disclosure of who has accessed the records, and a protective order governing storage and handling. The organizations assert both individual standing on behalf of Fulton County voters whose data was seized and associational standing, arguing that the seizure undermines voter trust and diverts organizational resources — a claim structure treated in the corpus under [[legal-standing|legal standing]].

The article notes that the seizure continues to draw criticism, citing an editorial in [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] published that Monday warning that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] is nationalizing elections.

Ashe in America's commentary characterizes the filing as evidence of "panic over election integrity," writing that "NGOs that register voters are begging a federal court in Georgia to not let the feds check their work" and that "the NAACP has unironically asked a court to prevent the federal government from using federal records to ensure federal compliance in federal elections." She emphasizes that the records at issue date from the [[2020-united-states-presidential-election|2020 election]] and invokes a previously cited figure that 83% of the nation supports verifying that voters are who they say they are — a majority she says includes Democratic and Black voters, meaning in her framing that the civil rights organizations are "arguing against the positions of the people they claim to be representing." She reasons that the only way opposition to an 83% position makes sense is if that position represents "an insurmountable threat to your existence," asks whether voter ID is an existential threat to get-out-the-vote organizations, and closes with a rhetorical reference to what Seth Rich was working on at the time of his death.

## Rumors of MBZ's death

Under the heading "PSYOP: Rumors of MBZ Death Greatly Exaggerated…Maybe," the Brief reports that online rumors circulated claiming that UAE President [[mohamed-bin-zayed-al-nahyan|Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan]] had died. According to Arab News, Turkish President [[recep-tayyip-erdogan|Recep Tayyip Erdogan]] posted on X on Sunday that he was postponing a visit to Abu Dhabi because bin Zayed had a "health problem." The message, later deleted, said Erdogan had spoken with him by phone and "expressed his sadness over the health problem experienced by (President) Al Nahyan and wished him a prompt recovery," with travel to be rescheduled. As of publication there was no official confirmation of any death.

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry writes that X users have spent a day declaring "The Emir is dead!" and that Erdogan's deleted post did not help. He says he cannot determine whether Erdogan's post started the story or amplified it, and notes that although both the Turkish and Emirati governments issued statements about the phone call, nobody is satisfied that this proves anything. He connects the episode to what he sees as a concerted effort to drive a wedge between [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] and the UAE, with both capitalizing on the dynamic to negotiate new bilateral agreements with countries including [[india|India]] and [[pakistan|Pakistan]]. Noting that the possible originator, Erdogan, is in his description "a known [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] bro," he speculates the whole affair was a ruse to sow confusion while geopolitical dynamics were reshaped.

## Disney, ByteDance, and Seedance 2.0

The Brief reports that the Chinese technology company [[bytedance|ByteDance]] released in China an AI video-generation tool called Seedance 2.0, capable of producing short cinematic videos from text, image, audio, and video inputs. Over the weekend, viral Seedance-generated clips featuring recognizably realistic portrayals of Hollywood actors and characters circulated online, including scenes interpreted as resembling big-budget productions.

Major studios — notably [[walt-disney-company|Walt Disney]] and [[paramount-pictures|Paramount]] — sent cease-and-desist letters to ByteDance alleging that Seedance 2.0 uses copyrighted material and characters without authorization. The letter was not publicly distributed but was first reviewed and reported by Axios. Disney's attorneys were quoted: "Over Disney's well-publicized objections, ByteDance is hijacking Disney's characters by reproducing, distributing and creating derivative works featuring those characters… ByteDance's virtual smash-and-grab of Disney's IP is willful, pervasive and totally unacceptable…We believe this is just the tip of the iceberg – which is shocking considering Seedance has only been available for a few days."

The [[human-artistry-campaign|Human Artistry Campaign]] added, per *Deadline*: "The launch of Seedance 2.0 is an attack on every creator around the world. Stealing human creators' work in an attempt to replace them with AI-generated slop is destructive to our culture: stealing isn't innovation. These unauthorized deepfakes and voice clones of actors violate the most basic aspects of personal autonomy and should be deeply concerning to everyone. Authorities should use every legal tool at their disposal to stop this wholesale theft." ByteDance promised improved safeguards against IP theft.

Ashe in America argues that the AI narrative "has gone off the rails" and that ByteDance's advances will test the authority and enforcement capabilities of US and global intellectual property frameworks. She likens the episode to Napster: the studios will try to enforce traditional rights, centralize power, and remain monopolistic players in an industry transforming regardless. She observes that music piracy permanently changed the world's relationship with music — no more record stores or CDs, but all music in one's pocket — and that the relationship with movies is already changing, with theater attendance down and award-show ratings near record lows. In her view people no longer care much about studios or even actors, "but they do love the characters." The worst thing studios can do, she argues, is stand on authorities their customers are rejecting: if courts stop Seedance, another tool will emerge "before the signature on the temporary restraining order is dry," just as stopping Napster produced Kazaa, LimeWire, BitTorrent, and a digital music revolution. Studios largely protected themselves in the 2023 strike negotiations, she notes, but their most valuable assets are franchises, and by the time a ruling or even a hearing arrives it will be too late to reclaim them. She adds a postscript referencing a discussion with Blue Eyes about AI doomerism and transformation on an episode of [[culture-of-change|Culture of Change]].

## Rubio at the Munich Security Conference

The Brief covers Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]]'s remarks at the Munich Security Conference, where he outlined the administration's foreign-policy approach with emphasis on national sovereignty, border security, and greater [[nato|NATO]] burden-sharing.

In the quoted passage, Rubio recalls that when the conference began in 1963 the continent "was divided against itself," with the line between communism and freedom running through the heart of Germany, the first barbed fences of the Berlin Wall two years old, and the Cuban Missile Crisis only months past — Americans and Europeans "staring down the barrel of a new global catastrophe" more apocalyptic than anything before. He describes Soviet communism on the march with thousands of years of Western civilization in the balance, and credits a common purpose: "We were unified not just by what we were fighting against; we were unified by what we were fighting for." He then delivers the passage that gives the segment its title, arguing that "the euphoria of this triumph led us to a dangerous delusion: that we had entered, quote, 'the end of history;' that every nation would now be a liberal democracy; that the ties formed by trade and by commerce alone would now replace nationhood; that the [[rules-based-international-order|rules-based global order]] – an overused term – would now replace the national interest; and that we would now live in a world without borders where everyone became a citizen of the world."

Rubio urged European allies to increase defense spending and take more responsibility for regional security, criticized mass migration policies, and questioned the effectiveness of some post–World War II international institutions while affirming continued US commitment to the transatlantic alliance. Reactions were mixed: some European officials welcomed the reaffirmation of alliance commitments while expressing concern about the criticism of migration and multilateral institutions, and commentators described the address as a formal articulation of a [[maga|MAGA]] foreign-policy doctrine, with supporters calling it realist and critics warning it could deepen transatlantic tensions.

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry calls it "a pretty good speech by Little Marco" and says he appreciated that Rubio was sent to Munich "to deliver these terms of surrender to the satanic elite." He states that he sees no chance of reconciliation with Europe's Old Guard, which he says has largely destroyed itself, while predicting that the people of Europe will be great allies once they escape their predicament — hopefully through a bloodless revolution, though he warns the European elite appear willing to start a third world war rather than surrender power. He closes: "Choose now, Europe."

## Bonus: nuclear microreactor air transport

A bonus item reports that the United States conducted its first air transport of a nuclear microreactor prototype as part of a Defense Department demonstration program tied to the [[pentagon|Pentagon]]'s effort to develop small, transportable reactors capable of powering remote or military locations. The demonstration showed the reactor system can be safely moved by aircraft.

The Department of Energy posted on X: "On May 23, 2025, President Donald Trump signed an executive order to deploy advanced nuclear reactor technologies for national security purposes. Yesterday, we took the next step toward achieving this historic mission." Energy Secretary Chris Wright wrote: "President Trump promised the American people that he would unleash American energy dominance. Yesterday, @ENERGY and @DeptofWar partnered to move a nuclear reactor by air for the very first time. This is the next chapter for U.S. energy."

In a related *Wall Street Journal* opinion piece, Senator [[tom-cotton|Tom Cotton]] argues that the US should not fall behind in nuclear capabilities and frames modernization as essential to deterrence, beginning: "The New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty expired this month. The end of [[new-start-treaty|New Start]] is a watershed moment in American nuclear strategy. Far from a failure of diplomacy, this expiration is an overdue correction of a strategic mistake that left America vulnerable to two nuclear rivals: Russia and China." The Brief notes that no new nuclear weapons policy has been announced.
