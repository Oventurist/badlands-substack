# Privatization in Mexico

The privatization program carried out under president [[carlos-salinas-de-gortari|Carlos Salinas de Gortari]] between 1988 and 1994 was the largest in Mexican history and, in the Badlands Media account, a deliberate precondition for the North American Free Trade Agreement rather than an independent economic reform.[1]

## Scale

When Mexico first announced it would sell off or shut down its network of government-run businesses, observers were skeptical, having heard similar promises from third-world governments running bloated industrial bureaucracies.[1] A decade later the government had sold or dissolved more than 80 percent of the 1,155 businesses it once ran, one of the most sweeping sales of state companies in the world.[1] Debt-payment savings allowed increased social spending and bolder reforms including the NAFTA negotiation.[1] Banks nationalized in 1982 were privatized again, and nearly 50 new foreign-currency financial institutions were licensed to compete with the revived private sector — a detail the article singles out as one of the most important aspects of the plan.[1]

## Outcomes

The program aggravated many of the problems it was meant to ease: whittling back government industry cost more than 400,000 jobs since 1983 and caused widespread social hardship, and getting government out of business did not reliably produce efficiency, competition or a better life for ordinary Mexicans, with new private owners proving as unresponsive to workers and consumers as the old bureaucrats.[1] The columnist Alvaro Cepeda Neri wrote in La Jornada that "the booty of privatization has made multimillionaires of 13 families, while the rest of the population — some 80 million Mexicans — has been subjected to the same gradual impoverishment as though they had suffered through a war."[1] [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] concludes that privatization created no competition at all and merely transferred monopolies from government into private hands, driving up costs.[1] Mexico had two billionaires in 1991; by the end of the Salinas administration there were 28.[1]

The paradigm case is [[carlos-slim|Carlos Slim]]'s 1990 purchase of the telephone monopoly [[telmex|Telmex]], followed immediately by a government-authorized 40 percent local rate increase and a guaranteed private monopoly on long-distance service until 1997.[1]

## The laundering thesis

Global Research, quoted in the article, states that the privatization program was launched under [[imf|IMF]] advice and subsequently evolved into a multibillion-dollar [[money-laundering|money-laundering]] operation in which narco-dollars were channelled toward the acquisition of state property and public utilities.[1] Lange's synthesis is that state monopolies were sold at rock-bottom prices to billionaires with drug connections using drug money, concentrating wealth in a few hands before the borders were opened for trade under [[nafta|NAFTA]] — with the concentration of wealth a benefit of the plan rather than its main purpose.[1] The re-privatization of the banking system, in this reading, supplied the financial plumbing; under NAFTA Mexico was to open its market to other foreign banks, but the Salinas administration granted the new entrants only limited access, preserving much of [[citibank|Citibank]]'s special status.[1]
