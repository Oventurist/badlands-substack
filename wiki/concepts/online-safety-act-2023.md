# Online Safety Act 2023

The Online Safety Act (OSA) 2023 is the United Kingdom statute that grew out of the [[online-safety-bill|Online Safety Bill]], receiving Royal Assent on October 26, 2023 and thereby becoming an operational statute rather than a proposal.[1] Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] treated it as one of the principal legal frameworks by which governments in the [[rules-based-international-order|International Rules-Based Order]] have expanded their capacity to censor, alongside Canada's [[online-streaming-act|Online Streaming Act]] and the EU's [[digital-services-act|Digital Services Act]].[1]

## Criticism

Like Bill C-11, the OSA drew heavy criticism from technology firms, privacy advocates, free speech groups and political figures.[1] DeLarme, who read the original bill text in 2023, argued that beneath a mass of dull jargon sit vague but alarming clauses.[1] Section 10 of the bill's original text set out offenses of transmitting "harmful, false and threatening communications" while declining to define clearly what would count as "harmful" or by what criteria something would be judged "false."[1] Clause 156 made it a crime to send a message containing false information, while clause 157 immediately granted immunity to every newspaper, television channel and streaming service.[1]

## Surveillance provisions

DeLarme also identified provisions that expand mass surveillance capability. Clause 122 allows [[ofcom|Ofcom]], the Office of Communications, to mandate scanning for child sexual exploitation and abuse (CSEA) or terrorism content even on encrypted platforms, which critics — including Amnesty International — regard as undermining end-to-end encryption.[1] DeLarme compared the framing to that of the [[patriot-act|Patriot Act]], arguing that invoking terrorism and child sexual exploitation is a pretense used to justify the UK government spying on its own citizens' private communications.[1]

He argued that what the OSA shares with C-11 and the DSA is that all were passed over widespread opposition, reflecting top-down imposition rather than democratic consensus and thereby undermining their legitimacy.[1]
