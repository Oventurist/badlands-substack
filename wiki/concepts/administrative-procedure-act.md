# Administrative Procedure Act

The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) governs judicial review of federal agency action.[1]

## Section 706 and rule vacatur

APA §706 is a statutory hook that permits courts to "hold unlawful and set aside" an agency action found to be arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law, at the court's discretion.[1] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], drawing on [[congressional-research-service|Congressional Research Service]] analysis, argued that challengers increasingly seek — and lower federal courts increasingly grant — this "rule vacatur" remedy, precisely as the Supreme Court closed the door on nationwide injunctions.[1]

Related to this, she argued the lower courts have broadened standing theories during the second Trump administration so that states, NGOs and ordinary litigants can reach court without previously applicable barriers, while the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] granted stays in more than half of the administration's emergency "shadow docket" applications — implying, in her reading, an ideological divide between the district courts and the high court.[1]

## The APA and the 2025 probationary firings

In March 2025 the APA became the principal legal instrument used against the second [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration's mass firings of probationary federal workers. Labor unions and other plaintiffs challenging terminations directed by the [[office-of-personnel-management|Office of Personnel Management]] argued the firings violated APA requirements as well as congressional statutes governing agency hiring and firing practices, and U.S. District Judge [[william-alsup|William Alsup]] ordered thousands of workers reinstated across six agencies.[2]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the plaintiffs were effectively asserting that the president's Article II constitutional authorities violate the APA, and that they were clinging to a statute already substantially weakened by recent Supreme Court decisions.[2] She catalogued those rulings: Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (June 2024), which overturned Chevron deference and requires courts to independently interpret laws rather than defer to federal agencies; Seila Law LLC v. CFPB (2020), which weakened the independence of certain agencies and increased presidential control over regulatory action; DHS v. Regents of the University of California (2020), which reinforced strict procedural requirements for rescinding policies; West Virginia v. EPA (2022), which restricted agencies' power to issue broad regulations absent clear congressional authorization; and Jarkesy v. SEC, which held that agency use of administrative law judges violated the Seventh Amendment.[2] In her reading these decisions gutted the APA and stripped away much of the administrative state's shadow power, leaving the establishment to argue in effect that the Constitution violates the APA — and she suggested the statute should be abolished outright.[2]
