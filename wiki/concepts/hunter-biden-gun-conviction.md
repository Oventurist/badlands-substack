# Hunter Biden Gun Conviction

The **Hunter Biden gun conviction** was the June 11, 2024 verdict in which a federal jury in Wilmington, Delaware found [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] guilty on all three felony charges arising from a 2018 firearm purchase, marking the first time in American history that the son of a sitting president was criminally convicted.[1]

## The charges and verdict

The charges included lying on a federal form about his drug use in order to purchase a revolver, submitting a false statement into a federal record, and unlawfully possessing the firearm for eleven days.[1] The jury deliberated quickly, reaching its verdict in roughly three hours.[1] Hunter Biden faced a statutory maximum of up to 25 years in prison, although as a first-time offender his sentence was expected to be far shorter.[1]

## Presidential response

President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], who was in Washington when the verdict was read, issued a statement saying he would accept the outcome of the case and continue to respect the judicial process as his son weighed an appeal.[1] "As I said last week, I am the President, but I am also a Dad. I love our son, and we are so proud of the man he is today," the president said.[1] The statement also appealed to other families whose loved ones had struggled with addiction, a subject aired in detail at trial: "Jill and I love our son, and we are so proud of the man he is today. So many families who have had loved ones battle addiction understand the feeling of pride seeing someone you love come out the other side and be so strong and resilient in recovery."[1]

Burning Bright further argued that the conviction exposed a bifurcation between what he called the "[[con-inc|Con Inc.]] Doom Brigade" — commentators who joined a "get Hunter … but for the wrong crimes" bandwagon immediately after complaining about the weaponized [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] — and the more discerning members of the Truth Community analyzing events from first principles.[1] He described the establishment's use of the verdict as "Narrative Shielding," deployed as a cudgel to codify the Trump witch hunt, and expected the media to cement positions it would regret around 2025 "when the Justice phase kicks off in earnest."[1]

## Second Amendment briefing (January 2024)

Ahead of trial, federal prosecutors argued in a January 16, 2024 brief that the Second Amendment does not protect [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] from the felony gun charges.[2] "Anglo-American law has long recognized that the government may disarm those who, by their conduct or characteristics, present an increased risk to public safety if they possess firearms," prosecutors wrote, contending that the federal prohibition on gun ownership by people who use or are addicted to drugs remains valid under the Supreme Court's 2022 decision striking down New York's concealed-carry restrictions.[2]

Biden, then 53, faced three felony counts arising from his 2018 certification on a federal form that he was not a user of or addicted to drugs, a claim contradicted by his own memoir.[2] His lawyers argued in late 2023 that the statute is unconstitutional under court precedent including New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen, writing that "in truth, the statute is indefensible under the Bruen framework."[2]

## February 2024 evidence disclosure

In February 2024 the [[justice-department|Justice Department]] released evidence showing that [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] had photos of apparent cocaine and drug paraphernalia on his phone and discussed his drug addiction in text messages both before and after buying a firearm and asserting on a federal form that he was not using drugs.[3] "Prior to October 12, 2018 (the date of the gun purchase), the defendant took photos of crack cocaine and drug paraphernalia on his phone," the department wrote in a court document released that Tuesday.[3] The filing showed him discussing the price he paid for cocaine in April 2018 and asking in May 2018 for "baby powder" — a cutting agent — specifying "the really soft stuff."[3]

Prosecutors also said that "on October 13, 2018, and October 14, 2018 (the day after and two days after he purchased the firearm), the defendant messaged his girlfriend about meeting a drug dealer and smoking crack," and the document featured messages from October 23, 2018, the day his then-girlfriend discarded the firearm.[3]
