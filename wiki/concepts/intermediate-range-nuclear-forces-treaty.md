# Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty

The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was a 1987 arms control agreement between the United States and the Soviet Union, later inherited by Russia, that banned ground-launched missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres.[1] For three decades it functioned as one of the load-bearing pillars of the post-Cold War European security architecture, removing an entire class of short-flight-time nuclear delivery systems from the continent.[1]

## Collapse and aftermath

The treaty collapsed in 2019 when Washington formally withdrew, citing Russian violations of the range limits.[1] Moscow denied the accusations and countered that the United States was itself developing missiles prohibited under the accord.[1] Following the American withdrawal, Russia maintained a unilateral, self-imposed moratorium on deploying intermediate-range ground-launched systems, effectively preserving the treaty's central restraint in the absence of the treaty itself.[1]

## 2025 Russian repudiation

In August 2025 the Russian Foreign Ministry announced that the conditions for maintaining the moratorium had "disappeared" and that Russia "no longer considers itself bound" by the corresponding self-imposed restrictions previously adopted.[1] The statement effectively terminated the last surviving element of the INF framework. Russian President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] has warned that the collapse of the INF regime would significantly erode the global security framework.[1]

The announcement coincided with a broader escalation in nuclear signalling between Moscow and Washington, including inflammatory remarks by former Russian president [[dmitry-medvedev|Dmitry Medvedev]], President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[2025-us-submarine-repositioning|repositioning of two nuclear-powered submarines]], and a call for restraint in "nuclear rhetoric" from Kremlin spokesman [[dmitry-peskov|Dmitry Peskov]].[1]

## Commentary

Badlands commentators framed the moment as an opportunity to revisit what they termed the "nuclear stalemate" narrative, arguing that Cold War-era nuclear fear had been used to suppress the proliferation of clean, cheap nuclear energy worldwide and had thereby stifled development, particularly in the third world.[1]
