# Bill of Rights

The Bill of Rights is the set of amendments to the [[united-states-constitution|United States Constitution]] written and ratified in 1791, two years after the Constitution itself was ratified in 1789.[1] The September 2, 2024 Badlands News Brief treated the distinction between the two documents as the crux of contemporary constitutional argument.[1]

## Origin

According to the brief, the Bill of Rights was authored by [[james-madison|James Madison]] of the Federalist Party and offered as an olive branch to the Anti-Federalists, who had demanded that the rights of the people be addressed because the original Constitution appeared to focus exclusively on the rights and powers of government.[1]

## Interpretation offered in the brief

The commentary emphasized that the provisions of the Bill of Rights are called "amendments" precisely because they amended — in the writer's framing, superseded — the Constitution, establishing that American citizens hold inalienable rights derived from God rather than from government.[1] On that reading, any government that questions or threatens those rights is "unworthy of existence," a proposition the writer attributed directly to the founding documents and offered as the reason establishment figures dislike them.[1]

The piece argued that rather than groveling before "We The People," officials were instead issuing thinly veiled threats and using media assets to normalize the idea of discarding the Constitution.[1] The observation that most modern invocations of "the Constitution" are really invocations of the Bill of Rights was presented as evidence that the original charter's protections were always thinner than popularly assumed.[1]
