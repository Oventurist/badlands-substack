# 2026 Winter Olympics

The 2026 Winter Olympic Games became the subject of several viral controversies in mid-February 2026, dominated in commentary circles by a curling officiating dispute and an unusual doping-adjacent allegation involving ski jumpers.[1]

## Curling "double-touch" controversy

A rules controversy engulfed Olympic curling at the Games, as both the Canadian men's and women's teams were accused of committing "double-touch" infractions during round-robin competition.[1] The dispute erupted during an 8–6 men's victory over Sweden, when Swedish curler [[oskar-eriksson|Oskar Eriksson]] alleged that Canadian third [[marc-kennedy|Marc Kennedy]] had touched his stone after release — a violation that, if confirmed, requires removal of the stone under [[world-curling|World Curling]] rules.[1] Kennedy vehemently denied wrongdoing, responded with profanity on the ice, and later suggested his team had been deliberately targeted in a planned effort to catch violations.[1]

In a subsequent women's match against Switzerland, Canadian skip [[rachel-homan|Rachel Homan]] was penalized for the same double-touch violation and had her stone removed; she protested, but officials upheld the call without video review, citing the sport's officiating standards.[1] World Curling acknowledged that umpires cannot visually monitor every delivery infraction and clarified that while accredited Olympic Broadcasting Services may film within venues, viral footage circulating on social media was not authorized, adding that no violations had been recorded during designated umpire observation periods.[1]

The controversy widened as other nations, including the British men's squad, also had stones removed under the same rule enforcement, and World Curling responded by increasing the number of monitoring officials at subsequent matches to better enforce the hog-line rule.[1]

## "PenisGate"

Commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] recounted viral Olympic headlines alleging that ski jumpers were injecting hyaluronic acid into their penises to inflate them before body scans used to size their suits, so that once the swelling subsided the extra fabric would act "like a sail" in flight.[1] The anti-doping league was reported to be investigating the claim.[1]

## Commentary

Ashe in America noted the outsized attention curling received at these Games even before the Canadian allegations, described the Swedish response as a "surveillance sting operation," and drew an analogy to election investigations in which, she quipped, authorities would prosecute the whistleblowers rather than the cheaters — pointing to Canada's complaint that the Swedes had recorded violations inside the arena.[1]
