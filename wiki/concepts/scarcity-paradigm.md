# Scarcity Paradigm

The "Scarcity Paradigm" is a framing used by Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] to describe what he characterizes as a deliberately constructed narrative of physical limits and decline, rather than a neutral observation about resources.[1]

## Argument

In this account, scarcity has functioned as the central operating lie of the globalist deep state's Hegelian machinery — a narrative imposition refined over decades and then reinforced by engineered "Actuals" on the ground.[1] The macro narrative of scarcity is paired with concrete projects designed to make the constraint self-reinforcing, from destabilization operations in the Middle East and other energy hotbeds to corporate and governmental limits on domestic energy production and distribution.[1]

Burning Bright describes the dialectic as running on parallel tracks: foreign chokepoints that punish sovereign peoples through dependence on adversarial or unstable regimes, and domestic architectures that constrain output while laundering political and financial power through the administrative state.[1]

## Green New Deal era

The [[green-new-deal|Green New Deal]] era is presented as an especially aggressive acceleration of both tracks: policy marketed as climate and justice work that in practice locked in lower energy densities and routed enormous flows of public capital through networks of aligned nonprofits and politically connected entities.[1] The 2026 criminal referrals by [[lee-zeldin|Lee Zeldin]] over the [[greenhouse-gas-reduction-fund|Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund]] and related grants are cited as illustrating how those programs functioned once the layers were pulled back.[1]

## Reversal indicators

Burning Bright argues that two years of executive orders dismantling energy limits, restoring pathways in nuclear power and supersonics, and applying legal pressure to the administrative machine amount to a broader restoration of "energetic sovereignty."[1] He reads mainstream coverage as an involuntary reverse indicator: outlets that once celebrated offshore wind commitments now lament halted projects in states such as Massachusetts, where promised long-term industrial footprints largely delivered temporary construction booms before cost realities and investor flight intervened.[1] The same channels, he notes, amplify near-daily warnings about data center expansion, framing the compute infrastructure of a new American frontier as a looming crisis for grids, water, and rural communities.[1]
