# Article V

**Article V** of the [[united-states-constitution|United States Constitution]] establishes the formal amendment process — the lawful mechanism by which a constitutional republic changes its fundamental law.[1] Defenders of [[originalism|originalism]] describe Article V as the machinery that originalist interpretation is designed to protect, arguing that by distinguishing judicial from political processes, originalism safeguards democratic legitimacy, popular sovereignty, and the formal amendment process at the heart of the American Founding.[1]

## The Lepore Dispute

Article V became the focal point of a public argument after Harvard historian [[jill-lepore|Jill Lepore]] published "How Originalism Has Killed the Constitution" in The Atlantic, whose central thesis was that originalism thwarts constitutional change and kills the amendment process.[1] Critics writing in Law & Liberty — among them legal scholar Robert Natelson and historian Paul Moreno — contended that the argument gets matters precisely backward, and that originalism in fact revives Article V by channeling constitutional change back to the amendment route.[1]

## State-Legislature Amendment Campaigns

Article V also underpins active campaigns to propose amendments through the state legislatures rather than through Congress. As described in Badlands Media's coverage, 28 states had certified a balanced budget amendment and 12 states had certified term limits; once 34 states certify, a proposed amendment can be sent for ratification, which requires approval from 38 states.[1] Commentary in the brief predicted that if a term limits amendment advanced, the precedent would be used to argue for dismantling the [[twenty-second-amendment-third-term-loophole|Twenty-Second Amendment]].[1] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] dismissed term limits as a "non-solution that sounds like a good solution to people who don't understand the problems," drawing a comparison to arguments over the [[smith-mundt-act|Smith-Mundt Act]].[1]
