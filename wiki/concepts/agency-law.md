# Agency Law

Agency law governs the relationship in which one party is authorized to act on behalf of another. In Badlands Media's account of how [[federal-reserve|the Federal Reserve]] came to hold authority over the credit of the United States, agency is the mechanism that transmits corporate jurisdiction from the federal government to the individual citizen.[1]

## Definition

The article cites the legal definition of an agent as "a person authorized to act on behalf of another person," noting that an agent may sign paperwork, make agreements, acquire and dispose of assets, enter contracts and spend the principal's money as if he were the principal himself.[1]

## Scope of authorization

Scope is illustrated with an everyday example: asking a roommate to buy apples designates him as an agent only with respect to buying apples, whereas asking him to buy "anything that looks good" authorizes him to purchase literally anything, and specifying "$20.00 worth of anything that looks good" limits the authorization by value.[1] The article treats the concept of an agent as more or less equivalent to that of a representative.[1]

## Agents need not be individuals

Because a corporation possesses [[legal-personhood]], it can serve as an agent; likewise a congressional representative is described as one of the citizen's agents.[1] Every time an individual or group is authorized to do anything on another's behalf, an agency relationship is created.[1]

## The central claim

From these premises the article draws what it calls the big revelation: the United States federal government, acting as the citizen's agent, became the *customer* of the Federal Reserve on the citizen's behalf.[1] By congressional act the United States made itself liable to pay the Federal Reserve system in order to receive the benefits of using Federal Reserve Notes.[1] Because the Federal Reserve is a corporate system, customers must abide by its internal corporate code to enjoy the benefit of its credit, and every issuance of that credit adds to the debt of the United States under the ordinary creditor-debtor relation.[1] The result, the article concludes, amounts to a corporate contract of indebtedness.[1] See [[jurisdiction]], [[federal-reserve-note]] and [[bastard-system-of-laws]].
