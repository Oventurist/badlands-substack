# Kansas-Nebraska Act

The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 was the federal statute that opened the territories west of the Mississippi to the possible extension of slavery.

## Role in the growth of the slave power

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s Avoiding WWIII identifies the Act as the measure under which "the slave power grew immensely," ensuring the spread of slavery west of the Mississippi. The article ties it to the explosive growth of the cotton economy: exports rose from 100,000 bales a year in 1801 to one million by 1840 and to four million by 1860, with 80 per cent of the trade going to Britain.

Ehret describes a "City of London–Wall Street–New Orleans triangle" dominating the world system, with New Orleans alone representing over 12 per cent of all U.S. banking capital, and the southern slave states growing into the world's fourth largest economy with British financial and logistical support for the importation of mass slavery into the Americas. He dates the acceleration of this degeneration to the presidencies of [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] and "his handler" Martin Van Buren. Against the backdrop of these developments, the article notes that while many radical abolitionists in the United States and British Canada advocated dissolving the Union as an alternative to civil war, "stronger souls" such as Frederick Douglass recognized the higher historic fight at hand.
