# FBI SCIF Records Cache

The FBI SCIF records cache refers to a body of as many as 1.9 million pages of records that the [[fbi|Federal Bureau of Investigation]] identified in March 2026 in response to a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit, reportedly stored inside a secured room at FBI headquarters.[1]

## Discovery and scope

According to a filing by the US [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]], the documents are housed in a Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility (SCIF), where multiple safes contain between roughly 950,000 and 1.9 million pages. The bureau stated the records must first be digitized and reviewed before it can determine what is responsive to the underlying FOIA request, a process it estimated would take 10 to 12 months, with a classification review likely required given the nature of the storage.[1]

The filing indicates the materials include legacy records tied largely to two closed investigations, together with more recent files connected to active probes and prior Special Counsel matters. Many of the legacy records fall under the authority of the [[department-of-justice-office-of-inspector-general|Department of Justice Office of Inspector General]] and must be reviewed in consultation with that office before release, while other documents may require coordination with additional federal agencies.[1]

## Litigation

The August 2025 lawsuit stems from a June FOIA request the FBI failed to fulfill, prompting litigation. Beyond the records themselves, the case seeks internal FBI communications involving Director [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] and former Deputy Director [[dan-bongino|Dan Bongino]] regarding the discovery of the cache, along with any directives governing how the materials were handled.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] asked how many secret rooms exist at the FBI, noting the implausibility that no one thought to check the safes in the secret SCIF before fulfilling prior FOIA requests. Her central reading was that the bureau had effectively bought itself another year of obstruction, and she contrasted the treatment of the agency with that of a private citizen, who would face prison for telling the government it needed an extra year to redact material it preferred to withhold.[1]

She concluded that government instead receives the additional time, keeps its secrets, and may release the truth in a reading room decades later — long after accountability is possible — invoking the line that "for too long, a small group in our nation's capital has reaped the rewards of government while the people have borne the cost," and asking when that would change.[1]
