# Deregulation

Deregulation, in the account given by Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]], was a deliberate narrative deployment by the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] rather than a genuine effort to streamline a bloated federal state.[1] Lange writes that he himself was fooled by it, just as he was later fooled by [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]]'s push for "free trade," whose real purpose he says was to massively increase drug trafficking across the Mexican border through [[nafta|NAFTA]].[1]

## Origins under Reagan

Reducing regulatory burdens was one of four "pillars" of [[ronald-reagan|President Ronald Reagan]]'s 1981 economic recovery agenda, as described by [[heritage-foundation|The Heritage Foundation]].[1] On taking office Reagan established the [[task-force-on-regulatory-relief|Task Force on Regulatory Relief]], chaired by Vice President Bush, to oversee reform, and issued an executive order barring any regulation whose costs exceeded its benefits while placing the newly created OIRA in charge of reviewing new rules.[1] Reagan's announcement promised "real reform and tangible results" and named Bush as a "vigorous leader, talented administrator, and absolutely, no doubt, a superb diplomat" to coordinate the interagency effort.[1] Reagan's famous formulation — "In the present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem" — served as the slogan of the campaign.[1]

## Banking deregulation and its consequences

The most consequential product of the effort, in Lange's telling, was the deregulation of the banking industry, allowing savings and loans to make riskier investments to cope with high interest rates.[1] Where thrifts had previously been limited to residential loans funded by depositors, the 1982 changes permitted commercial loans, "unsecured" business loans, and large investments in commercial real estate that could be 100 percent financed with no money down.[1] Combined with the 1980 increase in federal [[deposit-insurance|deposit insurance]] from $40,000 to $100,000 per account, this removed all risk from thrift operators gambling with other people's money.[1]

Lange argues these reforms "kicked down the door" that had prevented fraud in the savings and loan industry and stripped away safeguards in place since the Great Depression, making deregulation the catalyst of the entire [[savings-and-loan-crisis|savings and loan crisis]].[1] When Congress announced it intended to deregulate thrifts, [[herman-k-beebe|Herman K. Beebe]] "immediately saw the possibilities," and word spread that anyone needing money to take control of a thrift should see him — deregulation functioning, in Lange's phrase, as a siren call to every crook in the country.[1] Many writers allege the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] used the thrift deregulation process to help pay for covert operations Congress would not or could not support.[1]
