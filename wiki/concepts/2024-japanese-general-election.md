# 2024 Japanese General Election

The 2024 Japanese general election was a snap parliamentary contest held on Sunday, October 27, 2024, in which voters dealt Japan's longtime governing party a resounding blow and, in the framing of the New York Times, sent Asia's most stable democracy into chaos.[1]

## Result

The [[liberal-democratic-party|Liberal Democratic Party]], which has dominated Japanese politics for most of the postwar era, lost its majority in the lower house of Parliament.[1] On the surface the center appeared to hold, since the Constitutional Democrats — who won the second-most seats — are another relatively centrist party.[1] But minority parties on both the far left and the far right gained seats, a pattern read as evidence that Japan's long resistance to the populist waves that swept Europe and the United States was eroding.[1]

## Causes

Prime minister [[shigeru-ishiba|Shigeru Ishiba]], selected by the Liberal Democrats only a month before the vote, blamed the party's dismal showing on a protracted political finance scandal.[1] Analysts said voter grievance ran deeper. Kunihiko Miyake, a former Japanese diplomat and special adviser at the Canon Institute for Global Studies in Tokyo, pointed to "the last 30 years of stagnation and the deterioration of living standards, especially for young people."[1] Badlands commentary framed the rejection as a vote against inflation and global/western monetary policy.[1]

## Irregularity claims

Badlands noted that the result was accompanied by reports of an ineligible foreign voter whose ballot was allowed to stand, other claimed irregularities, and lawsuits filed — from the left — seeking to nullify the general election over vote disparities.[1] Writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] placed Japan within a global 2024 pattern of contested outcomes, arguing it offered a glimpse of one possible outcome for the United States election one week later.[1]

## Global context

Time magazine had reported in December 2023 that 2024 was "perhaps the election year," with at least 64 countries representing roughly 49% of the world's population going to the polls.[1] Badlands surveyed those results as evidence of a multipolar realignment: incumbents fleeing in Bangladesh, disputed military influence in Pakistan against Imran Khan's party, a US-described "sham" election in Belarus under Alexander Lukashenko, alleged EU meddling in Moldova, and dueling meddling claims in Georgia.[1] Landslides included [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] at 90% in Russia and Nayib Bukele at roughly 85% in El Salvador, while incumbent parties lost majorities in India, Madagascar, South Africa and elsewhere.[1] The brief closed by asking whether US elections could produce a legitimate and trustworthy outcome given the record of American democracy promotion abroad.[1]
