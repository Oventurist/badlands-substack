# Trafficking Victims Protection Act

The Trafficking Victims Protection Act is the federal statute under which damages may be sought on behalf of sex trafficking victims in the United States.[1]

## Role in the Epstein settlement dispute

In October 2023 the statute became central to an objection filed against [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]]'s $290 million class-action settlement with accusers of [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]].[1] The attorneys general of 16 states and Washington, D.C. complained in a letter made public in Manhattan federal court that the settlement contained language preventing "any sovereign or government" from seeking damages arising from sex trafficking by Epstein and his associates.[1]

The officials argued that including such a release without their consent would deter states from pursuing damages under the Trafficking Victims Protection Act for sex trafficking victims generally — not only those victimized by Epstein.[1] New Mexico Attorney General [[raul-torrez|Raul Torrez]] wrote that the agreement "improperly seeks to release (the states') claims for victim-specific relief."[1] By contrast, [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]]'s $75 million settlement with Epstein accusers contained no comparable language.[1]

## Obstruction claims against Epstein's banks (March 2023)

The statute also underpinned the civil suits brought against [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]] and [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]] by accusers of [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]].[2] On March 20, 2023, U.S. District Judge [[jed-rakoff|Jed Rakoff]] ruled that two plaintiffs suing as "Jane Doe" could proceed with claims that the banks "knowingly benefited from participating in a sex-trafficking venture" and "obstructed enforcement of the Trafficking Victims Protection Act," alongside negligence claims, while dismissing their remaining causes of action.[2] Rakoff separately allowed the [[us-virgin-islands|U.S. Virgin Islands]] to pursue its own knowing-benefit claim against JPMorgan.[2]
