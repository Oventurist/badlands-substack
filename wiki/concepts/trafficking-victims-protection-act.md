# Trafficking Victims Protection Act

The Trafficking Victims Protection Act is the federal statute under which damages may be sought on behalf of sex trafficking victims in the United States.[1]

## Role in the Epstein settlement dispute

In October 2023 the statute became central to an objection filed against [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]]'s $290 million class-action settlement with accusers of [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]].[1] The attorneys general of 16 states and Washington, D.C. complained in a letter made public in Manhattan federal court that the settlement contained language preventing "any sovereign or government" from seeking damages arising from sex trafficking by Epstein and his associates.[1]

The officials argued that including such a release without their consent would deter states from pursuing damages under the Trafficking Victims Protection Act for sex trafficking victims generally — not only those victimized by Epstein.[1] New Mexico Attorney General [[raul-torrez|Raul Torrez]] wrote that the agreement "improperly seeks to release (the states') claims for victim-specific relief."[1] By contrast, [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]]'s $75 million settlement with Epstein accusers contained no comparable language.[1]

## Obstruction claims against Epstein's banks (March 2023)

The statute also underpinned the civil suits brought against [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]] and [[deutsche-bank|Deutsche Bank]] by accusers of [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]].[2] On March 20, 2023, U.S. District Judge [[jed-rakoff|Jed Rakoff]] ruled that two plaintiffs suing as "Jane Doe" could proceed with claims that the banks "knowingly benefited from participating in a sex-trafficking venture" and "obstructed enforcement of the Trafficking Victims Protection Act," alongside negligence claims, while dismissing their remaining causes of action.[2] Rakoff separately allowed the [[us-virgin-islands|U.S. Virgin Islands]] to pursue its own knowing-benefit claim against JPMorgan.[2]

## Enactment and congressional findings

The Act was passed by the United States [[us-congress|Congress]] on October 28, 2000, and expanded the ability of law enforcement to investigate instances of trafficking where no legal basis for doing so had previously existed.[3] Its opening section of congressional findings stated that existing legislation and law enforcement in the United States and other countries were inadequate to deter trafficking and bring traffickers to justice, and that no comprehensive law existed in the United States penalising the range of offenses involved in the trafficking scheme, so that even the most brutal instances of trafficking in the sex industry were punished under laws that also applied to lesser offenses and traffickers typically escaped deserved punishment.[3] Further findings recorded that sentencing guidelines did not reflect the seriousness of the crime, that existing laws often failed to protect victims — who, being frequently illegal immigrants in the destination country, were repeatedly punished more harshly than the traffickers themselves — and that victims of severe forms of trafficking should not be incarcerated, fined, or otherwise penalised solely for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of being trafficked, such as using false documents or entering or working in a country without documentation.[3] Finding (20) noted that unfamiliarity with local laws, cultures and languages, coercion and intimidation including physical detention and debt bondage, and fear of retribution or forcible removal make it difficult or impossible for victims to report crimes or assist in prosecutions, while finding (24) characterised trafficking in persons as a transnational crime with national implications.[3]

## Definition of coercion

Section 103 of the Act supplies a broad definition of coercion, encompassing threats of serious harm to or physical restraint against any person, and any scheme, plan, or pattern intended to cause a person to believe that failure to perform an act would result in serious harm or physical restraint.[3] The same section defines involuntary servitude to include a condition of servitude induced by any scheme, plan, or pattern intended to cause a person to believe that non-compliance would result in serious harm or physical restraint to that person or another.[3] Badlands contributor [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]] emphasised that the statute therefore recognises verbal, emotional and mental manipulation and fraud alongside physical coercion, which he described as crucial because without a legal framework for identifying and prosecuting non-physical coercion traffickers and cartels can readily escape prosecution.[3]

## Section 105 and international cooperation

Section 105 establishes a set of expectations for foreign countries to assist in bringing traffickers to justice.[3] American Hypnotist characterised the section as "peppercorn in scope," since non-cooperating countries are penalised only by the revocation of a small portion of their funding, but argued that the establishment of any framework for international cooperation on the issue was a meaningful start, and that the Act's standards would become the de facto international standard the moment the United States began to enforce them seriously.[3]

## Successor legislation and Badlands commentary

Numerous subsequent acts have refined or added to the TVPA; a new version was proposed in 2017 by Senator [[chuck-grassley|Chuck Grassley]] and renamed the Frederick Douglas Trafficking Prevention Act in 2018.[3] American Hypnotist observed that October 28, 2000 falls exactly seventeen years to the day before [[qanon|Q]]'s first post, treating the coincidence as a deliberate "comm" and arguing that Q intended anons to discover the statute.[3] He contended that much of the TVPA lies dormant, awaiting use against trafficking networks and their accomplices, and that he was unaware of any large-scale official campaign under its banner with the possible exception of the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]].[3] Extending the Act's fraud and coercion provisions, he argued that a media complex that intentionally gaslights the public to run cover for traffickers, or that reliably discredits trafficking victims, would supply plausible cause to investigate those companies as potential co-conspirators.[3]
