# Hemispheric Specialization

**Hemispheric specialization** is the proposition that the left and right hemispheres of the brain attend to the world in categorically different ways, rather than functioning as redundant dual processors.[1] The Badlands Media essay "Expand Your Thinking" treats the idea, as developed by the neuroscientist [[iain-mcgilchrist|Iain McGilchrist]], as an established fact of neurology and as the biological basis for its argument about how to update one's beliefs.[1]

## The mainstream objection

The essay recounts that for decades many neuroscientists took it as given that the hemispheres do not specialize in different kinds of cognitive processing, since both are observed to participate in language processing, information acquisition, emotion and orientation in spacetime.[1] It characterizes this inference as itself a symptom of left-brain thinking and questions the sufficiency of imaging studies, comparing the observation of which brain regions "light up" under stimulus to watching characters appear on a computer screen and calling that an understanding of what is happening inside the computer.[1]

## Evidence from injury

According to the article, the distinctive natures of the hemispheres emerge when a person suffers damage to one side and not the other, that the resulting differences are consistent across patients rather than idiosyncratic, and that comparable specialization can be observed in animals.[1] A specific example given is *palinopsia*, a condition that can follow right-hemisphere damage, in which vision becomes "atomized" and broken into a series of still frames like the individual frames of a film.[1] The essay reads this as demonstrating that the left hemisphere's specialty is to see things in isolation rather than as connected, and that the experienced flow of time is a product of right-hemisphere activity.[1]

## Division of labour

On this account the left hemisphere reduces reality into static, manipulable parts, producing a map rather than direct experience — a capacity the article regards as indispensable, since nothing practical could be accomplished without reducing the world to workable components.[1] The right hemisphere, by contrast, sees things whole and in context.[1] Novelty is said to activate the right brain; once the novel has been categorized, compartmentalized or normalized, it becomes the province of the left.[1] The article stresses that neither hemisphere is presented as the "better" one, and that the danger lies only in becoming stuck in a self-generated reality without regular infusions of right-brained awareness.[1]

## Left-brain imbalance

The essay uses the term *left-brain imbalance* (or dominance) for the condition in which the left hemisphere, absent corrective input from the right, imposes its own reasoning on reality and refuses to acknowledge sensory evidence that contradicts its cherished maps.[1] It illustrates the refusal with an anecdote from Clyde Raymond Miller's *The Process of Persuasion*: a farmer who had never seen a giraffe, on encountering one when the circus came to town, pointed at it and exclaimed, "there ain't no such animal!"[1] The left brain is said to be more concerned with utility than with meaning or truth, so that people who go along to get along and never pursue deep truth for its own sake drift toward imbalance over time.[1]

The article warns that the temptation to conclude one does not suffer from this imbalance may itself be a symptom of it, and proposes humility about one's own comprehension as the principal safeguard against confusing the map with the territory.[1] It further reports that some researchers hold that humanity many thousands of years ago lived primarily in the right brain, which is offered as an explanation for ancient peoples seeing nature as a unified, living language of God — a perspective the author notes is precisely the one modern academia is most likely to ridicule.[1]
