# Election Denial

"Election denial" is the pejorative label applied to Americans who publicly question the outcome or integrity of elections, and who are frequently characterized in mainstream discourse as the greatest threat to democracy.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Defenders argue the practice is neither novel nor intimidating, but a long-standing and fully protected exercise of the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] that has historically driven electoral reform.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Historical precedents

The 1800 presidential contest between Thomas Jefferson and incumbent John Adams went to the House of Representatives, which elected Jefferson after 36 rounds of voting; the episode inspired the Twelfth Amendment.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The 1824 election of John Quincy Adams likewise went to the House when no candidate won an electoral majority, and was also settled after 36 ballots.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The 1860 election, in which Abraham Lincoln's name was struck from the ballot in ten Southern states, was reported as unprecedented at the time and was followed within weeks by South Carolina's secession.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The 1864 wartime election introduced mail-in ballots, which many argued were vulnerable to manipulation and fraudulent voting.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

Machine politics supplied further grounds: William "Boss" Tweed of [[tammany-hall|Tammany Hall]] rose to power in New York in 1863 and punished those who questioned his electoral practices, dying in prison in 1878.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) In 1876 Congress created a fifteen-member commission to resolve the Tilden–Hayes contest; its award to Rutherford B. Hayes produced the February 1877 compromise that ended Reconstruction by withdrawing federal troops from the South.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) By 1888 ballot fraud had its own vocabulary — "colonizers," "floaters," and "repeaters" — and Grover Cleveland's call on citizens to "restore the purity of their suffrage" fueled a ballot-reform wave that brought the secret ballot to 38 states by 1892.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Modern era

In 1948 the *Chicago Tribune*'s "Dewey Defeats Truman" headline demonstrated media fallibility, as Harry S. Truman — approved by only one in three Americans and himself believing he had lost — won.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) In 2000, broadcast media wrongly called Florida for Al Gore; recounts favored George W. Bush and the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] halted the Florida recount on equal-protection grounds.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) That controversy produced the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]] of 2002.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

Since HAVA, no presidential election has passed without allegations of irregularities, fraud, or corruption from both political parties.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) John Kerry raised voter-access and suppression concerns in 2004; ACORN conceded in 2008 that only about 450,000 of the 1.3 million voters it registered were legitimate; and Pew Research Center's 2012 Survey of the Performance of American Elections found nearly 20 million voters experienced at least one problem voting.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) After [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s unexpected 2016 defeat by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], Democrats alleged Russian collusion and launched the Mueller investigation, which cost taxpayers millions and found no evidence of collusion; Clinton wrote an election-denying book and toured speaking about it, and Democrats produced the film *Kill Chain: The Cyber War on America's Elections* about vulnerabilities in the centralized voting system.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

The [[league-of-women-voters-colorado|League of Women Voters]] published "The 2016 Presidential Election was Rigged" on November 23, 2016, and [[free-speech-for-people|Free Speech For People]] — later counsel for the plaintiffs in [[naacp-v-useip|*NAACP v. USEIP*]] — launched an impeachment campaign on Inauguration Day 2017.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Critics conclude that election-questioning is treated as intimidation only when it concerns 2020 and Trump.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Shifting narrative, 2026

By May 2026 the American narrative on election denial was described as rapidly shifting, with scepticism about election outcomes characterised as more popular and more bipartisan than ever before.[2] The seventeenth episode of [[the-blitz|The Blitz]], hosted by [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] and [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] and titled "Big Trump Visits Little China as Election Deniers Seize the Narrative," devoted a segment to this turn, framing election deniers as having seized narrative control rather than being marginalised by it.[2]

## The Tina Peters case as narrative anchor

In March 2026, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the imprisonment of [[tina-peters|Tina Peters]] functions as the load-bearing element of the election-denial narrative in Colorado, calling her the state establishment's "most valuable political scalp" and "the absolute last gasp" of a carefully constructed story about election denialism being dangerous to democracy.[3] On her account, officials needed that framing because it had been used to justify weaponizing government against political opponents.[3] The same brief noted the reverse case: Rep. [[jasmine-crockett|Jasmine Crockett]], a Democrat, alleged voter disenfranchisement after losing the [[2026-texas-primary-elections|2026 Texas Senate primary]] without any formal finding of fraud, illustrating how contestation of election administration is treated differently depending on the party of the person raising it.[3]

## Mainstream media framing (2026)

In May 2026 The Atlantic profiled cybersecurity expert [[clay-parikh|Clay Parikh]] as "a bit player in the world of election denial," citing his claims about felt-tip markers invalidating ballots and about federal lawmakers covering up foreign election interference.[4] Badlands commentary treated the coverage as proof of momentum rather than marginalization, agreeing with the magazine's premise that "the election deniers are winning" and attributing that success to the truth of the movement's message.[4]
