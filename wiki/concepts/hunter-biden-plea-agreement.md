# Hunter Biden Plea Agreement (2023)

The **Hunter Biden plea agreement** was a negotiated resolution announced on the morning of Tuesday, June 20, 2023, under which [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]], then 53 and the son of sitting President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], agreed to plead guilty to two federal tax violations and to resolve a single federal firearms charge without a conviction.[1] The arrangement was reported the same day as the scheduling of a trial date in the [[2023-trump-classified-documents-indictment|classified documents case]] against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], a coincidence that shaped much of the commentary around it.[1]

## Terms

Under the agreement, Hunter Biden was to plead guilty to willful failure to pay federal income tax — most likely, according to contemporaneous reporting, on his foreign earnings — while the gun charge was to be handled through a "pretrial diversion program," an outcome that indicated he would face no jail time.[1] The firearms allegation concerned possession of a firearm by a person who is an unlawful user of or addicted to illegal drugs, a felony that on paper carries a $250,000 fine and a five-year mandatory sentence.[1] [[david-weiss|David Weiss]], the U.S. Attorney for Delaware, agreed to recommend a sentence of probation on the tax felonies.[1] Commentators characterized the tax pleas as amounting to two misdemeanor counts and the overall package as a resolution that closed the matter as far as [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]]'s [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] was concerned.[1]

## Criticism and the two-tier framing

Badlands Media writers treated the deal as the clearest available demonstration of a [[two-tier-justice-system|two-tier justice system]].[1] [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] contrasted the probationary recommendation with what an ordinary defendant would face, noting that more than 150 Suspicious Activity Reports had been filed involving the Bidens and that any ordinary family with even one such [[suspicious-activity-report|SAR]] could expect to be investigated.[1] He summarized the outcome as "millions of dollars, concealed income, influence peddling — two years probation."[1]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the deal functioned as narrative cover, allowing officials and allied media to claim Hunter Biden had been prosecuted without addressing any of the 459 documented violations of the U.S. Code alleged to be evidenced on the [[hunter-biden-laptop|Hunter Biden laptop]].[1] She also highlighted a conflict-of-interest claim attributed to [[mike-davis|Mike Davis]], former Chief Counsel to Senate Judiciary Chairman [[chuck-grassley|Chuck Grassley]]: that federal prosecutor Derek Hines had served as special counsel to former FBI Director [[louis-freeh|Louis Freeh]] from 2013 to February 2015, and that in April 2016 Freeh gave $100,000 to trusts for two of then-Vice President Biden's grandchildren.[1]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode at the level of narrative rather than law, observing that the son of a sitting Democratic president pleading guilty to tax and gun crimes was itself a scandal, that the perceived leniency was becoming a larger scandal than the underlying conduct, and that the simultaneous release of Trump's trial date fixed the two-tier storyline as the defining theme of 2023.[1] [[just-human|Just Human]] was credited with predicting the shape of the deal on the prior week's episode of DEFECTED.[1]

## Background

Hunter Biden's business dealings first drew attention during the Obama administration, after he was discharged from the Navy for drug use and immediately joined the board of the Ukrainian energy company [[burisma|Burisma]].[1] Reporters questioned the [[white-house|White House]] about the arrangement given that his father, then Vice President under [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]], had been placed in charge of Ukraine policy; [[state-department|State Department]] officials complained about the conflict of interest but were told the Vice President was grieving for his son [[beau-biden|Beau Biden]], and the issue was never resolved.[1]

## Collapse in court, July 26, 2023

The agreement came apart on Wednesday, July 26, 2023, when U.S. District Judge [[maryellen-noreika|Maryellen Noreika]] declined to accept it during an hours-long hearing in Delaware federal court punctuated by multiple dramatic recesses.[2] Noreika objected to the "broad" immunity the deal afforded and to its "unique" structure, at times describing the arrangement as potentially "unconstitutional" and as lacking "legal precedent."[2] She asked attorneys on both sides several times whether any precedent or authority existed for the agreement, and each time was told there was none.[2] "I cannot accept the plea agreement today," she said.[2]

The judge ordered both defense counsel and federal prosecutors to submit further paperwork justifying the legal legitimacy of the plea deal and of the separate agreement under which prosecutors would dismiss the weapons felony.[2] She also ordered the parties to clarify language that could grant Hunter Biden immunity from future prosecution over the foreign investment and consulting work through which he had earned millions of dollars over the previous decade.[2] With the deal deferred, Hunter Biden pleaded not guilty to the federal charges and skipped a planned post-hearing press conference; his attorneys said they needed roughly two weeks to answer the judge's questions, leaving the timing of any revisited deal unclear.[2]

The June 2023 charges accused the president's son of willfully failing to pay tax on more than $1.5 million in income across 2017 and 2018.[2] In court, prosecutors said he had taken in $2.4 million in 2017 and $2.1 million in 2018 through the Ukrainian energy firm [[burisma|Burisma]], a Chinese development firm, and domestic business interests and legal services.[2]

## Media framing

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that Americans had been outraged by the original announcement of the deal, "rightfully decrying the unfair and unprecedented leniency with which the DOJ treated the first son," and argued that Noreika's ruling showed the judge agreed with the public.[2] She catalogued the divergent verbs used by major outlets to describe the same event — the deal was variously "on hold" (AP, CNN, USA Today), "delayed" (New York Times), "in doubt" (Washington Post), "fell apart" (CBS, Bloomberg, Politico, Fox), "derailed" (NBC), "torpedoed" (New York Post) and "collapsed" (BBC) — observing that each descriptor was accurate but that the choices revealed the outlets' inherent biases.[2]
