# Big Bad Wolf Parables

"Big bad wolf parables" refers to [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]]'s reading, in the [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay *Fake News Until Proven Real*, of the classic children's wolf stories as encoded warnings about the [[deep-state|Deep State]] and the media.[1] Carlson argues that while people have been lied to their whole lives, they have also been warned from early on, and asks readers to notice how many children's stories concern the big bad wolf — which he identifies as the Satanic cabal, the deceiver who lies with the intention of devouring.[1]

## The Wolf in Sheep's Clothing

In the fable, the sheep do not trust the wolf while they recognise it as a wolf; only once they believe it is one of them do they follow it away from the farmer's protection, and they survive only because the farmer kills the wolf first.[1] Carlson reads the flock as having stopped thinking and let the wolf tell them what to think, never realising they had been led astray.[1]

He applies this directly to surveillance: when the [[cia|CIA]] ran an information-collecting operation called [[lifelog|LifeLog]], nobody was going to hand over their personal information; once the same objective was presented as a social media site, [[facebook|Facebook]], people freely supplied everything needed to monitor and control them.[1]

## The Three Little Pigs

Carlson maps the three pigs onto degrees of defence against media deception.[1] The pig with the straw house did a poor job of protecting himself from the lies, the twig house somewhat better, and both depended on the brick-house pig's discernment to survive.[1] In his rendering the first pig watched [[cnn|CNN]], the second [[fox-news|FOX News]], and the third took the bulk of his information from [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]]; the first two reacted in fear to the news they consumed while the third used discernment and repeatedly had to walk the others back from the emotional cliff after they fell for propaganda.[1]

## Little Red Riding Hood

The girl travelling through the woods tells the wolf what she is doing and where she is going, which Carlson equates to willingly handing all one's information to social media and thereby enabling better control and deception.[1] On arriving at the house of someone she trusts completely, she finds the wolf has eaten her grandmother and intends to eat her — the wolf having disguised itself as a trustworthy, harmless old woman, much as the media disguises itself as an unbiased and trustworthy source of information.[1]

## The Boy Who Cried Wolf

Carlson considers this the most interesting of the wolf stories.[1] He casts the townspeople as the American people and the boy as the media: the boy elicits fear and exerts control repeatedly until the townspeople stop reacting emotionally to his claims, at which point the claims lose all power.[1] In his reading, by declining to react emotionally the people cause the deliverer of the news to die, and the machine of control is starved of fuel — the only way to avoid the emotional reaction being to disbelieve and distrust the news from the very beginning.[1] The point connects to [[fear-as-a-control-mechanism|fear as a control mechanism]] and [[media-distrust-as-default|media distrust as default]].[1]
