# Proximal Origin Paper

"Proximal Origin" is the shorthand name for the influential early-pandemic scientific paper whose authors publicly argued that SARS-CoV-2 had a natural origin, and which was widely used by media outlets and public-health officials to dismiss the [[lab-leak-theory|lab leak theory]] as a conspiracy theory.[1]

## Slack message disclosures

In July 2023, ZeroHedge — drawing on reporting by the outlets Racket and Public — described newly released Slack messages showing that the paper's authors privately entertained the possibility of a laboratory origin even as they publicly discounted it, with one exchange characterizing a Wuhan lab leak as "so friggin' likely."[1] All of the paper's authors reportedly declined to comment to the reporting outlets, as did Jeremy Farrar and [[francis-collins|Francis Collins]].[1]

Yuri Deigin, one of the independent researchers who had been dismissed for years as a crackpot for raising laboratory-origin hypotheses, told reporters that he felt "somewhat" vindicated, adding that "the Slack messages confirm what we long suspected."[1]

## Interpretation and limits

The reporting stressed that the documents do not prove the virus escaped from the [[wuhan-institute-of-virology|Wuhan Institute of Virology]], nor that American scientists were implicated in such an episode.[1] What the documents were said to show instead was that both scientists and journalists abandoned open-minded inquiry in favour of a discipline that explicitly weighed political factors and sought a "single message."[1] Josh Rogin of the Washington Post was singled out as one of the few mainstream journalists who continued to pursue the story.[1] Economist and physician [[jay-bhattacharya|Jay Bhattacharya]] responded to the disclosures by asking, "How does the public ever trust science again?"[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that the episode illustrated the breakdown of science as an institution, defining science by adherence to empiricism — basing conclusions on observation rather than bias or political interest — and objectivity, the minimization of bias from institutional doctrine, consensus, or political pressure.[1] He contended that a scientist who abandons those principles ceases to be a scientist in the same way a priest who rejects the tenets of a faith ceases to be its disciple, and described the conduct surrounding the paper as a "canary in the coal mine" for truth-divining institutions generally.[1] The commentary further argued that each accepted lie increases the capacity of institutions to push further lies, and that a sufficiently patient falsification effort can rewrite history once living witnesses are gone.[1]

## House GOP findings on the paper's origins (March 2023)

In March 2023, congressional investigators on the [[house-select-subcommittee-on-the-coronavirus-pandemic|House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Pandemic]] accused [[anthony-fauci|Dr. Anthony Fauci]] of having "prompted" the fabrication of the paper by a cadre of scientists in order to disprove the COVID-19 [[lab-leak-theory|lab-leak theory]].[2] According to the subcommittee memo, on February 1, 2020 Fauci, [[national-institutes-of-health|NIH]] Director [[francis-collins|Dr. Francis Collins]], and at least eleven other scientists took part in a conference call during which several participants warned that COVID-19 may have leaked from a lab in Wuhan, China, and may have been intentionally genetically manipulated.[2]

Three days after that call, four participants — Scripps Research virologist [[kristian-andersen|Kristian Andersen]], University of Sydney virologist Edward Holmes, Tulane School of Medicine virologist [[robert-garry|Robert Garry]], University of Edinburgh virologist Andrew Rambaut, together with Columbia University virologist Ian Lipkin — seemingly discarded their lab-leak concerns and drafted "The Proximal Origin of SARS-CoV-2," which they sent to Fauci and Collins.[2] Dr. Jeremy Farrar, later Chief Scientist at the [[world-health-organization|World Health Organization]], was also heavily involved though not credited.[2] The Washington Examiner separately reported that two authors who initially voiced lab-leak concerns and then changed their stance — Andersen and Garry — received millions in NIH grants under Fauci.[2]
