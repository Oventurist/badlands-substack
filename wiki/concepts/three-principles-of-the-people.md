# Three Principles of the People

The *Three Principles of the People* (*San Min Chu I*) is a 1924 tract by [[sun-yat-sen|Sun Yat-sen]], compiled from a series of lectures that were transcribed into book form.[1] It is presented by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as the philosophical core of modern Chinese statecraft and as a direct adaptation of the moral outlook of [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]]'s America rather than of Marxism.[1]

## The tricolon and its Lincoln origin

In the [[gettysburg-address|Gettysburg Address]] of November 19, 1863, Lincoln memorialized the Union dead by exhorting that through their sacrifice "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."[1] This tricolon became the direct inspiration for Sun's 1924 tract.[1] Sun laid out three corresponding principles that China should adhere to: *minzu* ("national feelings of the people"), *minquan* ("rights of the people"), and *minsheng* ("the people's livelihood").[1]

In his first lecture on nationalism, Sun described the path to national rejuvenation in explicitly Lincolnian terms, writing that the Three Principles "mean government 'of the people, by the people, and for the people' – that is, a state belonging to all the people, a government controlled by all the people, and rights and benefits for the enjoyment of all the people," and identifying the fulfilment of the *minsheng* principle with Confucius' hope of a "great commonwealth."[1]

## Critique of Marx

The tract contains Sun's sharpest attack on [[karl-marx|Karl Marx]], whose theories were then rapidly permeating the young Chinese intelligentsia.[1] Sun argued that "society progresses through the adjustment of major economic interests rather than through the clash of interests," and that if most economic interests can be harmonized, the majority benefit and society progresses.[1] Studying forms of government from ancient Greece onward, he concluded that revolutionary transformations of production, distribution and social norms were never static or zero-sum but always centered on the "overthrowing of old systems and giving rise to new systems," adding that "it is the constant emergence of new systems that makes constant progress possible."[1]

From this he derived his best-known formulation: "class war is not the cause of social progress, it is a disease developed in the course of social progress," and therefore "Marx can only be called a social pathologist; we cannot say that he is a social physiologist."[1] Sun added that Marx "found only one of the diseases of society; he did not discover the law of social progress and the central force in history," citing an American scholar's view that the struggle for subsistence is that central force.[1]

## Livelihood, technology and the tariff

The last four lectures deal with improvement of livelihood through scientific and technological progress.[1] Sun stressed great public projects centered on hydroelectric power from the Yangtze River, industrial growth through the protective tariff, and advanced technology applied to rail, roads and agriculture, writing that "we must use the great power of the state and imitate the United States' methods."[1] Arguing for machine power to replace human labor, he claimed that "if China with human labor can support four hundred millions, she should with machine power produce enough for eight hundred millions."[1] Ehret reads this as a means of overcoming the "law of diminishing returns" advanced by [[john-stuart-mill|John Stuart Mill]], offsetting the tensions that generated Marx's class struggle.[1]

Taking aim at the systems promoted by the [[british-empire|British Empire]], whose [[opium-wars|Opium Wars]] remained a painful memory, Sun wrote that "capitalism makes profit its sole aim, while the Principle of Livelihood makes the nurture of the people its aim."[1] He believed adherence to the Confucian *minsheng* principle could balance large state power against the liberty of the people, producing "an all-powerful government seeking the welfare of the people."[1]

## Warning to a strong China

Sun closed with a warning Ehret considers more apt today than in 1924: if China, once strong, "wants to crush other countries, copy the Powers' imperialism, and go their road, we will just be following in their tracks," whereas only by resolving to "rescue the weak and lift up the fallen" would the nation prosper.[1]
