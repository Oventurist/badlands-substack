# ESSER Funds

The Elementary and Secondary School Emergency Relief (ESSER) program is a federal grant scheme created under the American Rescue Plan and distributed to US school districts by the [[department-of-education|US Department of Education]] over three phases beginning in 2021.[1] The $122 billion program was intended to boost learning recovery after the COVID-19 pandemic, partially rebuild the educator workforce, and help schools operate safely.[1]

## 2024 spending deadline

By September 2024, many school districts faced the prospect of returning millions of dollars to the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|US Treasury]] if they failed to spend their remaining relief money within thirty days, according to federal data.[1] Georgetown University's Edunomics Lab, which tracks ESSER spending, had previously reported that most of the money went to labor — additional teachers, counselors, classroom aides, tutors and reading coaches, subject-area specialists and administrators.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for Badlands, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the rush to spend as evidence that districts "got more money than they needed, but they really like money," choosing to "make it rain rather than return the money to US taxpayers."[1] The commentary argued that if the need had genuinely existed, the money would already have been spent, and that theoretical fiscal responsibility is the only kind available in 2024 America.[1]

The Milwaukee district was cited as an example of windfall spending: Chromebooks for students, upgraded athletic facilities, tutoring services, out-of-session learning camps, mental health services, afterschool clubs, free sports physicals and free driver's education classes — all funded as pandemic relief.[1] Ashe labelled the program a boondoggle, "an unnecessary or wasteful project or activity," and argued that in the age of public-private partnerships no other name applies, closing that Americans' shrinking affordability underwrites upgraded sports facilities at failing schools.[1]
