# Selling Out and the Payola System

"Selling out" — signing with a corporate label or otherwise trading artistic independence for commercial backing — was regarded within [[punk-rock]] and underground hip-hop as one of the gravest offenses an artist could commit.[1] Badlands Media argues the term has lost its force among neoliberal would-be punks who now rely on corporate media and favored talking heads to tell them how to feel, what to think, and who to be.[1]

## How radio promotion works

The article describes the mechanics behind commercial radio's repetitive playlists. Major record companies pay radio stations thousands of dollars to play their records, which is why independent music is rarely heard on commercial radio.[1] Labels route the money through middlemen known as independent radio promoters, or "indies," who pass a share on to the stations, which in turn play what the promoters request.[1] In exchange for paying a station an annual promotion budget — roughly $100,000 for a medium-size market — the indie becomes that station's exclusive promoter and is paid by the record companies each time the station adds a new song.[1] Badlands Media stresses that independent promoters are themselves part of the system by design, and that the independent music scene is not self-regulated.[1]

## Major-label concentration

Quoting Bomb Hip-Hop.com, the article states that big business controls which types of music and which artists receive exposure, that the record industry is a $14 billion business, and that five major labels — [[sony|Sony]], Universal, BMG, EMI and Time Warner — dominate 85% of the compact-disc sales market, leaving only 15% to hundreds of independent labels and thousands of artists.[1] When an independent label grows too large or an artist begins making noise, the majors typically acquire the artist or label, thereby controlling them, taking a percentage of sales, and minimizing competition.[1]

## Extension to news and other genres

Badlands Media asserts that this dynamic is not confined to music and describes it as "exactly how the news works as well."[1] The article offers hip-hop as a parallel case: a genre whose horizon initially seemed limitless but whose message was reset within a decade by labels, agents, and social trend strategists, shifting from community, faith, and romance toward violence, criminality, and excess — themes the article says feed the [[war-on-drugs|War on Drugs]] and the [[prison-industrial-complex|prison industrial complex]] money machine.[1] The publication notes that most acts are at their purest before corporate influence arrives, while allowing that better producers can genuinely benefit artists when they respect artistic vision.[1] The label EMI's contentious history with [[john-lydon|John Lydon]] is cited as an illustration of the tension.[1]
