# FinCEN Whistleblower Tip Portal

The **FinCEN whistleblower tip portal** is a website launched by the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|U.S. Department of the Treasury]] on February 13, 2026 to accept confidential tips on fraud, money laundering and sanctions violations, expanding federal efforts to combat financial crime.[1]

## Structure

Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] announced that the portal would be operated through the [[financial-crimes-enforcement-network|Financial Crimes Enforcement Network]]'s [[fincen-office-of-the-whistleblower|Office of the Whistleblower]], allowing individuals to report suspected misconduct involving violations of the [[bank-secrecy-act|Bank Secrecy Act]], U.S. sanctions programs and other financial laws.[1] People whose tips lead to successful enforcement actions may be eligible for financial awards, and Treasury has encouraged submitters to provide detailed documentation supporting their claims.[1] The stated aim is to strengthen enforcement tools and encourage reporting by combining a confidential channel with potential monetary incentives, at a moment of broader federal scrutiny of financial abuses and fraud schemes.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] wrote that he had years earlier proposed a bounty system rewarding whistleblowers and researchers who exposed corrupt politicians, but judged the Treasury program a superior design.[1] His objection to the earlier idea was that a political bounty had too many moving parts — unresolved questions about the funding source (donors, crowdfunding), and about the qualifying trigger for payment, whether an election loss or an arrest — leaving the metrics subjective and the incentive structure open to corruption and unintended consequences.[1]

By contrast, he argued, the FinCEN program streamlines the bounty concept by focusing on the one objective and traceable form of evidence: the money.[1] Invoking the "follow the money" refrain, he described forensic accounting as the ideal method for mapping criminal networks.[1] He characterized the program's funding mechanism — paying awards out of fines collected from guilty parties — as a reliable, self-sustaining source of reward money, and noted that it inherently targets financial institutions, which he identified as the core culprit in organized crime syndicates.[1] Summarizing the design, he wrote that the government would pay criminals to inform on their associates and pay bureaucrats and accountants to report misconduct they observe at work, with investigations expected to produce criminal referrals and indictments — concluding that Bessent was "weaponizing the financial system against itself by tapping into base human instincts."[1]
