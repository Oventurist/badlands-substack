# Politicised Dollar Liquidity

"Politicised dollar liquidity" refers to the concern, raised publicly in October 2025 as finance ministers and central bankers gathered in Washington DC for the [[imf|IMF]] and World Bank Annual Meetings, that the [[federal-reserve|US Federal Reserve]] — its independence under fire and subject to political pressure — might become less willing or less able to provide dollar liquidity to the international system during a crisis.[1] The question was described as pressing and no longer merely whispered in official circles, and prompted arguments that the IMF should look past narrower debates and revisit its original purpose of ensuring global monetary co-operation and financial stability.[1]

## The Argentina case

The [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s announcement of US financial support to [[argentina|Argentina]] was cited as an illustration of a related concern.[1] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and Treasury Secretary [[scott-bessent|Scott Bessent]] described the arrangement as a "swap," but with details scarce, analysts argued that what Treasury was likely to offer Argentina was not a swap at all but rather a loan from the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF) — the distinction being that central banks extend swap lines to one another, while finance ministries do not.[1]

## The institutional argument

The underlying case was summarized as follows: for decades the Fed's swap lines have underpinned confidence in the global system, and if they become hostage to presidential whims, countries cannot count on dollar liquidity when it matters most.[1] In that scenario the IMF would need to be ready to provide its next-best substitute, requiring resources through increased quotas to ensure that liquidity shocks do not spiral into solvency crises.[1] The credibility of the system, on this argument, rests not on assuming the Fed will always be there but on preparing complementary options before the next crisis arrives.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] noted that it is rare for a Financial Times article not to be paywalled, and read the piece as evidence of "substantial panic" — arguing that "the Regime's tools for financially manipulating countries to implement their agenda are failing."[1]
