# Money Wars

**Money Wars** is an ongoing essay series published at [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] by contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]], the first installment of which — subtitled "The Great Con" — appeared on September 29, 2023.[1] The series advances the thesis that a largely unrecognized war is being waged through the world's money and financial systems, and that ordinary people are combatants in it whether they realize it or not.[1] Its stated purpose is to help readers "relearn the truth about money and unlearn the lies we've been told."[1]

## Central thesis

The series opens from the premise that money dominates modern life: success is measured by counting zeros, relationships are planned around finance, wars are fought over it, and healthcare is issued or denied on its basis.[1] Deschamps notes that every American child is born already beholden to a share of the national debt — roughly $78,000 per person at the time of writing — and argues that bankers increasingly use money, finance, and the stock market to wield extraordinary power over humanity.[1] He observes that although laws against monopolies and undue influence exist, enforcement is selective: some, such as Donald Trump, are "hit with the book," while others receive a free pass.[1]

Money, in this framing, was once a tool for freedom and prosperity, but tools can be misused — especially, the author argues, when the young are no longer trained in how to use them ethically.[1]

## Weaponized money

The first installment presents two case studies of finance used as a coercive weapon.

The first is the [[freedom-convoy|Freedom Convoy]] — the 2022 Ottawa protests — during which banks in Canada and elsewhere unlawfully froze the accounts of protestors and organizers, according to Canadian lawyer and commentator [[viva-frei|Viva Frei]].[1] Deschamps writes that while the ultimate authors of the move cannot be confirmed, it undoubtedly benefited globalist interests, and that because the [[justin-trudeau|Trudeau]] government could not shut down lawful protest directly, it performed an end run around citizens' rights by starving protestors of money.[1] The crowdfunding platform [[gofundme|GoFundMe]] unilaterally confiscated over $10 million in trucker donations, intending to redirect the money to charities of its own choosing on the claim that the funds supported "white supremacy and hate."[1] Every actor involved — the Canadian government, GoFundMe, and the banks — is described as guilty of one of the highest crimes known to man: breach of trust and contract, violation of fiduciary duty, or complicity therein.[1]

The second case is the [[ken-paxton-impeachment|impeachment of Ken Paxton]] in mid-2023, in which the Texas Attorney General's salary was unilaterally suspended while charges were pending.[1] Deschamps argues that money itself was used to disable a man later acquitted of all charges from mounting a defense.[1]

From these examples the series generalizes: under the excuse of money, finance, and "the market," frauds, illegalities, and tyranny are imposed on free people everywhere.[1] Because such abuses occur through the ostensibly "free market," the public has wrongly concluded they are merely unfair rather than unlawful; Deschamps insists they are unfair, unlawful, and wrong at once.[1] He points to the disparity by which a millionaire can buy a fair trial while the average person cannot afford equal treatment under law, and to advanced healthcare that is withheld from those who cannot pay.[1] Socialism and communism, he suggests, became popular in part because people sense the underlying truth — that the world is a "debt slave plantation" in which money and finance are the shackles and whips of the technocratic age.[1]

## The Great Con framework

The core analytical device of Part 1 is the anatomy of a confidence trick, applied to the financial system as a whole.[1] Deschamps enumerates its elements: the **Con Artist**, who initiates and orchestrates the scam; the **Mark** or target, the deceived victim; the **Pitch**, the story used to gain the Mark's trust, typically a mixture of true statements that make the false ones go down easily; the **Hook**, the enticement of something wanted or needed; the **Play**, the manipulation of the Mark into an act that benefits the con artist; and the **Misdirection**, which hides the con artist's true intentions.[1] The financial system, he concludes, is from top to bottom a sophisticated Misdirection.[1]

A good hook is both believable and unverifiable, containing enough truth to obscure the greater truth of the con.[1] The con artist's aim is to make the Mark fixate on the carrot so completely that he avoids investigating the misdirection narrative at all.[1] Applied to banking, the argument is that the reliably functioning parts of the system distract from its corruption: if a customer with $1,000 pays a $60 bill through Bill Pay, the bank accurately shows the charge and the resulting $940 balance.[1] Bankers do accurate accounting and math, and that accuracy is critical to pulling off the Great Con.[1] Because debit and credit card charges, checks, wire transfers, and auto payments are generally above board, customers assume the rest of the system is too.[1] Deschamps draws an explicit parallel to medicine, where reliable triage for a broken leg or a cut instills a confidence that is then extended to things he regards as harmful, such as mRNA vaccines.[1]

## Loans, credit, and money creation

As a teaser for later installments, Part 1 addresses loans and credit.[1] Borrowers assume that when a bank approves a $100,000 mortgage the money already exists — in a vault or in the bank's [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] cash account — but it does not; the bank creates the money after approving the application.[1] Deschamps accepts the popular "money out of thin air" trope as mostly true while arguing something more insidious is occurring: the bank is acting as a currency exchange house, converting money the borrower himself created by filling out the loan application, which is itself a form of money.[1] The bank then charges exorbitant interest on money it never had, and on default may foreclose and confiscate real property as surety in compensation for funds it was never at risk of losing.[1]

The vocabulary of finance — hypothecation, surety bonds, debentures, annuities, demand deposit credit, negotiable instruments, accommodation — is described as an intentional smoke screen supporting the misdirection.[1]

## Questions posed for the series

Part 1 raises puzzles the series promises to resolve: why fiat money, though said to be worthless paper, works well enough in everyday life; why gold-backed money, praised as constitutional and immune to banker manipulation, nevertheless coincided with inflation as high as 17.8% between 1900 and 1933; and why the Federal Reserve, credited by mainstream media with stabilizing the money supply and maintaining price stability, has presided over a cumulative inflation rate of 3,001.27% since its 1913 enactment.[1] Deschamps distinguishes problems that persist from ignorance from those that persist because con artists use them as backdoors for nefarious ends.[1]

The series' outlook is nonetheless optimistic. Using the image of a single candle driving the darkness screaming from a pitch-black room, and the anecdote of a college friend whose years-filthy room was restored in a single weekend, Deschamps argues that corruption can be reversed quickly once the collective mess is acknowledged.[1] As the founding fathers did in the mid-18th century, rediscovering the truth about valid money would allow a rapid switch to better systems.[1] He concedes that the technical details may seem boring and dry — precisely what the powers that be rely on — and promises the next installment will unpack money, currency, and "one of the most powerful secrets of money."[1]
