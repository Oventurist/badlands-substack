# Bump Stock Ban

The **bump stock ban** was a federal regulation issued by the [[bureau-of-alcohol-tobacco-firearms-and-explosives|Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives]] reclassifying bump stocks — accessories for semiautomatic rifles that allow a shooter to rapidly reengage the trigger and achieve a high rate of fire — as "machineguns" under 26 U.S.C. §5845(b).[1] The rule was adopted under the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration and defended in litigation by the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration.[1]

## Origins

For many years ATF consistently held that semiautomatic rifles fitted with bump stocks were not machineguns.[1] The agency reversed that position after the [[las-vegas-shooting|Las Vegas shooting]], in which a gunman using bump-stock-equipped rifles killed 58 people and wounded more than 500.[1] ATF then proposed a rule at 83 Fed. Reg. 13442 amending its regulations to "clarify" that bump stocks are machineguns, and ordered owners to destroy or surrender them or face criminal prosecution.[1]

## Invalidation

The rule was struck down on June 14, 2024 in [[garland-v-cargill|Garland v. Cargill]], where the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] held 6–3 that ATF had exceeded its statutory authority, because semiautomatic firearms requiring trigger reengagement for each shot do not fire "automatically more than one shot . . . by a single function of the trigger."[1] Justice [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]] dissented, arguing the decision eviscerated Congress's regulation of machineguns.[1] Badlands Media characterized the ban as an unconstitutional gun law enacted in the emotional aftermath of a mass shooting.[1]
