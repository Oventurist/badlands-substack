# Real Estate Money Laundering

Real estate money laundering is, per the compliance firm Comply Advantage cited by Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]], one of the oldest known ways to move and hide illicit funds according to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development.[1] It is popular because large amounts can be washed in a single transaction; a Global Financial Integrity report found more than $2.3 billion laundered through U.S. real estate between 2015 and 2021.[1]

## Why property works

Laundering through real estate integrates illicit funds into the legitimate financial system while giving the criminal a relatively safe property investment — houses, apartments, office space, factories, hotels, vineyards and the like.[1] Prices are easy to manipulate: with collusion, property can be deliberately over- or undervalued, permitting either the washing of greater sums or the acquisition of valuable property far below worth.[1] Gatekeepers in the sector — realtors, developers, mortgage advisors and brokers — have sometimes been found complicit, accepting compensation to turn a blind eye.[1] Real estate is also less tightly regulated than banking.[1]

## Techniques and red flags

Common techniques include setting up shell or front companies to purchase property — in the United States anonymous shells can be formed in Delaware, Nevada, Wyoming and North Dakota — using cash or other non-transparent financing, selling properties to co-conspirators, and using opaque trusts or third parties as the legal owner.[1] Red flags include investors using multiple banks to stay under reporting thresholds, all-cash sales with no mortgage lender, a large disparity between the buyer's income and the property's value, and purchases where the ultimate beneficial owner is unclear.[1] In Miami and Manhattan, more than 60 percent of real estate transactions above $2 million made by international investors are cash transactions — a fact Lange treats as a huge red flag that is nonetheless tolerated, and as no coincidence given that Miami, [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]]'s home, became one of the biggest money laundering centers.[1]

## Relevance to the S&L scandal

Lange argues real estate is the key to understanding the real purpose behind [[deregulation|deregulation]] and the [[savings-and-loan-crisis|savings and loan scandal]]: property was a central part of the plan to fund the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] outside congressional and presidential oversight, and the nation's savings and loans became the vehicle for laundering the money.[1] He points to the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] as the expert at setting up shell companies and helping drug traffickers do the same, and asks whether [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] could have been unaware of the technique.[1] The [[land-flip|land flip]] was the crisis-era method of choice.[1]
