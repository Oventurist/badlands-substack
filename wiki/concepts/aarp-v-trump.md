# A.A.R.P. v. Trump

A.A.R.P. v. Trump is a 2025 federal case arising from emergency litigation over the Trump administration's deportation authority, notable chiefly for the extraordinary speed at which it moved through the courts and for the subsequent friction it generated between the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] and the [[us-court-of-appeals-for-the-fifth-circuit|Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals]].[1]

## Procedural history

The Fifth Circuit initially dismissed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction.[1] On a Friday in May 2025, the Supreme Court vacated that judgment and remanded the case to the Fifth Circuit with what commentators described as very precise instructions, directing the lower court to proceed "expeditiously."[1] The following Tuesday, the Fifth Circuit responded by assigning the matter to the next available randomly designated regular oral-argument panel for reargument, writing that the case was "expedited" in accordance with the Supreme Court's direction.[1]

## Judge Ho's concurrence

[[james-c-ho|Judge James C. Ho]] wrote a seven-page concurrence to the expediting order.[1] Ho accepted the obligation of an inferior court to follow Supreme Court rulings "whether we agree with them or not," but used the concurrence to defend his colleague, district judge Wes Hendrix, against what he characterized as an unfair attack from the Supreme Court, and to express concern about the treatment of the district judge, the President, and other officials in the case.[1] He warned that "the disrespect they have been shown will not inspire continued respect for the judiciary, without which we cannot long function," citing his own earlier concurrence in In re Westcott, 135 F.4th 243 (5th Cir. 2025).[1]

## Badlands commentary

The May 21, 2025 [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] carried the Volokh Conspiracy report of the order under commentary from [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who noted that although Ho had formally concurred, he was plainly unhappy with the outcome.[1] Ashe described the case as the one in which the district court had been given roughly forty-two minutes to rule before the [[american-civil-liberties-union|ACLU]] escalated to the Supreme Court, which in her framing "inexplicably sided with the lawfare."[1] She summarized the concurrence as confirmation of a recurring Badlands thesis: that "the courts are only legitimate so long as we believe they are legitimate."[1]
