# Hunter Biden Pardon

The Hunter Biden pardon was the grant of clemency issued by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] on the evening of Sunday, December 1, 2024, covering his son [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]. Described in the pardon document as "full and unconditional," it insulated Hunter Biden from any federal charge arising from conduct over roughly the previous decade.[1]

## Scope and timing

The pardon spanned a period beginning shortly before Hunter Biden joined the board of the Ukrainian energy company [[burisma|Burisma]] — a role reported to pay about $1 million per year — and running through the evening the clemency was announced.[1] Reporting cited in the Brief indicated that the pardon had been in preparation for months, contradicting repeated prior denials by the White House that any such action was contemplated.[1]

Legal specialists quoted in coverage of the action said they could recall only one comparably sweeping grant in generations: the blanket pardon of [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]] by Gerald Ford in 1974.[1] Margaret Love, who served as U.S. pardon attorney from 1990 to 1997, said she had "never seen language like this in a pardon document that purports to pardon offenses that have not apparently even been charged, with the exception of the Nixon pardon," adding that "even the broadest Trump pardons were specific as to what was being pardoned."[1]

## Political reaction

The pardon produced what the Brief characterized as chaos on the political left, with Democrats and never-Trump conservatives alike arguing that it set a precedent enabling [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to pardon anyone he wished, while others defended it as necessary protection against an anticipated retributive prosecution by the incoming administration.[1] Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the pardon, delivered in full public view, damaged the establishment's "rule of law" narrative and represented an accelerant to a broader anti-corruption reckoning rather than a setback.[1]

## Litigation aftermath

Special counsel [[david-weiss|David Weiss]] objected in court filings to dismissing Hunter Biden's California tax case outright following the pardon, rejecting claims of vindictive or selective prosecution: "There was none and never has been any evidence of vindictive or selective prosecution in this case."[1] Weiss argued the charges should not be dismissed with prejudice but that proceedings should simply be closed with the pardon recorded as the final disposition — a largely procedural distinction — and maintained that the pardon neither absolved guilt nor identified any defect in the indictment.[1] He also noted that the government had not yet seen the official pardon document, writing that an "act of mercy" does not mean "the grand jury's decision to charge him, based on a finding of probable cause, should be wiped away as if it never occurred."[1] Hunter Biden made a parallel dismissal request in Delaware, where he had been convicted on federal gun charges earlier in 2024.[1]

## Judicial Watch FOIA lawsuit

[[judicial-watch|Judicial Watch]] filed a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit against the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] seeking records related to the pardon after the Department failed to comply with a December 2, 2024 FOIA request.[2] The suit demands all communications from the Offices of the Attorney General, Deputy Attorney General, and Associate Attorney General concerning the clemency grant, naming then–Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]], Deputy Attorney General [[lisa-monaco|Lisa Monaco]], and Associate Attorney General Benjamin Mizer.[2]

The pardon, issued December 1, 2024 during [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s final weeks in office, shielded [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]] from any federal charges "committed or taken part in" between January 1, 2014 and December 1, 2024 — a window encompassing his tenure on the board of the Ukrainian energy firm [[burisma|Burisma Holdings]].[2] It was the only one among more than 1,500 pardons issued in Biden's final days that the president signed personally rather than processing via autopen.[2]

## A new pardon playbook

Reporting by [[politico|Politico]] on December 3, 2024 argued that in pardoning his son, Joe Biden did not just protect Hunter but handed President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] a template to shield his own allies and stretch the pardon power even further.[3] Legal experts said Trump now had fresh precedent — and political cover — to issue expansive pardons absolving his allies not only of specific offenses but of any undetermined crimes they may have committed.[3]

With the singular exception of Gerald Ford's pardon of Richard Nixon, no modern American president had ever issued such a broad grant of clemency until Biden's "full and unconditional" pardon of his son on Sunday night, which effectively cleared the younger Biden of legal consequences for any federal law he might have broken over a nearly 11-year period.[3] The terms were so unusual — and the process leading to the pardon so secretive — that the Justice Department's Office of the Pardon Attorney, which typically advises the president on clemency issues, was taken by surprise, according to a person granted anonymity to disclose the details.[3]

In the final days of Trump's first term at least one close ally, former Representative [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]], requested a similarly sweeping pardon according to congressional testimony, but top White House aides made clear it was a nonstarter.[3] Legal experts and former Trump associates said that now that Biden had "crossed the Rubicon," it would be harder to restrain Trump next time, giving him a readymade rationale to follow suit on returning to office.[3]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] observed that in the immediate aftermath the media seemed split between defending and condemning the regime, but that battle lines had since been drawn and key narrative-setters within the establishment were "throwing the Bidens out with the bath water" — closing ranks not around the First Family but around the system that family was upending on its way out the door.[3] He noted that The Atlantic called the pardon a "strategic mistake" while Politico used language like "parting insult" and wrote that "the president delivered a vote of no confidence in a justice system preparing for siege," arguing the real signal lay just beneath the surface, close to but not quite [[narrative-shielding|narrative shielding]] terminology.[3]

## Motion to dismiss the indictment

The pardon came ahead of Hunter Biden's December 12 sentencing on his federal gun conviction and a separate December 16 sentencing in the case in which he pleaded guilty to federal tax evasion charges, and was expected to cover both matters.[4] ZeroHedge noted that the "full and unconditional" grant covered offenses "which he has committed or may have committed or taken part in" across a nearly 11-year period from January 1, 2014 through December 1, 2024 — a window that includes his joining the board of [[burisma|Burisma Holdings]], the subject [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was impeached for asking about.[4] Hunter Biden's lawyer promptly moved to dismiss his indictment on the basis of the pardon.[4]

## "Our Boy Blue" reading

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the pardon through the outlet's "Our Boy Blue" refrain — a half-joking analogue to "patriots in control" theories that he argued had mapped increasingly onto reality.[4] He offered three ways the pardon helps Trump and his allies: it grants [[narrative-shielding|narrative shielding]] to [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] and the incoming [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] to pursue investigations without effective cries of weaponization; it forces Hunter Biden to testify if called in future investigations touching the Biden family, Ukraine or Burisma, since he can no longer invoke the Fifth Amendment for conduct he cannot be charged with; and it advances the exposure of establishment corruption as, in his phrasing, "the Deep State ouroboros continues to turn in on itself in full view of an awakening American Mind."[4] Whether the Bidens were still operating as swamp creatures or "dancing to the tune of patriot pipers," he concluded, the result was the same: "exposure, disclosure … justice."[4]


## White House briefing response

The White House Press Secretary, [[karine-jean-pierre|Karine Jean-Pierre]], held a briefing the Friday after the pardon, her first since President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] granted clemency to his son, and faced questions about the reversal given her repeated prior assurances that no pardon would be issued.[5] She defended the decision by saying that "circumstances have changed, they have," blaming Republicans who she said were "not going to let up" as [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s sentencing in his gun case approached, and stating that the president felt Hunter and his family had been through enough.[5] She characterized the outcome as the product of the president wrestling with changing circumstances before changing his mind and issuing the pardon.[5]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] used the exchange to question the authorship of the accompanying presidential statement, arguing that referring publicly to a message as being "in his own voice" is communications jargon for a tightly controlled channel and that no professional should undermine that channel by naming it.[5] The commentary suggested Jean-Pierre had internally advocated for the president to speak in his own voice to project strength and authenticity while preserving her own ability to deflect questions, and speculated pointedly about who was actually writing in Biden's voice.[5]
