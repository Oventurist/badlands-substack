# 2024 Federal Race and Ethnicity Standards Revision

The 2024 federal race and ethnicity standards revision was a change, announced on March 28, 2024 by the [[office-of-management-and-budget|Office of Management and Budget]], to how the United States government categorizes people by race and ethnicity — the first such revision in 27 years.[1]

## Substance of the change

Federal officials said the revision would more accurately count residents who identify as Hispanic and of Middle Eastern and North African heritage.[1] Under the revisions, questions about race and ethnicity that had previously been asked separately on federal forms were combined into a single question, allowing respondents to pick multiple categories at once, such as "Black," "American Indian" and "Hispanic."[1] Research had shown that large numbers of Hispanic people were unsure how to answer the race question when asked separately, because they understand race and ethnicity to be similar, and often selected "some other race" or skipped the question entirely.[1]

The changes also struck the terms "majority" and "minority," which some officials said fail to reflect the nation's complex racial and ethnic diversity.[1]

## Reaction

Meeta Anand, senior director for Census & Data Equity at The Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights, said, "You can't underestimate the emotional impact this has on people. It's how we conceive ourselves as a society. ... You are seeing a desire for people to want to self-identify and be reflected in data so they can tell their own stories."[1] Allison Plyer, chief demographer of The Data Center in New Orleans, argued that the ability "to disaggregate can really be helpful to distinguish different kinds of discrimination, the ability to enforce laws around discrimination and do research on public health and economic outcomes."[1]

## Badlands analysis

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] treated the revision as the "change management" complement to media narrative control, describing it as redefining the underlying infrastructure — the people, their beliefs, and the rules, norms, and metrics associated with desired change outcomes.[1] Quoting George Carlin's line that "the quality of our thoughts and ideas can only be as good as the quality of our language," she argued the standards support societal segmentation and create new grievance categories, a purpose she said officials effectively admitted in describing the value of disaggregated data.[1] She also questioned the legal consequences of deleting the term "minority," noting that Google Scholar returns ten pages of cases referencing "minority" litigators and their specific rights, and asked whether the Office of Management and Budget had "just opened a massive can of worms."[1]
