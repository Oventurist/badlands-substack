# 2024 United States General Election

The 2024 United States general election was held on Tuesday, November 5, 2024, and was framed by Badlands Media as the culmination of years of activity by the "truth community" — a "Day of Days" comparable to the D-Day landings at Normandy.[1] Badlands Media itself ran continuous special coverage of the election on Rumble, beginning at 1 p.m. Eastern and running "straight through to the wee hours of the morning," with hosts, guests and audience participation.[1]

## Security posture and unrest fears

Ahead of the vote, [[national-guard|National Guard]] troops were placed on standby in Washington state, Oregon and Nevada as a precaution against potential election-related civil unrest.[1] The [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] warned that threats to "election infrastructure" remained high.[1] Reporting cited by Badlands noted that Oregon had deployed the Guard together with a unified command for election security in 2020, and that states had increasingly activated the Guard for election cybersecurity duties.[1]

## Media and narrative dimension

[[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] and [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] appeared back-to-back-to-back on the Joe Rogan Experience, the largest podcast in the world, on the eve of the election.[1] Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that these appearances were less about turning out the MAGA base than about "prepping the mass psychological terrain" for an expected cascade of information operations that would either defend or reject the popular result, noting Trump's insistence that he needed "a mandate in the vote" for what came after the election rather than for the election itself.[1]

Few operators at the "anon" layers of the information war trusted U.S. election systems, a distrust Badlands treated as healthy on the grounds that Americans needed to reckon with unreliable machinery for recording their sovereign mandate.[1]

## Foreign influence warnings

On the Monday before the vote, the [[office-of-the-director-of-national-intelligence|Office of the Director of National Intelligence]], the [[fbi|Federal Bureau of Investigation]] and the [[cybersecurity-and-infrastructure-security-agency|Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency]] issued a joint statement identifying Russia as the "most active threat" to public confidence in the election, describing manufactured videos and fake articles intended to delegitimize the process, frighten voters and suggest Americans were committing political violence against one another.[1] The statement predicted such activity would intensify through Election Day and focus on swing states.[1]

## Related disputes

Litigation over voter eligibility ran up to the eve of the vote, including the [[iowa-noncitizen-voter-challenge-ruling|Iowa noncitizen voter challenge ruling]].[1] Badlands also argued that the October economic and migration data — in which parole and illegal border entries far exceeded new job creation — was information voters should have had before casting early ballots, and used the episode to argue for a single Election Day so that "every American should be voting with the same available information."[1]

## Election night and results

Voting took place on Tuesday, November 5, 2024. [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] appeared to win decisively on election night, though several media organizations declined to call the race for him by sunrise, and Michigan, Wisconsin, Arizona and Nevada remained uncalled while the New York Times rated all four as likely Trump states.[2] Badlands commentary described the outcome as a landslide including a popular-vote win, and argued that the slow-walking of results and localized irregularities may have been intended to minimize Trump's mandate rather than to change the winner.[2] Trump delivered a victory speech at Mar-a-Lago in the early hours.[2]

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] streamed more than 15 hours of live special coverage through the night.[2] On that broadcast [[patrick-byrne|Patrick Byrne]] described the difference from 2020 in terms of "defenses in depth" — layered protections that made cheating too costly — and called for outright reform of US election systems rather than continued reliance on such defenses.[2]

## AP VoteCast exit polling

The Associated Press VoteCast survey of more than 110,000 voters found that 40 percent named the economy and jobs the most important problem facing the country, roughly 20 percent named immigration, and about 10 percent named abortion.[2] About 80 percent of voters said they wanted at least "substantial change" in how the country is run, and roughly 25 percent wanted a "complete and total upheaval."[2] Just under six in ten respondents said they were at least somewhat concerned a second Trump administration would move the US closer to authoritarianism, with just under half saying the same of [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]].[2] In swing states, top voter issues stayed within five points of the national average and abortion never exceeded 15 percent.[2]

A New York Times/Siena College poll released on October 25 had found 52 percent of likely voters trusted Trump to lead the economy against 45 percent for Harris.[2] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the exit polling as confirmation of an "America First" mandate and noted that pollsters had been badly wrong in three consecutive presidential elections.[2]

## Election Day disruptions

Officials in Maine and Georgia responded to threats against schools and polling places, none of which were found credible.[2] In [[fulton-county|Fulton County]], Georgia, five noncredible bomb threats briefly closed two polling locations in Union City, and Secretary of State [[brad-raffensperger|Brad Raffensperger]] said the source had been identified as Russia, while the FBI reported similar threats in several states appearing to originate from Russian email domains.[2]

Separately, a [[jerusalem-post|Jerusalem Post]] investigation reported a potentially illegal [[ballot-harvesting]] operation run by [[ivoteisrael|iVoteIsrael]] in central Israel ahead of the vote.[2]

## Related events

On the same day, Israeli Prime Minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] fired Defense Minister [[yoav-gallant|Yoav Gallant]], and the US military scheduled a test launch of an unarmed [[minuteman-iii|Minuteman III]] ICBM from [[vandenberg-space-force-base|Vandenberg Space Force Base]] for the hours after polls closed.[2]

## Pre-election irregularities and the mandate question

In the final week before the vote, the director of the [[cisa|Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency]] said Russian, Chinese and Iranian operators were attempting to disrupt the election with disinformation.[3] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] contrasted that framing with the Colorado voting-system password exposure under Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]], in which BIOS passwords for more than 600 machines were publicly accessible for months and the breach was concealed for days, yet officials declared no threat existed.[3]

Reported irregularities accumulated across the country in the same period, including stolen ballots, leaked passwords, machine glitches, "known issues," unscannable ballots and ballot-marking-device misprints.[3] Among the most widely circulated was a Laurel County, Kentucky incident in which a voter's selection of Trump registered as Harris; county clerk [[tony-brown|Tony Brown]] confirmed on Facebook that the issue "did occur" after first denying it, and the machine was withdrawn from service pending review by the attorney general's office.[3]

Politico reported that Trump was making Pennsylvania the focal point of preemptive claims of a rigged election, citing Bucks County mail-ballot lines and early cutoffs — over which Trump allies successfully sued to extend on-demand voting — as well as Lancaster and York County reviews of suspicious voter registration materials, which Trump characterized as "THOUSANDS of potentially FRAUDULENT applications."[3] Trump posted that "Pennsylvania is cheating, and getting caught, at large scale levels rarely seen before" and demanded prosecution.[3]

Badlands commentary described the environment as "the thick of the fog of war," with pollsters urging belief in close races and Politico floating a Harris electoral-college win without the popular vote.[3] Ashe in America wrote that she would not "trust the outcome" of an unverifiable election, while [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the true measure of the election was not reported vote totals but whether the public accepted them — a question of mandate rather than count.[3]

## Migration ahead of a possible Trump return

The Telegraph reported that roughly 2,000 migrants were travelling north through Mexico by foot and convoy in an attempt to reach the United States before a possible Trump return to the White House, having covered 90 miles in five days from Tapachula on the Guatemalan border with about 1,000 miles remaining.[3] Organizers described the march as "their exodus," and a second convoy was planned to depart on 5 November, deliberately timed for Election Day so that [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] "know we are heading their way."[3] The report noted Trump's demand for the death penalty for migrants who kill US citizens and his mass-deportation platform, alongside an existing arrangement between the Biden administration and the Mexican government that had already made the journey far harder.[3]

## Keshel's post-election "casino" analysis

In April 2025, election analyst [[seth-keshel|Seth Keshel]] argued that the 2024 result should be understood probabilistically: if the election were run ten times with slightly different variables, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] would almost certainly not win every time.[4] He observed that [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] trailed [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s 2020 vote count in 44 states but exceeded it in six — Utah, Maine, North Carolina, Wisconsin, Nevada and Georgia — four of which were critical battlegrounds, while lagging badly in California, New York, Florida and Texas, which drove down her national total.[4] Removing Georgia, Nevada, Wisconsin and North Carolina from Trump's column would have left him at 264 electoral votes, short of the 270 needed.[4]

Keshel contended that the system was "concentrated on delivering winning totals in the battleground states only," rather than in safe red or blue states, and credited [[automatic-voter-registration|Automatic Voter Registration]] not yet having fully taken hold in Pennsylvania for Trump's survival there.[4] He noted that AVR states went 18-2 for Biden in 2020 and that Harris won only five electoral votes in 2024 from non-AVR jurisdictions.[4] He read pre-election media messaging that Harris would lose the popular vote but win the [[electoral-college|Electoral College]] as a trailed plan that, had it succeeded, would also have pushed conservatives to demand a national popular vote.[4]

Keshel further argued that where the top of the ticket could not be stopped, down-ballot impacts were minimized, pointing to Senate races in Wisconsin, Nevada, Michigan and Arizona and to U.S. House contests in California where two Democrats declared victory in December, as well as a Maine House district Trump carried for a single electoral vote.[4] He noted that Trump overperformed with minority voters — predominantly Black in Georgia, Wisconsin and North Carolina, and largely Hispanic, Asian and Filipino in Nevada.[4]

## Pre-election commentary on the aftermath (October 2024)

Two weeks before the vote, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] published "The Aftermath of a Trump Victory," arguing that the significant consequence of a [[donald-trump|Trump]] win would be psychological rather than administrative.[5] He predicted that supporters of the losing side would pass through the [[five-stages-of-grief|five stages of grief]] — denying the result and insisting the election had to have been stolen despite four years of assurances that US elections are safe and secure, then anger, bargaining, depression and eventual acceptance as the predicted catastrophes failed to materialise.[5] He also forecast possible rioting and sabotage orchestrated by elites being forced from power, alongside a prosperous economy, while warning that gas prices held down and fabricated job numbers meant the true state of the economy would only surface once the [[deep-state|Deep State]] had no reason to keep manipulating the figures.[5] Carlson framed the whole event as the trigger for a [[mass-awakening|mass awakening]] with real casualties.[5]

## "Three Possible Election Outcomes" (October 2024)

In an October 10, 2024 essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] narrowed the realistic outcomes of the election to three scenarios: a [[donald-trump|Trump]] victory in which his support overcomes any election interference and he is announced the winner on or around Election Day; a rigged victory for [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] that is steamrolled through because her administration already holds power, with opponents of the result locked up as he says happened in 2020; and a rigged Harris victory that is later reversed because a trap had been set and the interference is exposed, with Trump declared the winner days, weeks or months after Election Day.[6] Carlson stated he saw no way Harris could win a legitimate election and considered a Trump declaration — whether on Election Day or later — the more likely result.[6]

He allowed that the [[deep-state|Deep State]] might refuse to certify a Trump win, but judged that this would damage them further and would be difficult to justify after four years of insisting American elections are secure and ridiculing those who questioned results.[6] Carlson urged readers not to expect an election-night victory and smooth transition, warning that the contest might function as "a giant sting operation" whose resolution could extend into the following spring, and that supporters must avoid demoralisation or rash action if Harris were declared the winner before being overturned.[6] He judged that an early Trump declaration could produce some rioting and violence, but that more would follow if the election were overturned later.[6] He also flagged the timing of the [[2024-east-and-gulf-coast-dockworker-strike|East and Gulf Coast port strike]], the North Carolina hurricane flooding and the administration's response as factors he expected to weigh on the Harris campaign.[6]

## Post-election analysis: why the concession came fast

Writing on November 21, 2024, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] noted that expectations within the MAGA and truther community had ranged from an Election Night landslide and concession by [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]], to a contest dragging on for weeks, to an outright steal installing Harris as the 47th president — and that even those who expected a landslide felt the [[deep-state|Deep State]] had given up too easily.[7] Within hours of [[donald-trump|Trump]] being declared the winner, he wrote, people were asking whether the apparatus had abandoned the presidential race and shifted to stealing down-ballot Senate and House seats instead, or whether the presidential margin had simply been too big to rig.[7]

Carlson's own conclusion was that the Deep State's polling showed it could not persuade the public that Harris was more popular, and that a steal would have destroyed confidence in the election system itself; the best available option was therefore to make the result appear close, at roughly 50 percent to 48–49 percent, suggesting an evenly split country rather than what he assumed was closer to a 60/40 result — some 90 million votes to 60 million out of about 150 million cast.[7] He further alleged that fake ballots were being submitted after Election Day not to overtake Trump in the Electoral College but to raise the aggregate vote total, reducing the gap with 2020 from roughly 20 million votes in the days after the election to about 5 million weeks later, and thereby making the [[2020-election-fraud-claims|2020 totals]] less glaring.[7] He warned supporters against complacency and argued that Trump's term was the only realistic window for comprehensive election reform before 2026 and 2028.[7]
