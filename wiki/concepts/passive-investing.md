# Passive Investing

Passive investing is an asset-management strategy in which client money is invested in indexes of stocks rather than through stock-by-stock selection.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] identifies it as the mechanism by which [[blackrock|BlackRock, Inc.]] and [[vanguard-group|the Vanguard Group]] came to sit atop the institutional holder lists of nearly every large public company.[1]

## Scale and clientele

BlackRock manages almost $10 trillion in assets, the vast majority of it invested passively, on behalf of larger-than-life institutions as well as pension funds.[1] The article observes that just about every American reader either has money, or knows someone with money, directly or indirectly in a BlackRock account.[1]

## Power despite passivity

DeLarme argues that great effort has been expended downplaying the influence that passive management confers, and that the downplaying is unwarranted.[1] Although BlackRock may not be the ultimate owner of the shares it manages, it can and does vote those shares on behalf of its clients under its proxy voting policy, and that voting power enables it to exert extraordinary influence over corporate management and policy at behemoths such as Exxon and Microsoft.[1]

Two illustrations are given. In the third quarter of 2021 BlackRock opposed the re-election of 800 separate company directors.[1] The article also states that [[exxonmobil|Exxon]] dropped several drilling projects after BlackRock removed board members and installed new ones, despite the world being objectively underinvested in energy production — leading DeLarme to conclude that higher gasoline prices are in part attributable to BlackRock.[1]

## Structural consequences

Because passive managers necessarily hold every major firm in an index, passive investing produces cross-holdings across all leading competitors in an industry, the condition known as [[horizontal-shareholding|horizontal shareholding]].[1] DeLarme treats this as the principal channel by which passive investing degrades competition, arguing that behavior such as forcing board turnover at energy companies makes no financial or logistical sense absent an agenda.[1]
