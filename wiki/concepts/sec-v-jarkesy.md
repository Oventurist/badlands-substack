# SEC v. Jarkesy

**SEC v. Jarkesy** is a decision of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] handed down at the close of the Court's October 2023 term, in which the Court held that defendants accused of fraud by the Securities and Exchange Commission have the right to a jury trial rather than being adjudicated solely within the agency's in-house administrative tribunals.[1]

## Significance in Badlands coverage

Badlands Media's July 2, 2024 news brief treated *Jarkesy* as one of the most consequential rulings of a term dominated in the headlines by the presidential-immunity case, arguing that presidential immunity "wasn't even at the top of the list this week" in terms of structural importance.[1] Contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the ruling as a blow to the administrative state, noting with evident satisfaction that critics were already lamenting that regulators would be able to bring fewer enforcement cases for fear of exhausting their budgets once jury trials became the norm.[1] The brief observed that jury trials for SEC fraud defendants had not previously been standard practice, a fact it presented as itself an indictment of the administrative adjudication system.[1]

## Relationship to Chevron

The brief paired *Jarkesy* with the Court's contemporaneous decision overturning the [[chevron-deference|Chevron deference]] doctrine, describing the two rulings together as shifting the benefit of the doubt away from federal agencies and toward the governed.[1] Both decisions were credited in part to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s Supreme Court appointments, which the brief said had been "packing quite a punch."[1]

## The decision and its scope

The ruling was issued on Thursday, June 27, 2024, and rested on the Seventh Amendment: defendants are entitled to a jury trial where the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|SEC]] seeks civil penalties for securities fraud.[2] The case arose because [[george-jarkesy|George Jarkesy]] ran two hedge funds the SEC alleged were fraudulent, prompting the agency to bring its case before an administrative law judge rather than in federal court.[2]

Because roughly 2,000 administrative law judges work across the federal government and report to executive-branch agencies rather than to the judiciary, the decision hobbles not only the SEC but also bodies such as the [[environmental-protection-agency|EPA]], the Labor Department and OSHA in their ability to enforce statutes through in-house adjudication.[2] Reporting at the time placed the case among a handful of decisions that term chipping away at the power of the [[administrative-state|administrative state]], with the pending question of judicial deference to agency decisions described as the most important of the group.[2]

## Contemporaneous Badlands reaction

In the June 28, 2024 Brief, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] listed *Jarkesy* first among the four rulings released that day, summarizing it as a vindication of the Bill of Rights.[2] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] devoted his take to reproducing the objections of Slate writer [[mark-joseph-stern|Mark Joseph Stern]], presenting the mainstream legal commentariat's alarm as inadvertent confirmation that the neutering of the administrative state was well underway.[2]

## Case posture ahead of the 2023 term

The Supreme Court agreed on June 30, 2023 to hear Securities and Exchange Commission v. Jarkesy (court file 22-859) during the term beginning the following October.[3] George Jarkesy and his advisory firm, Patriot28, had been sued in federal court in the District of Columbia in 2013 for infringing securities laws in managing two hedge funds; Jarkesy countersued, arguing that the structure of the commission ran afoul of the U.S. Constitution.[3] The lower court found for the SEC, which continued the proceedings and assigned the case to an administrative law judge who confirmed the violations, after which the commission reviewed and ratified the decision and ordered the parties to pay $300,000 in civil penalties.[3]

A divided U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit held that the two levels of removal protections applying to administrative law judges unconstitutionally shielded them from presidential oversight, that SEC proceedings infringed the Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial, and that Congress had unconstitutionally delegated its lawmaking powers to the commission.[3] Lawyers who frequently appear before the Court — including former solicitor general Paul Clement and Williams and Connolly partner Lisa Blatt, speaking at a September 20, 2023 Heritage Foundation event — suggested the justices might use the term to roll back the administrative state.[3]
