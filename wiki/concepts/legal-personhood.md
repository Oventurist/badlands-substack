# Legal Personhood

Legal personhood is the status that makes an entity a recognized actor within a system of law. Badlands Media's analysis of the American monetary system treats "person" not as a synonym for human being but as a *role* — a position within a rule system that carries defined rights and duties.[1]

## Definition used

The article cites Black's Law Dictionary, which defines a person as "a man considered according to the rank he holds in society, with all the rights to which the place he holds entitles him, and the duties which it imposes."[1] For ease of comprehension the author recommends thinking of personhood as essentially the same thing as a role one adopts from time to time.[1]

## Classes of person

From its grocery-store illustration the article derives four classes of person: the manager, the customer, the employee and the foreigner.[1] One can toggle between the foreigner role and the customer role simply by stepping inside or outside the store, and the applicable rules change accordingly.[1] A person who is foreign to an organization's internal policy is not subject to it at all.[1] See [[jurisdiction]].

## Corporations as persons

Because a corporation possesses legal personhood, it can itself act as an agent for another party.[1] The article uses this to argue that a congressional representative is one of the citizen's agents, and that every authorization given to another individual or group to act on one's behalf creates an agency relationship.[1] From this follows the article's central claim that the federal government, acting as agent, became the customer of [[federal-reserve|the Federal Reserve]] on the public's behalf, binding the United States to pay for the benefit of using Federal Reserve Notes.[1] See [[agency-law]] and [[federal-reserve-note]].
