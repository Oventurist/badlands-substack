# Global Energy War

The "global energy war" is the framing Badlands writers applied in March 2026 to the deliberate weaponization of energy supply and infrastructure during the United States–[[iran|Iran]] conflict, in which energy serves as both the primary arena of the struggle and its ultimate prize.[1]

## Military and maritime dimension

U.S. strikes hit Iranian military targets on [[kharg-island|Kharg Island]], the terminal handling about 90 percent of Iran's oil exports, with [[donald-trump|President Trump]] saying oil infrastructure was spared but broader strikes could follow if Iran interfered with shipping in the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]].[1] Iran threatened U.S.-linked energy infrastructure in Gulf states, and maritime traffic through the strait — roughly one-fifth of world oil supply — was disrupted.[1]

Trump told the Financial Times that [[nato|NATO]] faced a "very bad" future if allies did not help reopen the strait, calling on Gulf-oil-dependent countries including China, the United Kingdom, France, Japan and South Korea to contribute minesweepers and air defenses.[1] [[kevin-hassett|Kevin Hassett]] put the war's cost at roughly $12 billion so far and argued the United States, unlike in the 1970s, now produces enough oil to be insulated.[1]

## The narrative reading

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that a macro-narrative centered on energy is rising to subsume the war story emanating from Tehran, and that current disruptions — actual or merely perceptual — are accelerating the germination of a long-seeded energy renaissance.[1] While mainstream attention fixed on regime change and regional escalation, the deeper current was the deliberate targeting of production facilities, retaliation against Gulf assets, temporary shut-ins and the contraction of energy trade outside strategic exceptions.[1] The weaponization was real, he wrote, but so was the selectivity of its impact.[1]

Europe, in this analysis, is the most exposed party: dangerously dependent on imported energy after years of pipeline disruptions and an ideological campaign against its own nuclear capacity that some European capitals now concede was a catastrophic strategic error.[1] By contrast the emerging [[multipolar-world|multipolar]] alignment behaves differently — [[russia|Russia]] directing oil to its largest customer under negotiated temporary arrangements, India responding to external pressure with sovereign bluntness, and Chinese commercial vessels moving through contested waters with minimal disruption — reflecting coordination on the material plane even as narrative sparring continues on the surface.[1]

## The pincer on Europe

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read Trump's NATO ultimatum as a narrative pincer.[1] With the Iran war threatening maritime LNG shipments and Vladimir Putin suggesting Russia could stop supplying gas to European markets, the United States would be left as Europe's only LNG supplier — at severe cost to consumers.[1] Europe is thereby forced to choose between sending ships to the Strait of Hormuz, risking entanglement with Iran and the loss of Persian Gulf LNG, or refusing the demands of its sole reliable energy supplier.[1] The only remaining play, he argued, is negotiation — with Putin, Trump or Iran, all three of whom would make the same baseline demand: end support for Ukraine and make permanent peace with Russia.[1] Since America, Russia and Iran are all energy independent, he called the position "Checkmate."[1]

## Hormuz phase and Sovereign Disentanglement (March 2026)

By late March 2026 the energy war centered on the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], where [[iran|Iran]] required transiting vessels to submit cargo and crew details and in some cases pay transit fees, with at least two payments reportedly made in Chinese yuan.[2] Traffic through the strait fell by roughly 90% after the conflict began, lifting oil prices and disrupting supply, particularly in Asia.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the Energy War "isn't about Iran, but about what it represents," and that it is a war already won where it matters most.[2] He described the visible theater of missiles, chokepoints and escalation headlines as a shield over a decisive contest in energy markets, supply chains and the Collective Mind, forming the basis for a Sovereign Disentanglement operation enacted by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] against the Globalist Hegemon.[2] In his Reverse Hegelian Dialectic framing, energy shocks are the problem, American economic pressure translated into a public mandate for independence is the reaction, and a tri-polar energy renaissance rendering the scarcity cult obsolete is the synthesis.[2] By foregrounding escalation while shifting supply lines and exposing Europe's reliance on foreign energy, he argued, Trump turned the manipulation of energy corridors into the mechanism of the Globalists' exposure — a form of Narrative Disarmament.[2]
