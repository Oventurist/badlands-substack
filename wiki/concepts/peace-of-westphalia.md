# Peace of Westphalia

The Peace of Westphalia of 1648 was the settlement that ended the Thirty Years' War. In the Badlands Media article [[a-gateway-to-a-new-world-of-cooperation|A Gateway to a New World of Cooperation]], [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] cites it as the first of the great historical peace conferences and states that it "established the framework of the modern sovereign nation state system at the heart of international law."

## Function in the article's argument

Westphalia opens a sequence of precedents the article assembles — followed by the [[congress-of-vienna|Congress of Vienna]], the [[league-of-nations|League of Nations]], and the [[united-nations|United Nations]] with the [[bretton-woods-system|Bretton Woods system]] — illustrating the author's thesis that in pre-nuclear epochs peace conferences were arranged only after years of devastating war had become unbearable. Ehret argues that the sovereignty principle first codified at Westphalia is precisely what the [[tianjin-declaration|Tianjin Declaration]] reaffirms and what the [[rules-based-international-order|rules-based international order]] erodes, and that in the nuclear age such a settlement must be reached before, not after, catastrophe.

## Westphalia and Penn's Treaty

In the December 2025 essay [[a-new-american-renaissance|A New American Renaissance]], [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] returns to Westphalia in a colonial-American context, describing the mode of thinking expressed in the [[penn-treaty-of-1682|Penn Treaty of 1682]] as "very emblematic" of the Westphalian settlement and of what he calls the Principle of the Benefit of the Other, created in 1648 just forty years before [[william-penn|William Penn]]'s agreement with the Delaware. He characterizes the treaty ending the Thirty Years' War as enshrining into international law a Christian concept whereby "each nations' self-interest would now wholly be premised upon the benefit of their neighbor's welfare and the forgiveness of past transgressions" — a formulation he sees echoed in Penn's articles requiring that injuries, once satisfied, be "forgot and buried as in a bottomless pit."
