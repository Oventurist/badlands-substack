# Chisholm v. Georgia

*Chisholm, Ex'r v. Georgia* is an early decision of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]], cited in the Badlands Media article "A Startling Contradiction at the Heart of 'Our' Legal System" (May 16, 2023) as authority for the proposition that the authority of the several states — and therefore of the federal government they created — originates with the people.

## The quoted passage

The article dates the case to February 1794 and quotes it as follows:

> "A State does not owe its origin to the Government of the [federal] United States, in the highest or in any of its branches. It was in existence before it. It derives its authority from the same pure and sacred source as itself: The voluntary and deliberate choice of the people ... A State is altogether exempt from the jurisdiction of the Courts of the United States, or from any other exterior authority, unless in the special instances where the general Government has power derived from the Constitution itself ..."

## Use in the argument

For the article's author, [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]], the passage establishes that "the voluntary and deliberate choice of the people *is* the source of state authority, from which all else flows." Combined with the principle that no created entity can exceed the authority of its creator, the citation is used to argue that even if a corporate "United States" was later installed — see [[united-states-federal-corporation|United States as a Federal Corporation]] — it cannot displace the pre-existing chain of authority running from God, to the people, to the states, to the federal government. The article treats the installation of that corporate entity without popular knowledge or consent as an "extraordinary act of maladministration."

The decision is best known historically for holding that a citizen of one state could sue another state in federal court, a ruling whose practical effect was reversed by the Eleventh Amendment; the Badlands article does not discuss that aspect, drawing instead on the opinion's language about the origins of state sovereignty.
