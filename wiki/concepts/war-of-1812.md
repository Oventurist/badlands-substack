# War of 1812

The War of 1812 was a war between the United States and Great Britain. In the Badlands Media article "The Battle for Control," contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues that its underlying cause was a single thing: control of American money.[1]

## Lange's account of the causes

The [[first-bank-of-the-united-states|First Bank of the United States]] had been chartered in 1791 for a term of twenty years. When the charter expired in 1811 there was a drawn-out fight in [[congress|Congress]] over renewal, and in a close vote the Jeffersonian side prevailed; the charter was not renewed and the first central bank in America was dissolved.[1] One of the biggest objections was that two-thirds of the bank's stock was held by British interests, meaning the American people no longer controlled their own currency.[1] Lange concludes that although the British had been defeated on the battlefield in the Revolution, they retained control of the American money supply and commerce — and therefore of most of the government — until the central bank's dissolution ended that control.[1]

He treats it as no coincidence that war with Great Britain followed a year later.[1] The conventional background is that Britain was at war with France after the French Revolution had eliminated elite control there; Napoleon was at war with countries controlled by private bankers and had sold the United States the Louisiana territory in the early 1800s to fund his military; and the British were seizing American ships bound for the West Indies and impressing American sailors into the Royal Navy.[1] In Lange's reading, the decisive facts are that the United States no longer had a British-controlled central bank and was a trading partner of France — a loss of control that produced the war.[1]

## The debt template

Lange describes a repeating template used by what he calls the banking cabal: wars are started to force nations into debt, the nations are then persuaded to form a central bank to service that debt, and the citizens become debt slaves.[1] The Revolutionary War produced the debt that justified the First Bank; the expiry of that bank's charter was followed by a new war that generated new debt.[1]

## Citibank and war finance

Two days before the start of the war, the City Bank of New York — later [[citibank|Citibank]] — was chartered by the State Assembly.[1] Lange argues its sole purpose was to take the place of the dissolved central bank and return control to the bankers, with the government once again in war debt and dependent on private financiers.[1] The bank was made a federal depository in 1814, though in its early years it "mainly served the commercial interests of the merchants who owned it."[1]

## Aftermath

After the war the United States was in massive debt and the government chartered the [[second-bank-of-the-united-states|Second Bank of the United States]], recentralizing the banking system in the hands of a small group of private bankers.[1] The war also enabled [[john-jacob-astor|John Jacob Astor]], who had lost fur trading outposts during the conflict, to lobby Congress successfully for protectionist legislation that gave him a near monopoly on the American fur trade.[1]
