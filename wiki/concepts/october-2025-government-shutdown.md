# October 2025 Government Shutdown

The **October 2025 government shutdown** was a partial lapse in federal appropriations that began after the fiscal year expired on September 30, 2025, and which by October 7–8 had entered its eighth day with no clear path to resolution.[1] Coverage of the shutdown noted that most Americans had "hardly noticed, let alone grown agitated" as the funding lapse dragged on, a condition attributed to a process deliberately designed to minimize disruption when elected officials fail to perform their appropriating duties.[1]

## Structural analysis

Commentary reproduced in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] argued that efforts to limit the pain of shutdowns have converted them into an "all-too-common autumnal tradition," because the fiscal year expires every September 30 and the limited downside of a lapse breeds brinkmanship among legislators.[1] The argument holds that if a shutdown mainly inconveniences federal contractors who will not be paid, there is little political cost to allowing one.[1] By contrast, although both parties have repeatedly played chicken with raising the debt ceiling, the United States has never defaulted on its credit, because a sufficient number of lawmakers in both parties understand the dire consequences of default.[1]

A further conclusion drawn from the episode was that the federal government is too large and that "there is plenty of fat to cut," since the shutdown demonstrated that the federal bureaucracy performs many tasks classified as "nonessential" that have no direct bearing on the lives of most citizens.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] framed the shutdown on the [[devolution-power-hour|Devolution Power Hour]] as "an unprecedented opportunity" because the country could "actually wake up to how incompetent and wasteful and illegitimate and corrupt the government is."[1] He argued the correct lesson was not partisan victory over Democrats but a fundamental questioning of state legitimacy, contending that a government that "takes your money at gunpoint at the threat of state violence" to fund programs such as transgender surgeries "is not legitimate anymore," and that a government which does not serve the people and cannot prove the people ever gave their consent is "not your government."[1]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] offered a complementary framing, arguing that "the idea of an operating budget is not only silly for the federal government, but it's directly against the American people," and imagining a reversal in which citizens sit at a kitchen table "debating whether or not WE are going to fund THEM."[1]

The shutdown also formed part of the backdrop to other Badlands commentary during the period, with contributors citing "a shutdown government" and "a looming financial crisis" as features of the domestic scene in early October 2025.[1]

## Longest shutdown in U.S. history

On Tuesday, November 4, 2025, the shutdown surpassed the 34-day, 20-hour record set by the standoff that ended in January 2019, becoming the longest in U.S. history.[2] Just hours before that milestone, Senate Democrats privately agonized behind closed doors over how to bring the 35-day standoff to an end, with a lunch meeting of more than two hours ending without a clear consensus even after several senators involved in increasingly serious bipartisan negotiations laid out their thinking.[2] Senate Minority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] emerged from the meeting and signaled that his party was not yet ready to surrender.[2] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] blamed Republican losses in that day's off-year elections partly on the shutdown.[2]

## Military pay and NSPM-8

By October 15, 2025 the appropriations lapse had reached its fifteenth day, and it encompassed the annual appropriations that [[congress|Congress]] provides for the pay and allowances of military personnel.[3] Congressional leaders indicated that political negotiations had stalemated and that passage of the bipartisan legislation needed to end the lapse was unlikely before active-duty personnel were scheduled to receive their next paychecks on October 15th, an outcome the White House characterized as "a serious and unacceptable threat to military readiness and the ability of our Armed Forces to protect and defend our Nation."[3] In response President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] issued [[national-security-presidential-memorandum-8|National Security Presidential Memorandum 8]], directing the Secretary of War, in coordination with the Director of the [[office-of-management-and-budget|Office of Management and Budget]], to use available Fiscal Year 2026 [[department-of-war|Department of War]] funds to accomplish the scheduled disbursement of military pay.[3]

## Onset and immediate effects

The shutdown began at 12:01 a.m. ET on Wednesday, October 1, 2025, after [[congress|Congress]] failed to agree on a new budget before the midnight deadline.[4] Within its first days, funding for the [[special-supplemental-nutrition-program-for-women-infants-and-children|WIC]] program was placed on hold, which Rep. [[anna-paulina-luna|Anna Paulina Luna]] said cut aid to 6.7 million women, infants, and children.[4] Conservative outlets branded the lapse the "Schumer Shutdown," attributing it to Senate Democrats under [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]].[4]

## Vought's leverage and Republican unease

White House budget director [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]] planned an aggressive campaign of cuts during the lapse, prompting Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] to warn that "[t]his is the risk of shutting down the government and handing the keys to Russ Vought," while insisting Democrats had invited the outcome.[4] Republicans including Rep. Mike Lawler of New York and Reps. Blake Moore of Utah and Brian Babin of Texas objected respectively to held-up transportation projects and potential mass layoffs.[4] Asked whether Vought was muddying the party's message, Thune said, "yes, and we don't control what he's going to do."[4] Roughly $8 billion in what the administration called "Green New Scam" funding was cancelled across projects in CA, CO, CT, DE, HI, IL, MD, MA, MN, NH, NJ, NM, NY, OR, VT and WA — a list Badlands contributor Chris Paul noted consisted entirely of "blue" states.[4]

## Negotiating posture and commentary

House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] shut the door on negotiations on October 2, saying he had "nothing to negotiate" and had sent Democrats "exactly what they voted for before," while Democrats dug in on healthcare concessions.[4] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he had not wanted the shutdown but that it enabled firings and permanent cuts to Democratic priorities.[4] Construction of the privately funded [[white-house-ballroom|White House ballroom]] continued through the lapse.[4] Badlands commentators treated the episode variously as "Uniparty jousting" (Chris Paul), an opportunity for "the people to make the budget, while the politicians watch" (Burning Bright), and an occasion to stop paying "elected officials and entitlement fraudsters alike" (Ashe in America).[4]

## Republican pressure tactics in week three

As the shutdown entered its third week, Senate Republicans escalated pressure on Democrats by narrowing their procedural options and blaming the party for worsening hardship among military families.[5] On the night of Tuesday, October 14, 2025, the Senate rejected a House GOP measure to reopen the government by a vote of 49-45.[5] Senator Catherine Cortez Masto (D-Nev.) and Senator Angus King (I-Maine) were the only members of the Democratic caucus to vote to advance it, while Senator John Fetterman (D-Pa.), who had previously supported the measure, missed the vote; no other Democrats shifted position.[5]

The vote differed in one key respect from earlier rounds. Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] (R-S.D.), who until that week had allowed Democrats to easily schedule votes on their competing funding proposal, for the first time scheduled only a single vote to reopen the government — denying Democrats the chance to vote on their own plan, which had provided political cover to swing-state members of their caucus.[5] Thune and other GOP leaders timed the vote ahead of the critical October 15 payday for more than one million military service members, a constituency significant in both Republican and Democratic states.[5]

## Unfulfilled layoff threats in week two

As federal agencies entered the second week of the shutdown, the mass layoffs, agency closures and "irreversible" budget cuts warned of by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and senior [[white-house|White House]] officials had not materialized.[6] The White House budget office floated an argument that furloughed workers might not receive the back pay they are legally entitled to when they return to the job, and other plans remained on the table.[6] Even so, a growing belief took hold in Washington that Trump was becoming "trigger-shy" about adding to the disruption and federal workforce cuts already imposed during his second term.[6]

[[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] responded by advocating [[zero-based-budgeting|zero-based budgeting]] as a [[first-principles|First Principles]] reset for the federal government, writing "You are the mandate. The buck stops with you," and urging readers to "give Donald Trump permission to go all the way."[6]

## IRS furloughs

On Wednesday, October 8, 2025, the [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]] furloughed nearly half of its workforce under an updated contingency plan posted to its website, leaving only 39,870 employees, or 53.6 percent, on the job.[6] Most IRS operations were closed, the agency said in a separate letter to workers, and it was unclear which employees would remain.[6] The agency's initial Lapsed Appropriations Contingency Plan had covered the first five business days of operations and stated that the department would remain open using [[inflation-reduction-act|Inflation Reduction Act]] funds.[6] The furloughs followed the failure of Trump and [[congress|Congress]] to reach a funding agreement, with no discernible endgame in sight.[6] Badlands contributor [[gmoney|GMONEY]] remarked that "the IRS will literally have nobody left and people will still comply."[6]

## Day 16: defense bill gambit and blocked layoffs

By Wednesday, October 15, 2025 — day 16 of the shutdown — the Senate had rejected the House-passed continuing resolution for the ninth time, with another stopgap vote expected the following day.[7] Senate Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] responded by scheduling a Thursday floor vote on a full-year [[pentagon|Pentagon]] appropriations bill, a bipartisan measure that had cleared committee 26-3 and would fund military paychecks otherwise at risk if the impasse ran into November, effectively daring Democrats to block it while they pressed health care demands.[7] Democrats, who had received no guarantees about what Republicans might attach to the bill after the procedural hurdle, were divided on how to respond.[7] Badlands contributor Ashe in America argued the only complicating factor was Democratic powerlessness and asked what sixteen days of shutdown had actually saved.[7]

The same day, U.S. District Judge [[susan-illston|Susan Illston]] blocked the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] from laying off thousands of federal workers during the shutdown, ruling from the bench that officials had exceeded their authority and failed to follow legal requirements for reductions in force, and that the layoffs appeared politically motivated and unlawfully targeted at Democrats based on public comments by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], [[jd-vance|JD Vance]], and [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]].[7]

## Failed White House talks on the eve of the lapse

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and Democrats emerged from a private [[white-house|White House]] meeting on September 29, 2025 without an agreement to keep the federal government open, setting up the prospect of a wide-scale shutdown in major services beginning in roughly 30 hours.[8] The president and the four top congressional leaders failed to strike a deal, and if funding expired at midnight on October 1 — which appeared likely absent an unexpected breakthrough — the shutdown would begin at 12:01 a.m.[8]

House Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] described "a frank and direct discussion with the president of the United States and Republican leaders," but added that "significant and meaningful differences remain."[8] Both parties stood their ground: Republicans pushed a stopgap that would extend current funding levels until November 21, while Democrats fought for changes mostly related to health care access and subsidies as part of any deal.[8]

[[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] dismissed the blame contest entirely, writing that he did not care who got blamed because it was all political theatre, that he would cast blame on whoever voted to avert the shutdown or to end one, and that "All of congress sucks. They're all illegitimate."[8] The [[pentagon|Pentagon]] meanwhile issued its own [[pentagon-shutdown-contingency-guidance-2025|shutdown contingency guidance]] prioritising border security, Middle East operations, and critical defense programs.[8]

## End of the shutdown (November 12, 2025)

The longest shutdown in U.S. history ended on the night of Wednesday, November 12, 2025, when President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed a funding bill reopening the government.[9] The House had approved the measure earlier that evening by a vote of 222–209, with nearly every Republican and a handful of Democrats voting in favor.[9] Officials cautioned that a return to normal would take time: travelers continued to feel the shutdown's effects at airports, with more than 900 flights into and out of the United States cancelled that day, and the timing of full [[snap|food stamp]] benefit restoration was expected to vary by state.[9] The bill set up a new funding cliff in [[congress|Congress]] on January 30 while providing funding for a few key agencies through fiscal year 2026.[9]

The administration said most states would receive funds to distribute full SNAP benefits "within 24 hours" of the deal becoming law, a relief for the nearly 42 million people whose monthly benefits had been disrupted; the package restored the program to its original spending levels.[9] Separately, [[white-house|White House]] Press Secretary [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]] said the October jobs report and inflation data would likely never be released, because the [[bureau-of-labor-statistics|Bureau of Labor Statistics]] had been largely idle for the duration of the 40-plus-day shutdown and economists feared recall bias in any retroactive collection.[9]

[[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] focused his criticism on a provision slipped into the Senate-passed reopening bill that creates a backdated law allowing senators to sue the government and collect money, calling it "extra bullshit" that the carve-out applies to senators only and not to private citizens affected in the same way, and describing it as unconstitutional though unsurprising from "illegitimate government officials."[9]

## Day 1 (October 1, 2025)

The federal government shut down at midnight on 1 October 2025 after a deadlocked [[congress|Congress]] failed to reach a deal on funding, with Republicans and Democrats at odds over enhanced Obamacare subsidies.[10] Federal workers bore the brunt of the lapse, and the [[white-house|White House]] said federal workforce layoffs were "imminent," while agencies and activities deemed essential remained open.[10] Senators left town until Friday, meaning the shutdown would last at least until then and likely longer.[10] Badlands host [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] responded that the United States is in "$37 trillion of odious debt on paper" which "is not the People's debt," and questioned Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]]'s involvement in the negotiations.[10] The shutdown talks were also the backdrop to the [[deepfake-jeffries-sombrero-video|deepfake Jeffries sombrero video]] Trump posted after meeting [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] and [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]].[10]

## Vought and the threat of permanent cuts

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] warned Democrats that he would soon decide which "Democrat agencies" to cut and whether those reductions would be temporary or permanent, saying the shutdown had afforded him an "unprecedented opportunity."[11] On the Thursday morning after the shutdown began, he posted on Truth Social: "I have a meeting today with Russ Vought, he of PROJECT 2025 Fame."[11] The BBC reported that Trump was using [[project-2025|Project 2025]] as a threat to force Democrats to accept his budgetary demands, holding up [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]] — director of the [[office-of-management-and-budget|Office of Management and Budget]] and author of the blueprint's chapter on executive power — as a kind of budgetary angel of death ready to take a scythe to programs dear to Democrats.[11] That Thursday night Trump shared an AI-generated parody music video on Truth Social depicting Vought as the grim reaper, set to altered lyrics of Blue Öyster Cult's "Don't Fear the Reaper."[11]

Badlands host [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] said he and [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] had laid out in advance how Trump and Vought would roll out budget cuts during a shutdown they had baited, predicting "a death by a thousand cuts sort of thing... doing ironically what the DOGE deployment said it was going to do," and judged the early results "so far, so good."[11] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that the shutdown narrative was a rerun — "the covid death clock of 2025" — in which each side sacrifices principles on behalf of the State, and concluded that "it is good that the federal government is shut down," which should remain so at least until the People's relationship with the State has been totally reset.[11] The administration's warnings of major trimming of the federal government during the shutdown also preceded Defense Secretary [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]]'s firing of Navy chief of staff [[jon-harrison|Jon Harrison]].[11]


## Resolution

On the night of Monday, November 10, 2025, the [[us-senate|Senate]] passed a government funding package by a vote of 60–40, paving the way for ending what had become the longest shutdown in history.[12] The vote came roughly twenty-four hours after a bipartisan group of rank-and-file senators, working in tandem with Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]], reached an agreement that broke the weeks-long partisan stalemate.[12] Eight members of the Senate Democratic Caucus voted to pass the deal — the same configuration of lawmakers who had voted to advance the package the night before.[12]

The bipartisan bill still required passage by the [[us-house-of-representatives|House]] and the signature of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] before the government could reopen, and Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] advised his members that the House could vote on the package as soon as Wednesday.[12] Trump was expected to lean on potential House GOP holdouts, while a cadre of moderate House Democrats was thought likely to support the plan in a break with party leaders still smarting over the failure to secure an extension of expiring [[obamacare-subsidy-lapse|Obamacare tax credits]].[12] Senate passage put the federal funding lapse on track to be over by the end of the week.[12]

The package included a three-bill "minibus" funding the Department of Agriculture and the FDA, the Department of Veterans Affairs and military construction projects, and the operations of [[congress|Congress]] for the whole of the current fiscal year — the product of months of bipartisan, bicameral negotiation among top appropriators.[12] All other agencies would be funded through January 30.[12]

## Democratic backlash

The deal convulsed the Democratic Party, because the moderates' support all but ensured the shutdown would end without Republicans conceding anything on the Democrats' central demand for an extension of ObamaCare subsidies.[12] Liberals in and out of Congress, who had cheered the party throughout the weeks-long standoff and urged it to keep fighting to prevent health care costs from spiking at the start of the following year, were infuriated by the Sunday-night development.[12] Their frustration was intensified by the previous week's off-year election results, which delivered resounding Democratic victories across the country and suggested that voters wanted the fight over health care subsidies to continue.[12] Representative [[mark-pocan|Mark Pocan]] of Wisconsin, a former head of the Congressional Progressive Caucus, wrote on X that the senators who backed the deal had undone "all the hard work people put in to Tuesday's elections."[12] Liberal activist groups likewise accused Democrats of failing to fight hard enough against Trump since his return to the White House.[12]

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the democratic socialists were "big mad because they got nothing out of it," argued that Republicans had secured new provisions for themselves as legislators rather than for their constituents, and asked readers what the people actually gain by reopening the government — answering that it was "about the same as the DSA."[12]

## Cloture vote and effects on arms deliveries

The GOP bill to reopen the government secured 60 votes after at least eight Democrats crossed the aisle to support it, beginning what ZeroHedge described as the long and drawn-out process of Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] presenting the original House-passed continuing resolution for a vote, to which the minibus and updated CR would then be attached, followed by two more votes before the package returned to the House and, if passed, to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s desk.[13]

By the time of the November 9 breakthrough the shutdown had run 40 days, making it the longest in United States history, and it had delayed more than $5 billion in arms exports to European [[nato|NATO]] members and subsequent transfers to [[ukraine|Ukraine]], according to Axios reporting.[13] A senior [[state-department|State Department]] official said the lapse was "actually really harming both our allies and partners and US industry to actually deliver a lot of these critical capabilities overseas."[13] The affected exports included AMRAAM air-to-air missiles, HIMARS and other weapons; the delay stemmed from a lag in congressional approval, because the State Department bureau that normally briefs lawmakers on arms exports was operating with only a quarter of its usual staff.[13] The shutdown had also frozen talks between Washington and Kiev on future arms shipments.[13]

Badlands host [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the stalled deliveries as convenient rather than accidental, noting that the shutdown was choking off weapons to NATO that Trump had pressed the alliance to buy, even as Trump sought a summit with [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]].[13] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] tied the sudden availability of Senate votes to Trump's simultaneous announcement of [[trump-tariff-dividend-proposal|tariff dividends]], calling the sequence "quite literally The Art of the Deal."[13] In a separate item, Trump proposed a compromise on health insurance payments, urging Republicans to send federal payments that would normally go to insurers under [[affordable-care-act|Obamacare]] directly to Americans in order to end the shutdown, arguing that the insurance-industrial complex aligned with the Democrats had seen stock gains between 400% and nearly 1,200% since the ACA was introduced.[13]

## Midnight lapse and the failed Senate vote

The federal government partially shut down at midnight on Wednesday, October 1, 2025, hours after all but three Senate Democrats voted down a short-term funding bill.[14] It was the first shutdown since December 2018, an episode that saw non-essential operations cease and tens of thousands of federal employees furloughed or forced to work without pay for 35 days.[14] A House-passed bill backed by virtually all Senate Republicans and President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], which would have kept the government open at current funding levels until November 21, failed to clear the 60-vote legislative filibuster in the upper chamber by five votes.[14]

[[office-of-management-and-budget|Office of Management and Budget]] Director [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]] notified federal department and agency heads shortly after the failed vote to begin preparations, writing in a memo that "[a]ffected agencies should now execute their plans for an orderly shutdown."[14] Vought added that it was "unclear how long Democrats will maintain their untenable posture, making the duration of the shutdown difficult to predict."[14] In the run-up, OMB had also warned that absent a funding agreement before October 1 it would lay off some non-essential workers in addition to imposing furloughs.[14]

Badlands contributor [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] noted that Trump's first term had featured three shutdowns: January 20–22, 2018 over DACA; a hours-long lapse on February 9, 2018 arising from a Rand Paul protest; and the December 22, 2018–January 25, 2019 border-wall shutdown, the longest to that point at 35 days.[14] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] mocked the media framing, observing that shutdowns perpetually "loom" and that CNN was running a second-by-second countdown.[14] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] pointed to Trump himself posting a lighthearted image about the shutdown, arguing that the breathless panic was conspicuously not coming from the man nominally in charge of the government — a fact he said should tell readers something about what "the government" actually is.[14]
