# Puberty Blockers

Puberty blockers, clinically known as gonadotrophin releasing hormone analogues (GnRHa) or puberty suppressing hormones (PSH), are drugs that prevent the body from producing sex hormones, thereby halting the progression of puberty.[1]

## NHS restriction, March 2024

In March 2024 the [[nhs|National Health Service]] in the United Kingdom announced that children would no longer receive puberty blocker prescriptions, following expert conclusions that serious safety concerns existed.[1] The NHS had commissioned the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) to review the published evidence on GnRHa drugs.[1] An [[nhs-england|NHS England]] policy document published on Tuesday, March 12, 2024 stated that the body had "carefully considered the evidence review conducted by NICE (2020)" and had identified and reviewed any further published evidence available to date.[1] It concluded that "there is not enough evidence to support the safety or clinical effectiveness of PSH (puberty suppressing hormones) to make the treatment routinely available at this time."[1] Transgender lobbying organizations objected to the decision.[1]

## Regulatory origins and off-label use

Badlands writer [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] argued that puberty blockers are dangerous for children even within their approved indication.[1] He noted that the drugs were originally approved for pediatric use to treat precocious puberty, a condition whose listed risks are generally limited to reduced adult height and, sometimes, social ostracization — a mismatch he characterized as a shaky foundation for the drugs' later off-label use to halt puberty indefinitely as part of gender-affirming care.[1]

## Commentary on downstream effects

Esler contended that if the controversy amounted to a moral panic, it was a justified one.[1] He cited as an example the development of a micropenis in boys placed on long-term blockade, which he said can later leave insufficient penile tissue for vaginoplasty, obliging surgeons to harvest tissue from the intestines or colon further along what he termed the "gender affirmative conveyor belt."[1]

## Susan Bradley's recantation (2023)

Canadian psychiatrist [[susan-bradley|Susan Bradley]], who founded a pediatric gender clinic in 1975 and whose clinic began prescribing puberty blockers around 2000, publicly repudiated the practice in March 2023.[2] Bradley said the blocker-and-affirmation model — since adopted widely by medical establishments including in the United States — can cement a child's confusion out of which they would otherwise likely grow, and she raised concerns about the drugs' side effects.[2] Her account noted that under her earlier therapy-focused approach most patients outgrew their feelings of being transgender over time.[2] See [[gender-dysphoria|gender dysphoria]].[2]

## Off-label use for gender dysphoria

A February 2024 Badlands Media essay by [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] noted that puberty blockers have only been authorized for the treatment of "precocious puberty," and that when prescribed for that indication the effect is transient, with children soon free to resume growing into adults.[3] When prescribed for [[gender-dysphoria|gender dysphoria]], by contrast, the drugs are intended to halt puberty in a way that pushes most recipients toward hormone therapy and permanent bodily change.[3] Because this is off-label use, the essay argued the medication has not been well investigated as a treatment for gender dysphoria.[3]

The article further criticized [[wpath|WPATH]]'s position that access should not require a clinical diagnosis at all — that a youngster who "feels incongruent" has a right to delay puberty — arguing this opens a floodgate in which decades of research on true clinical dysphoria is disregarded.[3] It contrasted this with England's [[nhs|NHS]], whose October 2022 interim service specification restricted provision and called for local authorities to be alerted in some cases where young people obtained blockers or hormones on the private market.[3] See [[gender-affirming-care|gender affirmative care]].[3]

## Lupron and the "reversibility" claim

Reviewing the documentary [[what-is-a-woman|"What Is a Woman?"]] in June 2023, Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] singled out the claim that pausing puberty with Lupron is completely reversible as among the central myths propagated by gender theory advocates, noting that Lupron is the same chemical castration drug known for its use with sex offenders.[4] She placed the drug within a broader pipeline in which post-operative transgender patients face a lifelong battle of medications, additional surgeries and therapies - an industry she described as booming and worth billions, with detransitioner [[scott-newgent|Scott Newgent]] quoting surgery alone at "$70,000 a pop."[4]

## International restrictions cited by Badlands

[[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] wrote in March 2023 that girls were seeking access to Lupron — a drug he noted is typically used to castrate sex offenders — in order to halt puberty and avoid what they were told was the trauma of becoming a woman.[5] He documented two national reversals on the practice: in the autumn of 2022 the [[nhs|NHS]] in England restricted puberty blockers to research protocol settings, as reported by the [[society-for-evidence-based-gender-medicine|Society for Evidence Based Gender Medicine]], and in May 2021 Sweden became the first country to stop prescribing puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones to children under 16.[5]

The Swedish decision was announced in a letter from [[karolinska-hospital|Karolinska Hospital]] citing a December 2019 SBU review that found a lack of evidence for the long-term consequences of the treatments, and warning that they are "potentially fraught with extensive and irreversible adverse consequences such as cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, infertility, increased cancer risk, and thrombosis."[5] The letter also cited a court ruling holding that it is "highly unlikely, if at all possible, for an individual under the age of 16 to give informed consent to this treatment," and advising court approval for 16- to 18-year-olds because the treatment should be considered experimental.[5]
