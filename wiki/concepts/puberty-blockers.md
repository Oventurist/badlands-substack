# Puberty Blockers

Puberty blockers, clinically known as gonadotrophin releasing hormone analogues (GnRHa) or puberty suppressing hormones (PSH), are drugs that prevent the body from producing sex hormones, thereby halting the progression of puberty.[1]

## NHS restriction, March 2024

In March 2024 the [[nhs|National Health Service]] in the United Kingdom announced that children would no longer receive puberty blocker prescriptions, following expert conclusions that serious safety concerns existed.[1] The NHS had commissioned the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) to review the published evidence on GnRHa drugs.[1] An [[nhs-england|NHS England]] policy document published on Tuesday, March 12, 2024 stated that the body had "carefully considered the evidence review conducted by NICE (2020)" and had identified and reviewed any further published evidence available to date.[1] It concluded that "there is not enough evidence to support the safety or clinical effectiveness of PSH (puberty suppressing hormones) to make the treatment routinely available at this time."[1] Transgender lobbying organizations objected to the decision.[1]

## Regulatory origins and off-label use

Badlands writer [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] argued that puberty blockers are dangerous for children even within their approved indication.[1] He noted that the drugs were originally approved for pediatric use to treat precocious puberty, a condition whose listed risks are generally limited to reduced adult height and, sometimes, social ostracization — a mismatch he characterized as a shaky foundation for the drugs' later off-label use to halt puberty indefinitely as part of gender-affirming care.[1]

## Commentary on downstream effects

Esler contended that if the controversy amounted to a moral panic, it was a justified one.[1] He cited as an example the development of a micropenis in boys placed on long-term blockade, which he said can later leave insufficient penile tissue for vaginoplasty, obliging surgeons to harvest tissue from the intestines or colon further along what he termed the "gender affirmative conveyor belt."[1]
