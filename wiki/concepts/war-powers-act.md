# War Powers Act

The **War Powers Act** of 1973 (the War Powers Resolution, codified in part at 50 U.S.C. § 1541) governs the circumstances under which a president may introduce United States armed forces into hostilities without a declaration of war.[1] In the Badlands Media article "[[irregular-warfare|Irregular Warfare]]," the statute is presented as the president's "trump card" — the legal mechanism that allows unilateral military action.[1]

## The cited provision

Quoting We Are The Mighty, the article reproduces the policy section of 50 U.S.C. § 1541, which states that the constitutional powers of the president as Commander-in-Chief to introduce armed forces into hostilities, or into situations where imminent hostilities are clearly indicated by the circumstances, are exercised only pursuant to (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency created by an attack upon the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed forces.[1] The ordinary reading of the resolution is that it constrains presidential war-making by requiring notification of Congress and withdrawal absent authorization; the article instead emphasizes the third prong as an enabling clause.[1]

## The Badlands interpretation

Joe Lange argues that because a declared national emergency removes the requirement of congressional approval, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s repeated declarations of national emergency beginning in his first term "completely change the rules."[1] He cites the use of the military to build sections of the border wall as a concrete product of that authority.[1] Combined with the founding statutes governing the [[united-states-marine-corps|Marine Corps]] — which direct the Corps to be prepared for "such other duties as the President may direct" — the article concludes that Trump can deploy Marines domestically or abroad without congressional approval or the consent of any state governor, and that such deployment is not limited to combat.[1]

This framing supports the article's broader thesis that domestic disorder attributed to Soros-funded groups could be suppressed by presidentially directed Marines, and that military authority provided the "invisible hand" operating behind the civilian agencies.[1] The legal reading offered is contested: mainstream constitutional scholarship treats the War Powers Resolution as a limitation on, not a grant of, presidential authority, and domestic deployment of federal troops is separately constrained by statutes such as the Posse Comitatus Act, which the article does not address.
