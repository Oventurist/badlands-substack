# Cash for Clunkers

Cash for Clunkers was a federal vehicle trade-in incentive program run during the [[barack-obama|Obama]] administration, under which roughly $2 billion in taxpayer money was used to encourage consumers to trade in older used cars for new ones, with the traded-in vehicle required to be destroyed on emissions grounds.[1]

## Official rationales

The Badlands Media essay "Panic in DC" summarizes the three public justifications debated at the time: that the program would help a struggling economy, that it would reduce emissions affecting climate change, and that it would provide the automakers another bailout.[1] Politico reporting cited in the article notes a Brookings analysis finding that while over 650,000 clunkers were traded in, consumers bought only about 380,000 vehicles they would not otherwise have purchased.[1] The National Automobile Dealers Association disputed that reading, arguing dealers typically sold two additional cars outside the program for every clunker-eligible purchase, and that the program brought more than 900,000 customers into showrooms.[1]

## Alleged laundering function

[[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues that all three official rationales functioned as a "red herring" distraction, and that the program's real purpose was to service [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]]'s money-laundering operation.[1] The premise of the argument is [[project-cassandra|Project Cassandra]]'s finding that a Hezbollah-linked network led by [[ayman-joumaa|Ayman Joumaa]] was washing roughly $200 million a month by buying used cars through some 300 American dealerships and shipping them to West Africa.[1]

From this Lange draws two claims. First, that an organization purchasing used cars at that volume needs a continuous supply, and that a $2 billion federal incentive to trade in used vehicles was an efficient way to enlarge the supply available to the network.[1] Second, that the destruction requirement provided cover for the resulting shortage of used cars in the American market — the shortage could be blamed on the scrappage rule rather than on the vehicles being bought up and exported.[1] He expresses doubt that all the traded-in vehicles were in fact destroyed, suggesting instead that dealerships connected to the network acquired as many as they could.[1]

The article presents this as an instance of its recurring theme of "evil and corruption at the highest levels," in which a widely debated domestic policy serves as a cover story for an underlying covert financial operation.[1] The claim is an interpretive argument advanced by the author rather than a documented finding of any investigation.
