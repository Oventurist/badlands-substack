# Take Our Border Back Convoy

The "Take Our Border Back" convoy was a January 2024 movement of American truckers and citizens who traveled from multiple locations across the lower 48 states toward the southern border, organized amid escalating tensions between Texas and the Biden administration over border enforcement.[1] Organizers called on "active & retired law enforcement and military, Veterans, Mama Bears, elected officials, business owners, ranchers, truckers, bikers, media and LAW ABIDING, freedom-loving Americans" to assemble "in honor of our US Constitution and Bill of Rights" in protest of the federal government's failure to secure the border.[1]

## Leadership and rhetoric

[[pete-chambers|Pete Chambers]], one of the coalition's leaders, issued a call echoing the language of the Texas Revolution: "Fellow citizens and compatriots ... I call on you in the name of liberty, of patriotism and everything dear to the American character to come to our aid with all dispatch."[1] Chambers characterized Americans as "besieged on all sides" by evil "dark forces," and the convoy's stated aim was to send a message to officials at all levels about the need to secure the border amid what organizers described as a multi-year invasion of millions of illegal entrants.[1]

## The "Army of God" framing

Media coverage, including a ZeroHedge report, labeled the effort the "Army of God" convoy.[1] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], citing a source deeply connected to the organizers, wrote that no one associated with the event referred to it that way: Chambers had mentioned joining "God's Army" in an interview and participants had discussed putting on the "Armor of God" in the sense of Ephesians, but the "Army of God" branding was, in her account, a media fabrication.[1]

## Actual plans versus media positioning

According to the same reporting, the convoy was not in fact going to the border itself. Organizers rented a private 240-acre ranch on which participants would gather and protest; while promotional materials referenced Eagle Pass, the maps and routes directed convoy-goers to the private ranch.[1] The event's website still listed locations in California and Arizona as to-be-determined, with the main event in Texas proceeding rain or shine.[1]

Despite official attempts to frame the protest as vigilantism, the stated intent was a rally described as a festival atmosphere with cookouts, bounce houses, live music and high-profile speakers, with many politicians and candidates expected and rumors of major music acts.[1] Local law enforcement was reportedly to be present in plain clothes alongside private security to keep the event peaceful, with provocateurs to be detained and prosecuted, and the convoy was said to be escorted by National Guard forces.[1]

## Scale and security concerns

Organizers expected more than a million people to gather in Texas by that Saturday and expressed confidence that they had taken all available precautions against a January 6-style false flag event.[1] Badlands commentary held that the manner in which the event came together, and the parties involved in its production, made it distinctive from January 6, while cautioning that risks always attend mass gatherings and that participants needed "their heads on swivels."[1] The commentary further argued that the regime was desperate for a narrative change on the border and would attempt to manufacture real-world examples of vigilantism to fuel election-year anti-MAGA storylines, while affirming that the right to rally, protest and redress grievances is inalienable and retained only by exercising it.[1]

## European parallel

ZeroHedge framed the convoy alongside simultaneous farmer protests blockading European capitals from Bucharest to Warsaw to Brussels over climate policies, presenting both as social instabilities erupting ahead of key European and U.S. elections.[1] The research firm Stratfor assessed that the farmer protests would likely slow implementation of environmental reforms in the [[european-union|European Union]] and make it harder for Brussels to negotiate free trade agreements.[1]
