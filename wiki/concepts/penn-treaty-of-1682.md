# Penn Treaty of 1682

The Penn Treaty of 1682 was the agreement between [[william-penn|William Penn]] and the Delaware natives of Pennsylvania, commemorated in a 1771 painting by [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] and examined at length in the Badlands Media essay [[a-new-american-renaissance|A New American Renaissance]].

## Circumstances

The source stresses that Penn was under no obligation to make the treaty. Having been granted sole dictatorial authority over Pennsylvania by Crown Charter, he could have applied the "might makes right" principle of empire, as had been done many times before and after. Instead he negotiated an unprecedented settlement, paying the Delaware generously in a variety of ways and guaranteeing them total freedom to use the land as they saw fit even after European settlement began.

West's painting depicts not the signing but the respectful negotiations preceding it, with textiles and goods — pointedly "not alcohol or guns" — offered by Penn's negotiators beneath the famous cedar tree where the treaty was concluded. The essay notes an anomalous shadow cast over the central figures whose source cannot be identified in the composition, and suggests it may signify the dangers ahead, since European–native relations in the decades after 1682 were not always healthy. Nevertheless, Penn's treaty was renowned as one of the only treaties to endure, honored for many generations.

## Articles

The essay quotes the treaty at length. Article 1 declared that all of Penn's people or Christians and all the Indians "should be brethren, as the children of one father, joined together as with one heart, one head, and one body." Article 2 opened all paths freely to both. Article 3 opened the doors of Christian and Indian houses to each other, requiring each to make the other welcome as friends.

Article 4, singled out as the most important, provided that neither side should believe "any false rumours or reports" of the other, "but should first come as brethren to inquire of each other," and that such false reports should be buried "as in a bottomless pit." Article 5 required each to speedily acquaint the other with any ill news affecting them, "as true friends and brethren." Article 6 forbade harm to each other or their creatures, but acknowledged that "as there are wicked people in all nations," complaints should be made by the person suffering and satisfaction made, after which the wrong "should be forgot and buried as in a bottomless pit." Article 8 obliged each to assist the other "against all wicked people that would disturb them." Article 9 required both to acquaint their children with "this league and firm chain of friendship," which should be "kept bright and clean without rust or spot between our children and children's children while the creeks and rivers run and while the sun and moon and stars endure."

## Interpretation

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] reads Article 4 in particular as a deliberate defense against intelligence operations — he specifies [[jesuits|Jesuitical]] ones — that used slander and gossip to inflame hostilities between colonists and native peoples. The agreement to verify before reacting emotionally to provocation, he writes, "makes all the difference." The treaty's underlying ethic is identified with the [[peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]] of 1648, concluded just forty years earlier, and its Principle of the Benefit of the Other: the Christian idea, enshrined in international law after the Thirty Years' War, that each nation's self-interest is wholly premised on its neighbor's welfare and on the forgiveness of past transgressions.
