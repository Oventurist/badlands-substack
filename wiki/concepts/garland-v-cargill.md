# Garland v. Cargill

**Garland v. Cargill** is the United States Supreme Court case, decided on Friday, June 14, 2024, in which the Court invalidated the federal rule classifying bump stocks as "machineguns." The decision came by a 6–3 vote, with the majority opinion authored by Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]].[1] The rule at issue had been promulgated by the [[bureau-of-alcohol-tobacco-firearms-and-explosives|Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives]] during the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration and was defended in court by the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration.[1]

## Holding

The Court held that "ATF exceeded its statutory authority by issuing a Rule that classifies a bump stock as a 'machinegun' under §5845(b)."[1] Justice Thomas wrote that Congress has long restricted access to machineguns, a category of firearms defined by the ability to "shoot, automatically more than one shot . . . by a single function of the trigger" under 26 U.S.C. §5845(b) and 18 U.S.C. §922(o), and that semiautomatic firearms — which require the shooter to reengage the trigger for every shot — are not machineguns.[1] The question presented was whether a bump stock, an accessory for a semiautomatic rifle that allows the shooter to rapidly reengage the trigger and therefore achieve a high rate of fire, converts the rifle into a machinegun; the Court held that it does not and affirmed the judgment below.[1]

## Background

The opinion recounts that for many years ATF consistently took the position that semiautomatic rifles equipped with bump stocks were not machineguns under §5845(b).[1] The agency abruptly changed course after the [[las-vegas-shooting|Las Vegas shooting]], in which a gunman using semiautomatic rifles equipped with bump stocks fired hundreds of rounds into a crowd, killing 58 people and wounding more than 500.[1] ATF subsequently proposed a rule, published at 83 Fed. Reg. 13442, repudiating its previous guidance and amending its regulations to "clarify" that bump stocks are machineguns; the rule ordered owners of bump stocks to destroy or surrender them to ATF to avoid criminal prosecution.[1]

## Dissent

Justice [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]] issued what Badlands Media described as a fiery dissent, writing that the majority's reading "flies in the face of this Court's standard tools of statutory interpretation" and that by casting aside the statute's ordinary meaning both at the time of enactment and today, the majority "eviscerates Congress's regulation of machineguns and enables gun users and manufacturers to circumvent federal law."[1]

## Litigant and reception

The lawsuit was brought by Texas gun dealer [[michael-cargill|Michael Cargill]], who owned several bump stocks before the ban took effect and later surrendered them to the government.[1] Cargill posted a two-word message on X after the ruling: "I won."[1] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the case as the undoing of an unconstitutional gun law imposed in the wake of the Las Vegas shooting, noting that [[brian-lupo|Brian Lupo]] had remarked on that Friday's Daily that bump stocks are "stupid" and that Cargill himself acknowledged hearing frequently that "nobody cares about bump stocks" — but that Cargill's five-year journey was motivated by the principle that the right "shall not be infringed."[1]
