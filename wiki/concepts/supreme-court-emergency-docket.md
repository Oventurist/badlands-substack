# Supreme Court Emergency Docket

The emergency docket of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] is the mechanism by which the Court issues quick-turn orders dictating whether government policies should be left in place while they are litigated through the lower courts.[1] It has become a growing part of the Court's work in recent years and took on greater importance amid the flood of litigation challenging President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s efforts to expand executive power after his return to office.[1]

## The 2025 judicial backlash

In October 2025, more than three dozen federal judges told The New York Times that the Court's flurry of brief, opaque emergency orders in cases related to the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] had left them confused about how to proceed and were hurting the judiciary's image with the public.[1] Sixty-five judges responded to a Times questionnaire sent to hundreds of federal judges across the country, and of those, 47 said the Supreme Court had been mishandling its emergency docket since Trump returned to office.[1] The Times reached out to more than 400 judges, including every judge in districts that had handled at least one legal challenge to a major piece of Trump's agenda; most who declined to participate gave no reason, while others said they did not think it was their place to judge the work of the Supreme Court.[1]

Although the orders are technically temporary, they have had broad practical effects, allowing the administration to deport tens of thousands of people, discharge transgender military service members, fire thousands of government workers, and slash federal spending.[1] The critique from lower court judges was described as striking and highly unusual, revealing the degree to which litigation over Trump's agenda has strained the federal judicial system.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] inverted the framing, asking why the episode was not being characterized as a judicial coup, and questioning why there were no complaints about the federal judges themselves delegitimizing the Court.[1]
