# Biden Offshore Drilling Ban

The Biden offshore drilling ban was an eleventh-hour executive action announced on January 6, 2025, prohibiting new offshore oil and gas drilling along most of America's coast in federal waters.[1]

President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], then 82, announced the move two weeks before leaving office. The newly off-limits ocean zone was described as larger than the states of Alaska and Texas combined and more than six times the area of California.[1]

Biden used a law that has no clear mechanism for a future president to reverse the order, meaning congressional action may be required to undo it. President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s transition team called the action "political revenge," and Trump himself pledged "I'll unban it immediately," telling conservative radio host Hugh Hewitt on the morning of the announcement that he was preparing to undo the order.[1]

## Legal obstacles to reversal

Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] argued in January 2025 that undoing the ban would prove far more difficult than a simple "un-ban" order.[2] After a presidential campaign in which both President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] pushed back on claims that they were trying to shut down much of the fossil fuel industry, Biden waited until the final days of his administration to ban oil and gas drilling across 670 million acres of America's coastline.[2] President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] responded that "It's ridiculous. I'll un-ban it immediately. I have the right to un-ban it immediately."[2]

Biden acted under Section 12(a) of the 1953 Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), which states that the president "may, from time to time, withdraw from disposition any of the unleased lands of the Outer Continental Shelf."[2] As a Congressional Research Service report noted, there is an ongoing debate over whether presidents can reverse the withdrawals of prior presidents; Trump faced that question in 2017 when he sought to overturn a ban imposed by President [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]], and two years later a judge on the U.S. District Court for the District of Alaska struck down Trump's order, with litigation running out the clock before Biden overturned the Trump executive order entirely.[2] Turley concluded that grounds exist to assert an authority of reversal, but that establishing it would take years in court, making Congress the alternative and preferred route.[2]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] agreed that the congressional route was preferable but doubted Congress would prove functional despite Republicans controlling both chambers.[2] She suggested the ban could be framed either as punishment for voters rejecting Biden's party or as "our boy" teeing up a legal battle to clarify ambiguity in the law — ambiguity she argued had been intentionally preserved by those who benefit from it, whereas "clarity benefits the people."[2] She predicted that in four years the law would be less of a weapon, the United States would be a net exporter of energy, and road trips would be affordable again.[2]
