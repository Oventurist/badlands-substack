# Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act was the 1999 US law that [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] calls the final nail in the coffin for the [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall]] separation of commercial and investment banking in the United States.[1]

## Passage

Ehret writes that by 1999 a "politically-castrated" [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] found himself signing into law legislation authored by then Treasury Secretary [[larry-summers|Larry Summers]] known as the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act.[1]

## Consequences

The new age of unregulated trading and creation of over-the-counter [[derivatives|derivatives]] caused these instruments to grow from $60 trillion in 2000 to $600 trillion by 2008.[1] With Glass-Steagall removed, legitimate capital such as pension funds could be used to fuel a hedge to end all hedges, with billions poured into mortgage-backed securities in a market where the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] had held interest rates at record lows of 1–2% for over a year.[1] House values skyrocketed far beyond real values, with hundred-thousand-dollar homes selling for five to six times that price within several years.[1]

The apparent success of securitizing housing debt induced a wave of sovereign wealth funds to apply the same model — used for mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations — to the debts of entire nations, so that securitizing bundled sovereign debts for infinite leverage on deregulated markets came to be seen not as national treason but as the key to easy money.[1] This is the system Ehret says died in the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 crash]].[1]
