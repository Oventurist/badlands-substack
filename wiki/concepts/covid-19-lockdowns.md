# COVID-19 Lockdowns

The COVID-19 lockdowns are treated by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] in *A Virus, or a Metaphor?* not as a public-health measure but as the intended payload of the pandemic itself.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

## Alleged purpose

Carlson states his firm belief that the plan of the [[deep-state-cabal|Cabal]] was to keep America and the world shut down for a far longer period — perhaps up to five years, or however long was necessary to destroy the middle class and create a two-tier society consisting of the working-class poor and the elite.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor) In his account the lockdowns would have continued until the [[deep-state|Deep State]] had complete control of everything, culminating in a one-world totalitarian government.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

## Consequences claimed

The [[covid-19-narrative|Covid narrative]] forced the lockdowns, which in turn produced an overreaching control of people, an abandonment of human rights, and a justification for mail-in ballots; through the work of these communists, Carlson writes, a duly-elected President was overthrown and removed from power.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

He extends the point to the classification of workers: under communism there is a ruling elite and a working-class poor with nothing in between — one is either the served or the server, the slave or the master.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor) Anyone not designated an "essential" worker during the lockdowns, he suggests, may have been receiving an indicator that the ruling elite have no place for them in the two-tiered system; when the Soviet Union no longer had a place for people, they faced a firing squad.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

## Weighed against vaccination

The lockdowns supply the central premise of Carlson's defense of Trump on vaccines: he argues [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] weighed the possible damage from vaccines against the damage of extended lockdowns and concluded the lockdowns were significantly worse.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-virus-or-a-metaphor)

## Elite exemption and profiteering

In "Trump Knows," [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] wrote that during the pandemic response citizens were asked to shelter in place, lock down their lives and ruin their businesses in the name of public safety, while the politicians, experts and industry leaders who demanded it refused to participate themselves.[3] He listed the consequences borne by ordinary people: mothers unable to see sick children in hospital, husbands unable to support their families, children kept home from school, and mask and vaccine mandates imposed on a public that was largely willing to shoulder its share of the burden.[3]

Meanwhile, he argued, elites worldwide ignored the lockdowns, exploited reduced competition to expand and pad profit margins, and enacted laws and policies seeking to make permanent the freedoms surrendered temporarily.[3] He cited a March 2021 Yahoo News report that fifteen companies gained over a trillion dollars in market capitalization because of the pandemic, with Netflix up $97.51 billion, Zoom up $77.67 billion and Facebook up $192.86 billion, none of which lowered prices to help the economically depressed.[3] Alongside pandemic-era [[2020-congressional-insider-trading-scandal|congressional insider trading]], this is offered as proof of a [[two-tier-justice-system|two-tiered]] order that [[first-inaugural-address-of-donald-trump|Trump's 2017 inaugural address]] had anticipated.[3]

## Economic asymmetry between listed and private firms

Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argued in February 2023 that the lockdowns fell with radically unequal force on public and private enterprise.[2] When the lockdowns began in March 2020 the [[sp-500|S&P 500]] fell 35% in an extremely short period, but most of the stock market fully recovered within six months — a recovery, he said, only of the perceived value of the 0.1% of US businesses that are publicly traded.[2]

Many listed companies, technology firms especially, never had to shut down: their employees could work from home and their products and services remained purchasable during near-full lockdown, and firms such as [[zoom-video-communications|Zoom]] and [[peloton|Peloton]] thrived while people could not meet in person and gyms were closed.[2] Grocery stores stayed open and restaurants could operate drive-throughs, so publicly traded corporate chains captured spending that had gone to family-run restaurants without drive-through or take-out.[2] Retailers that also sold groceries — [[target|Target]], [[walmart|Walmart]], [[costco|Costco]] — could sell general merchandise as well while small competitors were closed, and online sellers led by [[amazon|Amazon]] took large market share from brick-and-mortar rivals.[2]

Carlson estimated that roughly a third of [[small-business-in-the-united-states|small businesses]] did not or would not survive the lockdowns and their aftermath, leaving large corporate businesses with an even bigger share of the market.[2] He also linked the lockdowns to the [[covid-19-stimulus-checks|stimulus checks]] funded by [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] money printing and the [[inflation|inflation]] that followed.[2]
