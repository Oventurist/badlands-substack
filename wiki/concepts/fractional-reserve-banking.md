# Fractional Reserve Banking

Fractional reserve banking is the banking model in which institutions hold only a fraction of deposits in reserve, and which underpins the paper-currency systems criticized in Badlands Media's monetary commentary.[1]

## Badlands critique

Writing in July 2023, [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] described the fractional reserve banking system — "one where paper currencies rule the day" — as "the perfect financial warfare weapon because it can be manipulated very easily."[1] In his account prices can be manipulated through transactions, allowing "whales," meaning rich investors, to engage in market activity that rigs the financial system in their favor.[1]

He linked the system historically to the modern stock market, noting that the market emerged in the Netherlands only a few years after the formation of the first modern fractional reserve banking system, and arguing that it is in part designed to cause price instability by instilling avarice in the general public.[1] Because currency is constantly deflated in value under such a system, he wrote, everyone is pushed to play a game of profiting above all else even where that harms the rest of society.[1] Against this backdrop he presented [[gold-backed-brics-reserve-currency|gold-backed currencies]] and the [[gold-standard|gold standard]] as attempts to fix prices and stabilize the measuring power of money by pegging it to something comparatively stable.[1]

## Mechanics as described by Modern Money Mechanics

A February 2023 Badlands Media feature by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] set out the mechanics of the system in detail, drawing on [[modern-money-mechanics|Modern Money Mechanics]], a 1961 workbook by the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago.[2] The article argues that most people believe a bank lends money that another depositor placed with it, when in fact the bank creates new money each time a loan is processed and then lends it out at interest.[2]

The worked example begins with the United States government requesting $10 billion from the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], which in turn requests government bonds; the government issues [[treasury-bond|Treasury bonds]] and the Fed issues [[federal-reserve-note|Federal Reserve Notes]], the two are exchanged, and the notes are deposited, expanding the money supply by $10 billion.[2] Under a 10 percent reserve requirement, $1 billion of that deposit is held and $9 billion becomes excess reserve available as the basis for new loans — money that is created instantaneously rather than drawn from the deposit.[2] Repeating the cycle produces $8.1 billion, then $7.29 billion, then $6.561 billion, and so on, until roughly $90 billion has been created from the original $10 billion; for every deposit, about nine times its value can be generated.[2]

Almost none of this occurs physically: bonds, loans and new money are created electronically, and the article states that 97 percent of the money supply has been created through loans while less than 3 percent exists as notes and coins.[2]

## Debt, interest and the impossibility of repayment

Because money enters existence only through lending, the article contends that money *is* debt and that participation in the system amounts to willful debt slavery — a point it supports with Proverbs 22:7 and with [[henry-ford|Henry Ford]]'s remark that a public understanding of the banking system would produce a revolution before morning.[2] [[marriner-eccles|Marriner Eccles]], a former Fed governor, is quoted to the same effect: "If there were no debts in our money system, there wouldn't be any money."[2]

The article identifies interest as the system's most consequential feature. Only the principal is ever created in the money supply, so the money required to pay accrued interest does not exist; the amount owed will always exceed the amount in circulation, meaning the national debt can never be fully repaid and can only grow.[2] The last complete repayment of the national debt occurred in 1835, after [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] shut down the central bank of his era, whereas the present Federal Reserve was installed in 1913.[2] American household debt reached a record $14.6 trillion in the spring of 2021.[2] This perpetual interest obligation, the article argues, drives money upward to the top 10 percent and exposes the absurdity of [[trickle-down-economics|trickle-down economics]].[2]

## Critics and alternatives

Detractors have proposed several replacements. [[irving-fisher|Irving Fisher]] proposed [[full-reserve-banking|full reserve banking]] in 1935; no country requires it today, Iceland has considered it, and Switzerland rejected the [[sovereign-money-initiative|Sovereign Money Initiative]] in a 2018 referendum.[2] [[jesus-huerta-de-soto|Jesús Huerta de Soto]] and [[murray-rothbard|Murray Rothbard]] of the [[austrian-school|Austrian School]] called for fractional reserve banking to be outlawed and criminalized as a form of embezzlement legalized by banker influence over corrupt governments.[2] The [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]] report [[the-chicago-plan-revisited|The Chicago Plan Revisited]] describes the practice as "problematic," and the Kucinich [[need-act|N.E.E.D. Act]] proposed a debt-free government money unit backed by full reserve lending.[2] Others, including the economic historian [[gary-north|Gary North]], favour gold, though the article cautions that gold-based loans are what brought the [[rothschild-family|Rothschilds]] to power.[2]

## Transition to no-reserve banking

On 26 March 2020 the Federal Reserve Board reduced reserve requirement ratios to zero percent, eliminating reserve requirements for all depository institutions.[2] Under that regime a bank receiving a $10 billion deposit may lend — and thereby create — 100 percent of the amount deposited, a shift the article characterizes as the move from fractional reserve to no reserve banking.[2]
