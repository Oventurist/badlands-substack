# Living Constitution

The "Living Constitution," also described as judicial pragmatism, is the constitutional viewpoint that the United States Constitution holds a dynamic meaning that can evolve even when the document is not formally amended.[1] The doctrine has become widely influential in American constitutional jurisprudence.[1]

## Badlands critique

Writing in the Badlands News Brief following the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruling on [[affirmative-action]], [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that living-constitution pragmatism is "one of the most toxic ideas for the true rule of law," on the grounds that a legal system cannot remain consistent if its rules are rewritten at will.[1]

Deschamps grounded the critique in a contract theory of law: all law used by human beings to govern themselves — between two people, within a family, or within a country — is in substance an agreement, and the rules governing agreements therefore determine whether a rule system is valid, effective, and applicable.[1] On this view the central test for whether a rule system applies to a person is consent, imperfectly recognized in modern jurisprudence as personal jurisdiction; the rules binding a Walmart employee do not bind a Post Office employee because consent flows through the employment contract.[1]

From that premise, he argued that the United States already possesses a lawful mechanism for updating its laws — Congress — because a chain of consent runs from the voter to the elected official who consents to a proposed change by voting for a bill.[1] Allowing the Supreme Court to revise law through opinion and decision, he wrote, robs citizens of the right to govern themselves, which he described as the basis of the nation, and amounts to a fraud.[1]

Deschamps illustrated the point with analogies: rewriting constitutional meaning is like changing the rules of baseball because the rules are old, after which the game may no longer be fair or even recognizable as baseball; it is the same unfairness, he argued, that allows [[lia-thomas|Lia Thomas]] to compete against biological women such as [[riley-gaines|Riley Gaines]].[1] Changing law in a way that destroys the spirit or basis of the law, he concluded, is comparable to changing the laws of murder to allow murder.[1]
