# Trump v. United States

Trump v. United States is the 2024 Supreme Court decision on presidential immunity, which Badlands commentators treat as the controlling precedent governing whether former presidents can be compelled to answer for conduct outside their official duties.[1]

## Holding as invoked by Badlands

The nationally binding rule drawn from the case is stated plainly: there is no immunity for unofficial acts.[1] Badlands contributor Ashe in America recalled the oral-argument exchange in which Justice Sonia Sotomayor pressed whether a president could order SEAL Team Six to assassinate a political opponent, and argued that the resulting limits on immunity are directly relevant to later accountability fights.[1]

## Oral argument (April 2024)

Ahead of the ruling, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] prepared to hear what Fox News described as possibly the most closely watched case of the term, involving the highest-profile appellant, after [[donald-trump]] offered a sweeping argument for why he should not face trial for alleged election interference.[2] The high court scheduled arguments for Thursday morning in a case that could determine the former president's personal and political future, with Trump — the presumptive GOP nominee — betting that his constitutional assertions would win a reprieve from the court's 6-3 conservative majority, three of whose members he had himself appointed.[2] The official question presented was whether, and if so to what extent, a former president enjoys presidential immunity from criminal prosecution for conduct alleged to involve official acts during his tenure in office.[2]

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief]], [[ashe-in-america]] argued that historical precedent supports the immunity position only because no one had ever attempted to prosecute a president as she said Democrats were then doing.[2] She framed the outcome as a win-win: a ruling for Trump would neuter the witch hunts, while a ruling against him would place the alleged crimes of Obama, Biden, Bush and Clinton on the table for future legal remedy, since in her view Trump would beat the allegations on their merits regardless, leaving the matter before the high court one of clarity and precedent.[2]

## The ruling (July 1, 2024)

The [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] issued its decision on Monday, July 1, 2024, holding 6-3 that presidents have immunity for "official acts" while punting to the trial court the question of whether [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s alleged conduct in his January 6 case was protected.[3] Chief Justice [[john-roberts|John Roberts]], writing for the majority, held that a former president is entitled to "absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for actions within his conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority," to "presumptive immunity" for other official acts, and to "no immunity for unofficial acts."[3] Roberts further wrote that Trump is "absolutely immune" from prosecution for any conduct in the indictment involving his discussions with Justice Department officials.[3] It fell to U.S. District Judge [[tanya-chutkan|Tanya Chutkan]], overseeing the January 6 case, to determine which of the charged acts were official.[3] Though not a sweeping victory, the decision all but guaranteed the case would not reach trial before the November 2024 election, a trial already postponed indefinitely from earlier in the year.[3]

### Concurrences, dissent, and Badlands reaction

In a concurring opinion, Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] questioned whether Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]], appointed by Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]], had lawful authority to prosecute Trump at all.[3] Justice [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]] wrote a scathing dissent warning that a president who "uses his official powers in any way" would be insulated from criminal prosecution — ordering SEAL Team Six to assassinate a political rival, organizing a military coup, or taking a bribe for a pardon would all be "immune, immune, immune."[3] Justice [[amy-coney-barrett|Amy Coney Barrett]] joined the judgment overall while joining parts of the separate writing.[3] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] dismissed the dissent as "melodramatic screeching," emphasized that the ruling preserves no immunity for unofficial acts, and predicted intensified calls to pack the court, escalating hysteria over Trump's supposed plans for "retribution," and the frustration or termination of the remaining cases against him.[3] Contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the immunity ruling, while the biggest headline, was less structurally important than the same term's [[sec-v-jarkesy|SEC v. Jarkesy]] and [[chevron-deference|Chevron]] decisions.[3]

## Application to the Clintons

The precedent was invoked in January 2026 after [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] declined subpoenas from the [[house-committee-on-oversight-and-accountability|House Oversight Committee]] in its [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] investigation.[1] Ashe in America conceded that Bill Clinton, as a former president, might claim a partial excuse, but argued that his protections end at official acts and that the Epstein-related conduct occurred long after he left office.[1] Hillary Clinton, never having held the presidency, was said to have no immunity claim at all despite her public service.[1]

The commentary paired Trump v. United States with the contempt prosecutions of Steve Bannon and Peter Navarro, who were imprisoned for defying congressional subpoenas, arguing that equal justice would require comparable consequences for the Clintons.[1]

## Anticipation ahead of the June 27 debate

In the days before the ruling, the pending immunity decision loomed over the [[2024-presidential-debate|first debate]] between [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[4] The Hill reported that the question of whether Trump is immune from prosecution for certain acts while president could upend his criminal trials and, more immediately, throw a wrinkle into the prime-time showdown on [[cnn|CNN]], where his legal troubles were expected to be a key point of discussion.[4] Grant Reeher, director of the Campbell Public Affairs Institute at Syracuse University, said that "if there's a decision before then, whoever that decision favors is going to be wanting to draw attention to it," and expected it to be worked into the conversation or posed as a moderator question.[4] The Court had two opportunities to release the opinion before the debate, with opinions scheduled for Wednesday and Thursday mornings, and a third and final opinion day on Friday.[4]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the ruling might come on debate day itself, said she had no instinct which way it would go, and described the Wednesday opinions as depressing enough that she was bracing for disappointment.[4] She argued that if immunity were taken off the table, the "Biden Crime Family" would be squarely on it, and joked that if the opinion came down on debate day she hoped Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] would deliver it, "if only for the social reaction."[4]
