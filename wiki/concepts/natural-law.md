# Natural Law

Natural Law is the body of legal philosophy holding that human beings possess rights and freedoms inherent to their nature, existing prior to and independent of any government or statute. In Badlands Media's legal-analysis series on the American monetary system, Natural Law is presented as the foundational system of law from which the United States itself derives, and as the standard against which later corporate and federal legal arrangements are measured.[1]

## Role in the American founding

The analysis argues that [[declaration-of-independence|the Declaration of Independence]] is explicitly an invocation of Natural Law, because it states that governments are instituted among men and receive their just authority from the consent of the governed.[1] On that reading, Natural Law is "THE system of law invoked in the Declaration as the origin of the United States itself," making popular sovereignty — not statutory grant — the origin of governmental authority in America.[1]

## Consent as the operative principle

The central operative premise of Natural Law in this framework is that inherent freedoms cannot be transferred away without the free consent of the individual. The article quotes Emerich de Vattel's *The Law of Nations or Principles of the Law of Nature* (English translation, 1805): "IT IS EVIDENT FROM THE LAW OF NATURE, THAT ALL MEN BEING NATURALLY FREE AND INDEPENDENT, THEY CANNOT LOSE THOSE BLESSINGS WITHOUT THEIR CONSENT."[1] See [[law-of-nations]] and [[emerich-de-vattel]].

## Conflict with corporate jurisdiction

The article frames a direct tension: if the people are sovereign under Natural Law, how can they simultaneously be bound as debtors under corporate contracts between the federal government and [[federal-reserve|the Federal Reserve]] that they had no part in creating?[1] The proposed resolution turns on the distinction between state citizenship and federal citizenship drawn from [[fourteenth-amendment|the 14th Amendment]], and on the maxim from [[throckmorton-v-united-states]] that fraud vitiates everything — meaning that consent obtained without disclosure is no consent at all under Natural Law.[1]

The concluding argument is that "all is not lost because we never truly consented as a country to let a corporation control our credit," and that Americans remain obligated, as the Declaration commands, to institute systems of governance that genuinely serve the people.[1]

## Transcendent source of rights

A March 14, 2023 Badlands Media essay develops the same premise in explicitly theological terms, arguing that the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]] grounds [[unalienable-rights|unalienable rights]] in God rather than in any Pope, King or human agreement.[2] The essay frames the underlying question as a strict alternative: either authority is given to man from a source outside himself, or it is claimed by him as a matter of his own choosing.[2]

Because every chain of authority requires a starting point, the essay argues, [[secularism|secularism]] necessarily returns that starting point to man, leaving morality relative and rights contingent on continued agreement.[2] For the sake of civilizational stability, it concludes, rights need to come from a source greater than man — a claim it regards as the core of the Declaration and of natural-law reasoning generally.[2]

## Spooner and the science of justice

A Badlands Media essay of 5 October 2025, "On Nations and Natural Law," approaches the subject through the writings of [[lysander-spooner|Lysander Spooner]], whose essay [[natural-law-the-science-of-justice|Natural Law – The Science of Justice]] treats the principle as universal for all time and in all places because it is part of the fabric of Creation as much as light or magnetism, and therefore capable of being studied and discerned like gravity.[3] On that basis Spooner called it the "science of justice," defining it as "the science of mine and thine" — the science of all a man's rights of person and property and of his rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness — and equally "the science of peace."[3]

The essay classes Natural Law as a "second tier" of [[first-principles|First Principles]], a logical extension of the ultimate First Principle of God as Creator, since a law of this kind begs the question of a Law Giver while still functioning as a practical starting point for theories of how human governments should operate.[3] Its two conditions of peace are that each man do what justice requires — pay his debts, return borrowed or stolen property, make reparation for injury — and abstain from what justice forbids, such as theft, robbery, arson and murder.[3] From these follow three fundamental legal rights, framed reciprocally as duties: to be dealt with honestly, not to be intentionally and undeservingly hurt, and to be given what one is due.[3]

Spooner distinguished the legal, which pertains to property rights informed by Natural Law, from the moral, which concerns ethical social relationships adjudicated in the court of public opinion; the essay argues that much of the legal morass of the present day stems from government wading into the moral sphere, citing the [[civil-rights-act|Civil Rights Act]] as its example.[3] Justice being part of nature is immutable, and attempts by any body of men "whether calling themselves governments, or by any other name" to substitute their own will for it are an absurdity, a usurpation and a tyranny.[3]

## Evidence for its existence

The same essay presents Spooner's retroductive argument — the method [[cs-lewis|C. S. Lewis]] later used in *Mere Christianity* — that all cultures throughout all time have possessed some rudimentary understanding of how to live peacefully together, and that children learn the principles of justice before they can name them and independently of instruction.[3] It connects this to the Apostle Paul's claim in Romans 2:14–16 that Gentiles without the law show its work written on their hearts, and notes Spooner's fifteen "If/Then" propositions, of which the first two hold that without a natural principle of justice every historical struggle for justice was for a mere fantasy and there could be no such thing as injustice.[3]

The essay's American framing holds that appeals to Natural Law were common in Colonial political conversation — as imagined at the [[green-dragon-tavern|Green Dragon Tavern]] — and were preserved in the founding documents' references to "the Laws of Nature and of Nature's God" and "certain inalienable rights endowed by our Creator."[3] The lack of explicit provision for natural rights in the original Constitution is said to have prompted the amendments that delineated free speech, the bearing of arms and the free exercise of religion as logical conclusions drawn from Natural Law.[3] The author, host of [[no-treason-podcast|The No Treason Podcast]], attributes the degradation of American liberty in part to a lost understanding of the principle and makes its restoration to political debate his stated goal.[3]
