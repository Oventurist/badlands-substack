# Pressure Campaign for Biden's Withdrawal

The pressure campaign for [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s withdrawal was the coordinated push by Democratic congressional leaders, close friends, and major donors in July 2024 to persuade the sitting president to abandon his re-election bid, which Badlands coverage tracked closely in the days surrounding the [[2024-republican-national-convention|2024 Republican National Convention]].[1]

## July 2024 reporting

Axios reported on 18 July 2024 that several top Democrats privately believed rising pressure from party congressional leaders and close friends would persuade Biden to drop out as soon as that weekend.[1] The 81-year-old president, then self-isolating with COVID, remained publicly dug in but was described as privately resigned to mounting pressure, poor polling, and untenable scrutiny.[1] The blunt private message was that party leaders, friends, and key donors believed he could not win, could not change public perceptions of his age and acuity, and could not deliver congressional majorities.[1] Biden was told that if he stayed in, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] could win in a landslide, wiping away Biden's legacy and Democrats' November hopes.[1]

One of the president's close friends framed the choice starkly: "His choice is to be one of history's heroes, or to be sure of the fact that there'll never be a Biden presidential library."[1] An AP-NORC poll released the previous day, showing nearly two-thirds of Democrats wanted Biden to withdraw, ricocheted through the [[white-house|White House]] and [[congress|Congress]].[1] Axios characterized the effort as "a panic pressure campaign... relentless — and coordinated."[1]

## Obama's role and congressional leaders

The *Washington Post* reported on 18 July 2024 that [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] — who had spoken to Biden only once since the disastrous debate — had told allies that Biden should seriously consider whether to stay in the race, believing his path to victory had greatly diminished while insisting the decision must be Biden's own.[1] A senior House Democratic aide said "it feels the most real it's ever been," and another senior Hill aide put the chances of Biden pulling the plug as "high" with timing "fluid."[1]

Biden publicly insisted he would be the nominee and that only God or a "medical condition" would compel him to bow out, a stance supported by family members including first lady [[jill-biden|Jill Biden]] and son [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]].[1] Privately, however, leaked accounts of his conversations with Senate Majority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]], Speaker Emerita [[nancy-pelosi|Nancy Pelosi]], and House Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] indicated they had delivered hard truths, warning that he would lose the White House and could take congressional majorities down with him.[1]

## Succession question

Axios separately laid out five reasons that Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] was likely to inherit the nomination despite a movement among some Democratic officials and operatives to bypass her or force a contest rather than a coronation, floating names such as [[gretchen-whitmer|Gretchen Whitmer]], [[josh-shapiro|Josh Shapiro]], and [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]].[1] The five reasons were money — the Biden campaign's $91 million war chest as of the June filing could be transferred to Harris but to no other candidate; history — as a Black and Asian American woman she could be the first woman president; expected endorsements from party heavyweights including [[jim-clyburn|Jim Clyburn]]; party unity, with senior Democrats wary of a multi-candidate fight a month before the convention; and legitimacy, since the Biden–Harris ticket won 14 million votes and swept the largely uncontested primaries.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that Biden was "being hunted by his own in full view of the normie hivemind" while the party desperately attempted to crown "the least popular politician of the last three election cycles," Kamala Harris, as his replacement.[1] He situated the episode within the [[narrative-warfare|War of Stories]], arguing that whoever has the better story wins the war and that over 30% of Democrats believed Trump had faked the assassination attempt — an inversion of the conspiracy paradigm.[1]

## Senate Democrats push back (June 2024)

In late June 2024, before the first presidential debate, Senate Democrats expressed exasperation at their inability to stamp out persistent speculation that party leaders had a plan B to replace [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] atop the ticket over concerns about his age and weak polling.[2] The Hill reported that they were tired of hearing about the hand-wringing of Democratic voters and donors and sick of reading about Rube Goldberg-like schemes to shake up the ticket, some of them pushed by Republicans or conservative media.[2] One senator feigned putting a make-believe pistol to their temple when asked about pulling Biden before the August convention or the November general election, and, speaking anonymously, said such stories sounded "juicy" but showed only that pundits had "too much time on their hands," insisting "there's no way in hell that's true."[2] The same senator credited Biden with a small polling bump after a Manhattan jury convicted [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] on 34 felony counts of falsifying business records, citing a national poll putting him at 50 percent.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that "the donkey in the room" was impossible to ignore and that The Hill's Alexander Bolton was doing heavy lifting for Democrats, noting he used the term "Democratic" sixteen times in a way that reinforced the contention that "Democracy is on the Ballot in November."[2] She argued that with voter angst now undeniable, the correct public-relations play was to confront it directly, whereas the takeaway of the piece was that the Democrat establishment knew its voters did not want Biden and simply did not care — a gaslighting response she called fatal.[2] She observed that the media, Bolton aside, was reinforcing voter concerns and floating alternative candidates, which combined with leadership's defiant dismissiveness suggested Democratic leadership had lost control of the operation: unable to replace Biden, unable to pacify the base, and unable to rely on the press for cover.[2]

## Early July 2024: Biden's defiance

In the days immediately after the June 27 debate, the campaign entered an acute phase.[3] On Wednesday, July 3, 2024, a defiant Biden vowed to keep running for reelection, rejecting growing pressure from Democrats to withdraw after a debate performance that raised questions about his readiness to keep campaigning, much less win in November.[3] Two Democratic lawmakers had called on him to exit the race, a leading ally publicly suggested how the party might choose someone else, and senior aides said they believed he might only have a matter of days to show he was up to the challenge before anxiety in the party boiled over.[3]

"Let me say this as clearly as I possibly can as simply and straightforward as I can: I am running … no one's pushing me out," Biden told staffers on a reelection campaign call, adding, "I'm not leaving. I'm in this race to the end and we're going to win."[3] In private conversations he focused on course-correcting from the debate and on the threat he believed [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] posed to the country, scouring for feedback on what went wrong in Atlanta and taking responsibility for his performance.[3]

Major Democratic donors, meanwhile, began planning to go bigger on contributions to House and Senate candidates in a bid to build a Capitol Hill firewall around a second Trump term they viewed as increasingly likely.[3] Axios described this as the beginning of a potential massive political hedge by skittish donors weighing where their dollars could have the most impact, and as an acknowledgment that they saw Biden's chances of besting Trump on November 5 as diminishing.[3]

Reuters reported that Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] was the top alternative to replace Biden should he step aside, according to seven senior sources at the campaign, the [[white-house|White House]] and the [[democratic-national-committee|Democratic National Committee]].[3] Some influential Democrats floated alternatives including [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]], [[gretchen-whitmer|Gretchen Whitmer]] and [[josh-shapiro|Josh Shapiro]], but the sources said sidestepping Harris was wishful thinking and would be nearly impossible.[3] Harris, then 59, would inherit the campaign's money and infrastructure, had the highest name recognition among alternatives, and trailed Trump 42% to 43% in a Reuters/Ipsos poll — within the 3.5-point margin of error.[3]

On the same day, more than twenty Democratic governors met Biden at the White House in person and virtually for over an hour.[3] [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]] of Minnesota said, "We, like many Americans, are worried," while stressing that all the governors and the president agreed a path to victory in November was the top priority and calling the feedback good and the conversation honest.[3] [[wes-moore|Wes Moore]] of Maryland said, "The president is our nominee. The president is our party leader," reporting Biden "was very clear that he's in this to win it"; [[kathy-hochul|Kathy Hochul]] of New York and Newsom also attended, and the group declined to join calls for withdrawal.[3]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] responded by joking that the team might have to throw its "full, memetic support behind Joe Biden this summer," arguing that if the goal is mass awakening and the [[deep-state|Deep State]] is the target, then the most chaotic situation possible for the powers that be is desirable — "let our boy run… and then watch them eat each other."[3]

## Hersh reporting on a 25th Amendment threat

Reporting published by investigative journalist [[seymour-hersh|Seymour Hersh]] in late July 2024 placed former President [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] at the center of the pressure campaign, describing him as "deeply involved" and as the impetus for pushing [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] out of the race.[4] According to a senior Washington official cited by Hersh, Obama telephoned Biden after breakfast on July 20 and said, "Here's the deal. We have Kamala's approval to invoke the [[twenty-fifth-amendment|25th Amendment]]."[4] The call is said to have followed Biden's "incident" in Las Vegas, which outwardly resembled a serious fall with a significant head injury, and Hersh's source described a White House "in complete disarray."[4]

[[nancy-pelosi|Nancy Pelosi]], [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]], and [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] were reported to have been directly involved in the effort.[4] Obama reportedly declined to endorse [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] immediately, though it was understood she would "get the nod," because he "had an agenda and he wanted to seek it through to the end, and he wanted to have control over who would be elected."[4]

[[conservative-treehouse|Conservative Treehouse]] argued that the account "tracks" with every known datapoint leading to Biden's withdrawal announcement and with the long-standing presence of Obama's team inside the Biden administration.[4] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], said the piece laid out both the regime's motives and the stakes behind the orchestrated chaos, and used it to catalogue what she described as the weaponization of the NSA, [[department-of-justice|DOJ]], [[fbi|FBI]], [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]], IRS, and [[cia|CIA]] against political opposition during the Obama years.[4]
