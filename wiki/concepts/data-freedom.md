# Data Freedom

"Data freedom" is the term used by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] for the body of civil-liberties questions surrounding the collection, retention, sale and analysis of personal data by centralized entities.[1] She argues that data freedom is the civil rights issue of our time, but that because so many already-established civil liberties are simultaneously being trampled, it does not receive the attention it deserves.[1]

## Scope

The concept is deliberately broad. Ashe describes a landscape running from "the super computers we hold in our hands" to "the friendly and helpful wire taps in our homes" to the cars that transport us, characterizing the present moment as "the Wild West of data freedom."[1] Governments, corporations and NGOs extract enormous value from user data, and depending on the state of residence there are very few limitations on how they may use it.[1] Asked what counts as data, her answer is "all of it."[1]

Video games are offered as an illustrative case. Citing Patrick Stafford's May 2019 Polygon piece "The dangers of in-game data collection: Can your choices come back to haunt you?", she notes that developers can see charts outlining how a game's players are motivated, how they interact within a game and what choices they make; that information can be used to make better games, but can also be combined with other information to build robust personal profiles, typically used to target advertising, with privacy experts warning it may later be used in ways that cannot now be anticipated.[1] The same mining, distillation, structuring, analysis and visualization occurs for every conversation, transaction and social media post across every sector and nation, producing what data practitioners call "data exhaust" — the stream of intimate ones and zeros individuals leave in their wake.[1]

## Argument

Ashe concedes that data freedom is highly complex and "not as politically sexy as censorship or monopoly grievances," but argues it is more important than either.[1] Her central thought experiment asks the reader to imagine possessing the ability to intimately view humanity on a global scale, interacting in real time with any slice of the data and running predictive modeling at scale, and whether one would then develop a God complex; her answer is yes.[1]

She judges that too few officials with statutory authority are willing to fight for data freedom, ostensibly because they all benefit from data harvesting and its immeasurably valuable outcomes.[1]

## Regulatory examples

Ashe credits California with attempting to tackle the issue as early as 2018 through its consumer privacy legislation, writing that despite her political disagreements, "Cali is prioritizing data privacy better than any state in the nation."[1] She also welcomed Texas Attorney General [[ken-paxton|Ken Paxton]]'s June 2024 investigation into car manufacturers' collection and sale of driver data, brought under the [[texas-deceptive-trade-practices-act|Texas Deceptive Trade Practices – Consumer Protection Act]], as an example of an official acting on the issue, closing with the line "'Alexa, remind me to move to Texas.'"[1]
