# Our Job in the Pacific

*Our Job in the Pacific* is a 1944 book by [[henry-wallace|Henry A. Wallace]], vice president of the United States from 1941 to 1945, setting out a vision of the post-war world that included Asia as a full partner.[1]

## Contents

Wallace dated the march of the peoples of the East to 1911, "when the revolutionary movement among the Chinese people, inspired by the teachings of [[sun-yat-sen|Sun Yat-sen]], overthrew the Manchu dynasty and established a republic," calling it the first time an Asiatic people turned its back on monarchy and hereditary rule and set out toward "government of the people, by the people and for the people."[1]

Having helped initiate thousands of American water, energy, agriculture and transportation projects under [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin D. Roosevelt]]'s New Deal, Wallace held that any durable international order must be centered on large-scale national development projects that eradicated poverty and elevated minds.[1] Against Wall Street monetarists who called spending on poor colonial countries an inflationary waste, he argued that investments directed to large-scale agro-industrial endeavours on sufficiently long repayment horizons carried no risk, writing that "we need to look forward to the kind of world we shall be living in twenty years from now."[1]

Citing Sun Yat-sen, Wallace argued the task could be accomplished neither by communism nor capitalism alone but by a higher synthesis yet to be created, expecting "more than one mechanism… to serve as a gear between the capitalism of America and Britain, the socialism of Russia, and the mixed state and private enterprise which we can expect to see develop in countries like China," possibly including an international government bank.[1]

He held that stability required a core alliance of Russia, China and America, writing that peaceful relations among the three were vital to each, and rejecting zero-sum thinking: "We cannot have prosperity for ourselves alone. We cannot sell unless others can buy."[1] The pamphlet closes by urging a Pacific policy willing "to associate with others in minding the world community's common business" while avoiding interference in other people's private business.[1]

## Warning on fascism

Wallace warned in 1944 that if his program for an international New Deal were rejected, "fascism in the postwar inevitably will push steadily for Anglo-Saxon imperialism and eventually for war with Russia," noting that American fascists were already using the prospect as an excuse for internal hatreds toward certain races, creeds and classes.[1]
