# Electoral College

The Electoral College is the constitutional mechanism by which the President of the United States is chosen, allocating electors to states rather than awarding the presidency directly to the winner of the national popular vote. Because of this structure, the popular-vote winner has lost the White House in five American elections, the first three of them in the nineteenth century.[1]

## Twenty-first century disputes

The two modern cases most cited by Democrats are the 2000 and 2016 races, though commentators distinguish between them: Al Gore lost to George W. Bush while carrying the popular vote by roughly half a percentage point — a virtual tie — whereas [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] won the presidency in 2016 despite finishing a full two points and about three million votes behind Hillary Clinton.[1] Four years later, [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] defeated Trump by more than four points and seven million votes nationally, but his Electoral College majority rested on a series of narrow victories in a handful of decisive states.[1]

Those two cycles hardened a widely held view that the Electoral College carries a structural pro-Republican bias. With millions of Democratic votes "wasted" in large safe states such as California and New York, analysts argued that a Democratic nominee needed a national popular-vote plurality above roughly three percentage points to be confident of an Electoral College majority.[1]

## The 2024 abolition debate

In the closing week of the 2024 campaign, [[politico|Politico]] published a column arguing for finally abolishing the Electoral College, noting that the New York Times–Siena College poll and numerous other national surveys showed the presidential race a dead heat, with battleground-state polling either tied or well within the margin of error.[1] The column raised the prospect of an unprecedented outcome — a Republican winning the popular vote while losing the Electoral College — and suggested such a result might restore a once-bipartisan consensus in favor of letting voters directly decide the presidency.[1]

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the piece as a coordinated pre-emptive narrative, noting that MSNBC's [[joy-reid|Joy Reid]] had made similar arguments days earlier, and framed the timing as an admission that Trump was positioned to win the popular vote.[1] Her counter-argument was structural rather than partisan: without the Electoral College, the United States would cease to function as a representative republic, since presidential outcomes would be dictated by a handful of population centers. She urged readers to "hold the line" against the coming push.[1]
