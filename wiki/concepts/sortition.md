# Sortition

Sortition is the selection of public officials by random lottery from a pool of candidates rather than by election. Invented in ancient Athens, where many government officials were chosen this way, it survives in modern practice chiefly in the selection of jurors.[1]

## The 2023 revival of the idea

In August 2023, [[new-york-times|The New York Times]] published an opinion essay by [[adam-grant|Adam Grant]] proposing that the United States extend sortition to mayors, governors, legislators, justices and even presidents.[1] The argument rested on the observation that trust in government was near historic lows and on experiments by the psychologist Alexander Haslam showing that groups made smarter decisions under randomly chosen leaders than under elected or merit-selected ones, because random selection produces more democratic leadership and avoids the tendency of anointed leaders to "assert their personal superiority."[1] Randomly chosen leaders, on this account, feel responsibility rather than power: "I did nothing to earn this, so I need to make sure I represent the group well."[1]

## Criticism from Badlands

Badlands treated the piece as a trial balloon for abolishing elections. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted the essay's original headline — "Elections are Bad for Democracy" — and argued that a lottery would simply relocate the corruption, since the people administering a lottery would be no less self-interested than candidates, and those willing to steal elections would rig a lottery.[1] The decisive objection was the vetting step: Grant conceded candidates would be screened to "improve our odds of avoiding the worst candidates in the first place," but never said who screens them, which the critique framed as the end of representative government and of consent as a condition of legitimate governance.[1] The commentary further faulted the citation of national division as a rationale, given the role Badlands assigns the Times in producing that division, and proposed watching whether the idea propagated across other outlets — going [[operation-mockingbird|mockingbird]] — as the measure of how seriously it was intended.[1]
