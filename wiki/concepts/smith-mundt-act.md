# Smith-Mundt Act

The Smith-Mundt Act is a United States statute governing the government's production and distribution of information intended for foreign audiences. As described in coverage aggregated by the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], the law was "originally developed to regulate programs broadcasted to foreign audiences while prohibiting the dissemination of such materials on U.S. soil."[1] In its original form the act therefore functioned as a firewall: state-produced messaging could be aimed outward at foreign publics, but was barred from being circulated domestically to Americans.[1]

## The Benz critique

Former U.S. Department of State official [[mike-benz|Mike Benz]] brought renewed attention to the statute during an appearance on [[joe-rogan|Joe Rogan]]'s podcast, where he argued that former President [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] repealed the act.[1] Benz contended that the repeal "allowed the use of propaganda against Americans, including infiltration of U.S. universities, unions, media, politicians, and judges through covert means."[1] In his own words: "So the Smith–Mundt Act was always designed to say, listen, you can do this dirty stuff abroad, but it can't come home. We had that protection, which lasted for 70 years, and we only lost it a decade ago [during Obama's administration]."[1]

The relevant change is generally identified as the Smith-Mundt Modernization Act, which loosened the domestic-dissemination bar. Benz's framing treats that modernization as the moment at which the machinery of foreign influence operations was legally permitted to be turned inward on the American public.[1]

## Reaction and repeal campaign

The podcast segment was clipped and circulated on X by the account Wall Street Apes, and [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] — Tesla chief executive and then head of the U.S. Department of Government Efficiency — reposted the video with the caption "Restore the Smith-Mundt act!"[1] The episode illustrated how a technical media-law question migrated into mainstream political discourse through podcast and social-media amplification rather than through legislative debate.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] was skeptical of the restoration campaign, writing: "If you think that repealing the Smith-Mundt Modernization Act is going to stop government propaganda, you have consumed too much government propaganda."[1] The remark reflects a recurring Badlands position that statutory firewalls are weak constraints on agencies that operate through cutouts, contractors and non-governmental organizations regardless of what the text permits.[1]

## Invoked in the 2025 Politico funding controversy

During the February 2025 controversy over federal payments to [[politico|Politico]], Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] predicted that the scandal would force mainstream audiences into a reckoning with the Smith-Mundt Modernization Act of 2013, arguing that "the only thing more scandalous than State-run propaganda is the fact that this has all been quite legal."[2] He framed the issue as structural rather than personal — "It's not the players. It's the game." — and connected [[usaid|USAID]] funding of the U.S. media industry to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s earlier claim that adversaries were waging "illegal psychological warfare operations on the American people."[2] While allowing that Smith-Mundt might render such arrangements legal, he denied that they were constitutional, summarizing the sequence as "Exposure > Disclosure > Justice."[2]

## Statutory history and the Obama repeal

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] set out the act's lineage in December 2024, noting that the Smith-Mundt Act of 1948 was passed by [[congress|Congress]] just one year after President Harry Truman created the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]] through the National Security Act of 1947.[3] In his account, Smith-Mundt was designed to protect the American public from the psychological warfare operations the CIA had been created to deploy — though he added that he did not personally believe such a statute would have stopped Langley in any case.[3] In 2012, he wrote, President [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] effectively repealed Smith-Mundt, "giving the CIA the full authority to impose its ruthless will upon the unsuspecting American People," with results he described as devastating.[3]

He raised the argument in the context of Niger's suspension of the [[bbc|BBC]], contending that state broadcasters of that kind are not news organizations but "highly funded and highly sophisticated propaganda disseminators" capable of destabilizing societies and toppling governments, and that they are at minimum heavily influenced by intelligence agencies given journalists' routine reliance on anonymous officials "familiar with the situation."[3]
