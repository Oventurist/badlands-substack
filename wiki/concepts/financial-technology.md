# Financial Technology (FinTech)

Financial technology, commonly shortened to FinTech, is the term used for new technology that seeks to improve and automate the delivery and use of financial services.[1] As summarized in an Investopedia definition quoted by Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]], fintech at its core helps companies, business owners and consumers better manage their financial operations, processes and lives through specialized software and algorithms running on computers and, increasingly, smartphones.[1]

## From back end to consumer

When fintech emerged in the 21st century, the term applied chiefly to technology used in the back-end systems of established financial institutions; since then there has been a shift toward more consumer-oriented services and a correspondingly consumer-oriented definition.[1] Lange argues that fintech was first used to streamline and cut costs at the back end of financial institutions and now leads in providing front-end technology that serves customers directly, making their lives easier and giving them more control by creating competition for the big legacy banks.[1] He attributes the shift to the movement from the internet revolution into the smartphone revolution, with ever more people conducting personal banking on their phones.[1]

## Scope of services

Fintech now describes activities such as money transfers, depositing a check with a smartphone, bypassing a bank branch to apply for credit, raising money for a business startup, and managing investments, generally without the assistance of a person.[1] Citing EY's 2017 Fintech Adoption Index, Lange notes that one-third of consumers use at least two or more fintech services and that these consumers are increasingly aware of fintech as part of daily life — a number he expects to grow rapidly.[1] Because fintech lowers operating costs and increases efficiency, he argues, it ultimately produces better services for customers.[1]

## Significance for banking power

Digital banks and financial services are described as disrupting the control that [[central-banking|central banks]] have exercised for generations, offering convenience, competitive rates and often no fees.[1] These firms bypass legacy banks and also compete with credit card companies, notably through [[peer-to-peer-payments|peer-to-peer payment services]].[1] Advancements in fintech such as digital transactions are also credited with making [[financial-inclusion|financial inclusion]] easier to achieve.[1] Lange positions [[block-inc|Block]], [[cash-app|Cash App]], [[paypal|PayPal]] and the payment ambitions of [[twitter|Twitter]] as leading examples of this fintech-driven [[decentralization|decentralization]] of finance.[1]
