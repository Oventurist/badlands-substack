# Smart Homes

A smart home is a residence in which Internet of Things (IoT) devices — appliances, cameras, speakers, sensors and their companion mobile apps — are networked together. The concept has existed considerably longer than [[smart-clothing|smart clothing]], but Badlands Media argues it merits renewed attention in light of recent findings by international researchers.[1]

## The IMDEA–Northeastern study

Scientists at [[imdea-networks|IMDEA Networks]] and [[northeastern-university|Northeastern University]] demonstrated a variety of security and privacy threats arising from the local network interactions of IoT devices and mobile apps. Their comprehensive study, "In the Room Where It Happens: Characterizing Local Communication and Threats in Smart Homes," was presented at the ACM Internet Measurement Conference in Montreal and examined local network interactions among 93 different IoT devices and mobile apps.[1]

David Choffnes, associate professor of computer science and executive director of the Cybersecurity and Privacy Institute at Northeastern University, said in a media release: "When we think of what happens between the walls of our homes, we think of it as a trusted, private place. In reality, we find that smart devices in our homes are piercing that veil of trust and privacy — in ways that allow nearly any company to learn what devices are in your home, to know when you are home, and learn where your home is." He added that these behaviors are generally not disclosed to consumers and that better protections are needed in the home.[1]

## Findings

Contrary to the widespread belief that local networks are secure spaces, the research identified risks from IoT devices unintentionally exposing sensitive data within local networks using common protocols such as UPnP or mDNS. Exposed data included Universally Unique Identifiers (UUIDs), unique device names and even household locations — information that companies engaged in surveillance capitalism can readily exploit. The study emphasized that local network protocols can act as backdoors to obtain information, such as home location, that is ostensibly protected by mobile app permissions.[1]

Study co-author Vijay Prakash, a PhD student at the [[new-york-university|New York University]] Tandon School of Engineering, explained that analysis of data collected by IoT Inspector found evidence of IoT devices inadvertently exposing at least one piece of personally identifiable information — a unique hardware (MAC) address, UUID or unique device name — in thousands of real-world smart homes. He noted that any single identifier is useful for identifying a household, but combining all three makes a home highly unique: whereas the simplest browser fingerprinting technique makes a person as unique as one in 1,500 people, a smart home fingerprinted with all three identifier types is as unique as one in 1.12 million smart homes.[1]

## Consequences

The article stresses that the implications extend beyond academia, requiring decisive action from manufacturers, software developers, IoT and mobile platform operators, and regulators to improve smart home security and privacy. Researchers responsibly notified vulnerable IoT device vendors and Google's Android Security Team, which led to security updates for some of the affected products.[1]

Badlands situates smart homes alongside smart clothing and [[digital-identification|digital identification]] as components of an advancing [[technocracy|technocratic]] surveillance architecture, noting that homeowners rarely got a say when smart meters were installed or 5G towers erected in their neighborhoods.[1]
