# Maine Ballot Disqualification of Donald Trump

The Maine ballot disqualification was the December 2023 administrative decision by Maine Secretary of State [[shenna-bellows|Shenna Bellows]] to strike [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from [[maine|Maine]]'s 2024 presidential primary ballot, invoking the disqualification clause of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]].[1]

## Mechanism and criticism

The decision was made unilaterally by a single state officer rather than through criminal conviction or adversarial adjudication, which critics identified as its defining defect: removing candidates "based on unilateral opinion rather than any kind of legally arbitrated decision or criminal conviction is the most slippery of slopes."[1] The operative danger, according to that critique, is that officials need only allege criminal behavior or criminal intent and then act on the allegation alone, effectively applying a "guilty until proven innocent" standard to ballot access.[1]

Commentary framed the tactic as ideologically asymmetric — "a decidedly leftist/Democrat affair clearly engineered to benefit the progressive power structure for many years to come" — and warned that it was "not only about Donald Trump," since the same instrument could later be used to deny ballot access to any conservative candidate said to "might" represent a "threat to Democracy."[1]

## Dependence on the Colorado insurrection finding

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the Maine action was downstream of the [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|Colorado litigation]]. Legal scholars had dismissed the Colorado case as inconsequential on the assumption that no court would ultimately disenfranchise tens of millions of voters, and indeed Trump remained on Colorado's primary ballot.[1] The lasting product of the case, on this analysis, was the trial court's finding of fact that January 6 was an insurrection, upheld by the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] — a finding that supplied Bellows the predicate she needed to act.[1]

For that reason the argument was made that the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] must rule on the underlying findings when the Colorado GOP and Trump appeals in Anderson v. Griswold reach it, and that ruling narrowly on the Fourteenth Amendment question would be "unacceptable," because the dispute "has never been about ballot access" but about using progressive courts to punish political opponents and neutralize dissent.[1]

## Appeal timeline

Trump's campaign announced an immediate appeal to the Maine Superior Court, with statute requiring a decision by January 17, 2024 to affirm or overturn the disqualification — a date treated by Badlands commentators as significant.[1]

Writing on January 10, 2024, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] summarized the end-of-month calendar as "a flurry of deadlines in Maine": January 17 was the last day for the Maine Superior Court to issue a written decision on any specific motion, any such decision could be appealed by January 20, and January 31 was the deadline for the court's final decision on Trump's ballot access in the Pine Tree State.[3] She placed the Maine schedule alongside the parallel Oregon Section 3 challenge, in which parties had that week filed supplemental briefing on plaintiffs' standing and on the Oregon Secretary of State's authority to remove Trump from the ballot, and characterized the multistate ballot challenges as "overt, direct election interference by uniparty government officials and progressive lawfare NGOs."[3]

## Judicial deferral and dismissal of the appeal (January 2024)

In January 2024, Maine Superior Court Justice [[michaela-murphy|Michaela Murphy]] deferred judgment on review of Secretary of State [[shenna-bellows|Shenna Bellows]]'s disqualification decision, citing the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]]'s upcoming February 8 argument over the Colorado case.[2] Bellows appealed the deferral, but the Maine Supreme Court declined to take the matter, observing that Murphy had only deferred and had not ruled, and holding that "because the appeal is not from a final judgment, we dismiss the appeal as interlocutory and not justiciable."[2]

Legal scholar [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] summarized the outcome as "the Maine event will not happen," and noted that with Massachusetts also ruling against disqualification that week, Colorado remained the only state whose supreme court had adopted the theory.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the whole disqualification effort was aimed at legitimizing the insurrection narrative advanced by the [[january-6-committee|J6 Committee]], and that the decisive question before the Supreme Court was whether January 6 constituted an insurrection at all.[2]
