# Presidential Records Act

The Presidential Records Act (PRA) is the U.S. statute governing the ownership and custody of the records of presidents and vice presidents, requiring that presidential records be turned over to the [[national-archives-and-records-administration|National Archives]] rather than retained as personal property.[1]

## 2023 classified documents sweep

In January 2023 the National Archives formally asked former presidents and vice presidents to re-check their personal records for any classified documents or other presidential records, following the discovery over the preceding year of classified documents at the homes of former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], former Vice President [[mike-pence|Mike Pence]] and President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[1]

The Archives sent a letter on Thursday, January 26, 2023 to representatives of former presidents and vice presidents from the last six presidential administrations covered by the Presidential Records Act — from Ronald Reagan's White House to the present.[1] The letter requested that they check their files to ensure that material thought to be personal does not "inadvertently" contain presidential records that are required by law to be turned over to the Archives.[1]

Representatives for four former presidents had previously stated that they held no classified records: the representatives for Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Barack Obama and the late George H. W. Bush all said that all classified records had been turned over to the National Archives upon leaving office.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands Media contributor [[just-human|Just Human]] observed that because NARA had written to every president and vice president from Reagan through Biden, all of them were now "on the record denying having any classified records," and predicted that such claims would prove "erroneous at best."[1] He characterized the control of classified documents as "a long time method of hiding abuses and crimes by those in high office."[1] The statute is a companion to the [[federal-records-act|Federal Records Act]].

## Invoked in the Trump documents case (2023)

After [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s June 2023 arraignment in the [[trump-classified-documents-case|classified documents case]], Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that the Presidential Records Act supplies special privileges and protections to those who have served as president, and that it supersedes the [[espionage-act|Espionage Act]] under which Trump was charged.[2] On that reading, the volume of material a former president takes with him on leaving the [[white-house|White House]] is legally immaterial — "whether it was a single postcard or a million documents ... he is allowed to do so."[2]

DeLarme contrasted that protection with the position of [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], who as vice president would enjoy no comparable shield and who stood accused of pay-to-play and bribery schemes as well as of using the [[fbi|FBI]] to cover them up.[2]

## Origins and text of the 1978 statute

The Presidential Records Act of 1978, codified at 44 U.S.C. §§2201-2209, governs the official records of Presidents and Vice Presidents created or received after January 20, 1981 — that is, beginning with the Reagan administration.[4] The PRA changed the legal ownership of the official records of the President from private to public and established a new statutory structure under which Presidents, and subsequently NARA, must manage the records of their administrations.[4] It establishes public ownership of all presidential records, defines the term "presidential records," and requires that vice-presidential records be treated in the same way as presidential records.[4]

Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] treats the law as a landmark transfer of power to the citizenry, arguing that because all official documents — even classified ones — legally belong to the people, the PRA and the [[freedom-of-information-act|Freedom of Information Act]] together form the legal basis on which past presidential crimes can be exposed.[4] He cites the statute as evidence that [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]] was not the worst president but was instead working, without much help, to expose and hamper the [[shadow-government|shadow government]].[4] Lange also argues that the equal treatment of vice-presidential records explains why [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] retained classified documents on leaving the vice presidency.[4]

## The 2014 amendment

The PRA was amended in 2014 during the presidency of [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]].[4] The amendment requires the [[archivist-of-the-united-states|Archivist of the United States]], upon determining to make publicly available any presidential record not previously released, to promptly provide written notice of that determination to the former President during whose term the record was created, to the incumbent President, and to the public, and to make the record available to the public within 60 days — except for any record as to which the Archivist receives notification from a former or incumbent President of a claim of constitutionally-based privilege against disclosure.[4] That claim of [[executive-privilege|executive privilege]] can be overridden in only two ways: the incumbent President withdraws a decision upholding the claim, or the Archivist is directed to release by a final court order not subject to appeal.[4]

Section 10 of the same legislation prohibits an officer or employee of an executive agency from creating or sending a record using a non-official electronic messaging account unless the officer copies an official account in the original transmission or forwards a complete copy to an official account within 20 days.[4] Section 9 transfers responsibility for records management from the Administrator of the General Services Administration to the Archivist and requires the transfer of records from federal agencies to the National Archives in digital or electronic form to the greatest extent possible.[4]

Lange reads both provisions as self-inflicted wounds. He argues the non-official messaging clause implicates Obama and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]], who used a private server to transmit emails and did not back up those communications as the law Obama signed required, and he asserts that military intelligence captures all electronic communications regardless.[4] The transfer of records management to the Archivist, he contends, centralized control of all government records in one office — a mistake, in his telling, once that office was held by [[david-ferriero|David Ferriero]] through the entire [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration.[4] He further notes that part of the law prevents documents from being released until five years after a president leaves office, while maintaining that a sitting president's sole authority to declassify overrides that delay.[4]

## Argued to supersede the Espionage Act (June 2023)

In "Smoke and Mirrors," published June 16, 2023, [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] treated the question of whether the Presidential Records Act of 1978 or the [[espionage-act|Espionage Act]] governs a former president's retention of records as the deciding factor in the [[2023-trump-classified-documents-indictment|classified documents case]] against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[5] He characterised the DOJ's failure to address the point as its "biggest oopsie," and one that could not plausibly have been overlooked.[5] Unlike [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]], he argued, Trump enjoys special privileges and protections for having served as president, so that whether he removed "a single postcard or a million documents" from the [[white-house|White House]] is legally immaterial — a latitude he compared to [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]], said to still be working through some three million pages of documents.[5]

DeLarme cited [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]] for the statutory-construction argument that if [[congress|Congress]] had wanted the Espionage Act to supersede the Presidential Records Act it would have written the law differently: the later law supersedes the earlier unless Congress says otherwise, which it has not.[5] He concluded that if the case were handed to an unbiased artificial intelligence reviewing the evidence against the entire database of U.S. court records, it would never reach a jury.[5]
