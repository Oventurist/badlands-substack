# Win-Win Cooperation

"Win-win cooperation" is the moral principle placed at the heart of open-system political economy in [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s account of the contest between [[open-systems-vs-closed-systems|open and closed systems]].[1] It holds that relations between nations should be structured so that each party gains from the development of the other, in contrast to zero-sum or "might-makes-right" frameworks in which one nation's advance requires another's loss.[1]

## Origins and formulations

Ehret traces the principle to [[sun-yat-sen|Sun Yat-sen]], who in his Three Principles of the People called it the principle of "Right makes Might."[1] Sun understood in 1924, as Presidents [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] and [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] are said to today, that a nation adhering to win-win / right-makes-might thinking will never lose the Mandate of Heaven, or Tianxia.[1]

In the Western tradition the same idea is expressed through the [[peace-of-westphalia|Principle of Westphalia]], which in 1648 founded the first modern nation states on the principle of the "Benefit of the Other."[1] Ehret argues that when [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]], [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]] or [[tony-blair|Tony Blair]] invoke a "post-Westphalian age," their target is this moral principle rather than the mere existence of borders.[1] The UN Charter, drafted under the anti-colonial President [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin D. Roosevelt]], is presented as a further codification of the same outlook, committing member states to international cooperation on economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems.[1]

## Application

Win-win cooperation is described as the operating principle of China's [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]], which Ehret says has won over 135 nations to a framework that presumes no fixed limit to resources or endpoint to national progress.[1] He argues that where nations are empowered to stand on their own feet, build full-spectrum agro-industrial economies, and encourage rather than sabotage new technologies and scientific discovery, human potential is bounded only by the capacity to discover, create, plan and inspire future generations.[1] Concrete arenas identified for applying the principle include space diplomacy, asteroid defense, Arctic and Far East development, and nuclear energy.[1]
