# 2025 North Carolina Redistricting

The 2025 North Carolina redistricting was a rapidly advancing mid-decade congressional map redraw by the state's Republican legislature, undertaken at a time when President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was encouraging states to redistrict so that Republicans could hold Congress after the [[2026-midterm-elections|2026 midterm elections]].[1]

## Partisan, not racial, defense

Republicans denied accusations that the map diluted the Black vote, asserting that their motivations were purely political.[1] State senator [[ralph-hise|Ralph Hise]] (R-Mitchell) claimed credit for drawing the new map in hearings on October 20, 2025, saying its purpose was "simple and singular — draw a new map that will bring an additional Republican seat."[1] The distinction matters legally: partisan gerrymandering has been treated by federal courts as largely non-justiciable, while racial vote dilution remains actionable.[1]

## The 1st congressional district

The map's principal target was the 1st congressional district in northeastern North Carolina, a historically Black area that has elected Black representatives since 1992 and Democratic ones for a century before that.[1] As of October 2025 it was the state's only remaining swing district, represented by Democrat [[don-davis|Don Davis]].[1]

The North Carolina fight ran parallel to litigation in Utah, where 3rd District Judge [[dianna-gibson|Dianna Gibson]] faced a November 10 deadline to choose a congressional map amid dueling expert filings and gerrymandering accusations from both the legislature and plaintiffs.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands host [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] used the moment to criticize the Supreme Court's approach to redistricting, arguing after listening to oral arguments that the Court "doesn't interpret the Constitution — it rewrites it," and that when justices say "constitutional" they mean whether a measure comports with how their government operates.[1] He objected that statutes, regulations and precedent are folded into the Constitution "because a court once said so," cited Justice [[elena-kagan|Elena Kagan]]'s reference to "all the law this court has ever made," and argued the arguments rest entirely on the Reconstruction Amendments, which he characterized as a post–Civil War inversion replacing a republic of liberty and individual rights with a democracy for the good of the state.[1]
