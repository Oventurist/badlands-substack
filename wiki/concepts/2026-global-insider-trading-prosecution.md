# 2026 Global Insider Trading Prosecution

The 2026 global insider trading prosecution is a [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] case in which charges were unsealed against thirty defendants in connection with a large-scale, decade-long insider trading scheme that allegedly netted tens of millions of dollars in illicit profits.[1]

## Allegations

The defendants, who include corporate attorneys and other financial professionals, are alleged to have stolen and used confidential information on nearly 30 merger and acquisition deals taken from several of the nation's premier law firms, including a firm headquartered in Massachusetts.[1] Nineteen defendants were arrested and were to appear in federal court in Los Angeles, Fort Lauderdale, and New York, among other locations; two defendants located in [[russia|Russia]] and [[israel|Israel]] are considered fugitives.[1]

A first indictment charges sixteen defendants with two counts of conspiracy to commit securities fraud, two counts of securities fraud, and one count of money laundering conspiracy. A second indictment charges five defendants with the same counts. Charges against nine other defendants for securities fraud conspiracy were also unsealed, and the government's investigation remains ongoing.[1]

U.S. Attorney [[leah-foley|Leah B. Foley]] said the country's financial markets and professional firms "should be free from the rampant fraud and breaches of duty that these charges allege."[1]

## Commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the case marks the entry of professional services firms into the global corruption story, noting that modern M&A due diligence gives a wide circle access to actionable inside information — Big Four accounting firms, strategy consultancies, cyber diligence firms, investment banks, ESG and compliance consultants, insurance diligence providers, HR and payroll vendors, and data room administrators.[1] Because dozens or hundreds of outside professionals can access virtual data rooms before a deal is announced, and because the indictment alleges at least 30 affected deals, she contended that the firms themselves must bear liability and that a negligence explanation is implausible at that scale.[1] She also noted that the indictments do not name the law firms, that the grand jury discusses "known and unknown" coconspirators, and that the DOJ's phrase "corporate attorneys and other financial professionals" leaves open an extension beyond law firms.[1]

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] focused on the defendants' identities, citing [[jewish-news-syndicate|Jewish News Syndicate]] coverage that framed the indicted group as Jewish and alleged use of "going to Israel" as coded language for illegal sales, along with references to defendants [[god-izraelov|God Izraelov]] and [[david-bratslavsky|David Bratslavsky]].[1] He speculated about whether [[jonathan-greenblatt|Jonathan Greenblatt]] and the [[anti-defamation-league|Anti-Defamation League]] would accuse the Trump DOJ of fomenting antisemitism through the indictments.[1] These are the commentator's interpretations rather than claims contained in the charging documents.[1]
