# January 5, 2021 Pipe Bombs

On January 5, 2021 — the eve of the congressional certification of the electoral college vote — pipe bombs were placed at the headquarters of the Democratic National Committee and the Republican National Committee in Washington, D.C. Although the suspect was captured on video, the case produced no publicly known arrest, in contrast with the aggressive prosecution of nearly everyone else connected to the events of January 6.[1]

## Congressional pressure on the FBI

[[fbi|FBI]] Director [[christopher-wray|Christopher Wray]] told Fox News anchor Bret Baier in early 2023 that the bureau was working hard on the case but declined to discuss informants or agents who may have been involved on January 6.[1] In May 2023, [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] chairman [[jim-jordan|Jim Jordan]], Representative [[andy-biggs|Andy Biggs]] — head of the Subcommittee on Crime and Federal Government Surveillance — and Representative [[bill-posey|Bill Posey]] wrote to Wray outlining a record of unanswered requests: Posey first sought a briefing on September 2, 2021, a formal request followed on March 9, 2022, and another was made at the start of the new Congress on January 17, 2023.[1] The lawmakers said the FBI had failed to comply for more than a year and a half and disclosed that a whistleblower had raised questions about the "unusual" nature of the bureau's investigation.[1]

## Commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing in the [[badlands-media|Badlands]] News Brief, argued that the substance of the disclosure was that the FBI already knows the pipe bomber's identity and has withheld it, noting that investigators tracked the suspect's metro card and that Washington is comprehensively surveilled.[1]

She contended that the January 5 bombs are essential to understanding the confrontation between rallygoers and police on January 6: bomb threats against both major parties on the eve of certification placed law enforcement in a highly defensive posture, so that officers engaged the crowds with an expectation of terrorism.[1] In her account, video released over the following two years showed police openly hostile to peaceful rallygoers, and the escalation at the rear of the Capitol was instigated when police attempted to fire toxic gas into the bleachers and accidentally gassed their own officers; the police then retreated from the line, and gassed rallygoers advanced into the abandoned position in a panicked effort to breathe.[1]

Ashe concluded that the withholding of the bomber's identity — like the withholding of Capitol security footage — is itself evidence, reasoning that "the Feds would not withhold the pipe bomber's identity if that identity supported their crumbling narrative."[1]
