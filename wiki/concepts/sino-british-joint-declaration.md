# Sino-British Joint Declaration

The Sino-British Joint Declaration is the agreement governing the return of [[hong-kong|Hong Kong]] to Chinese sovereignty, under which the territory was to retain the high degree of autonomy it had enjoyed under British rule.[1]

## Background

Britain controlled Hong Kong for over one hundred and fifty years — apart from a brief Japanese occupation during the Second World War — and the territory did not fully return to Chinese rule until 1997.[1] Hong Kong had been ceded to Britain after the First Opium War, becoming a British colony or territory, and conversations between the Chinese and the British over its future did not begin until the 1970s and 1980s.[1]

## Terms

The agreement provided that Hong Kong would keep its freedoms and basic rights — things that people in mainland [[china|China]] "could only dream of" — while again belonging to China, and that for 50 years things would remain unchanged.[1] The arrangement was ratified by the [[united-nations|United Nations]] and supported by the United States, and was captured in the slogan "[[one-country-two-systems|One country, two systems]]."[1] Within the 50-year deal it was promised that steps would be taken to ensure universal voting rights for Hong Kong's citizens by 2010.[1]

## Erosion

Badlands Media wrote that the Joint Declaration, meant to protect the freedoms and autonomy of Hong Kong, "has been eroded, broken, and is worth less than the paper it was printed on."[1] When protesters accused Beijing of breaking its promise in 2014, China responded by explaining that, to it, the agreement was no more than a historic document and was worth nothing.[1] Hong Kong was promised 50 years of autonomy and received less than half of that.[1] The article noted that the United States and the [[united-kingdom|United Kingdom]], instrumental in drawing up the framework, have been urged by activists such as [[samuel-chu|Samuel Chu]] to return to the table and uphold the promises made.[1]
