# Aztec–German Word Correspondences

The **Aztec–German word correspondences** are a comparative vocabulary list published in part five of [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]]'s [[the-werewolf-game|Werewolf Game]] series at [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], offered as the primary evidence for the [[ancient-german-language-thesis|ancient German language thesis]] — the claim that all the peoples of the world once spoke ancient German.[1] The list is drawn from [[frederick-dodson|Frederick Dodson]]'s book [[extraterrestrial-linguistics|*Extraterrestrial Linguistics*]], which in turn relays research by the German author [[erhard-landmann|Erhard Landmann]]; the author states that he has reproduced only a handful of the pairs Dodson lists.[1]

## Method

Each entry is presented in a fixed format: the Aztec word, its meaning, the ancient German counterpart, and that word's meaning.[1] The author stresses that ancient German is not the same as modern German, which is why readers may not recognise the German forms.[1] He also stresses that exact identity is not required — resemblance in sound, spelling or theme is treated as sufficient, and words whose meanings merely "point in the same direction" are counted as connected.[1]

## Sample entries

Among the pairs listed are *A Wizotla* (wise, old) against *Uizota* ("the wise one"); *Tes Uin* (wine) against *Te Win* (wine); *Te Tilia* (vanity) against *Is Itila* ("is vain"); *Palatenco* (palace) against *Palato* (palace); *Ite Cucuc* (a type of bird) against *Cucuck* (cuckoo); and *Ihi Otema* (to fill something with air) against *Ihi Atemo* ("I breathe").[1]

Further pairs include *Xo Comic Tia* (to be drunk) against *So Comic Tuan* (to act weird); *Uica Tiuh* (to march, to accompany) against *Ueca Zeigon* (to show someone the way); *Teote Machtilli* (religious instruction) against *Teot Machtilih* (mighty God); *O Nech Uel Mati* (not well done) against *O Ne Uola Machti* ("that is not well done"); *Tl Alte Cutli* (God of Earth) against *Te Alt Cot* (the old God); and *Uel* (good) against *Uola* (well, good).[1]

The list continues with *Ton Antzin* ("our mother") against *Tin Ana Sin* (female ancestor); *Tlanque* (long) against *Lang* (long); *Tia Machi Liztli* (skilled, crafty) against *Tia Machoti Lizt* (the art of making); *T Lanque Ce Mila* (long-distance runner) against *Langa Mila* ("the long mile"); *Eo* (everyone) against *Eo* (everyone); *Pohua* (to read) against *Pouhan* (book); and *Ic Notl* (poor person) against *Ic Not* ("is not," glossed as "is in need").[1]

Additional entries cover *Te Neuel Machiti* (healer) against *Niuwa Machoti* (one who "makes new"), which the author connects to the English idiom "good as new"; *Mixca Hui* (mixture) against *Miscan*/*Miskan* (mixture); *Quena Mi* (to know me) against *Chenna Mi* (to know me); *Quenamican* (a person nobody knows) against *quen nami* (no name); *Namen* (names) against *Namo* (names); *Te Owa* (priest) against *Te Ewa* (priest); and *Te Yo Liuht La Machtiana* (master, preacher) against *Te Goliuhta Machonti* ("the one who enlightens").[1]

The remainder of the published list pairs *Ochan* (search) with *Suochan* (search); *Qualitla* (to like, nice) with *Qualitat* (quality); *I Uin Tia* (to get drunk) with *In Uin Tua* (to put into wine); *T Lazi Ui* (to be lazy) with *Lazzan* (lazy); *Ipan*/*Pan* (above, on, over) with *Upan*/*Ufan* (above, on, over); *Xac* (basket) with *Sackil* (sack); *Itz Caltic* (cold) with *Iz Calta* ("it's cold"); *Nic*, *Nicte*, *Nite* (not) with *Nicht* (not); *Ic*, *Ix*, *Iz* (is) with the identical ancient German forms; *Caualatia* (defense, to prevent) with *Caualtia* (violence); *Vel* (many) with *Vil* (many); *Ehe* (wind) with *Wehen* (blowing of wind); *Te Apoa* (opening) with *Te Opanon* (opening); *Qua Lanca Nemi* (to take a long time) with *Lanca Nemi* (to take a long time); and *Nite Ne Machtia* (to warn, to admonish) with *Nit Ne Mach Tiza* ("don't do that!").[1]

## Place and ancestor names

Beyond ordinary vocabulary, the essay offers two proper-name correspondences it treats as especially significant. The Aztec word *Sacatlan*, said to mean "ancient Mexico," is rendered in ancient German as *Saschenland*, glossed as "country" and more specifically as [[saxony|Saxony]].[1] The Aztec phrase *Ani Sazi*, said to mean "ancient ancestors who built the cities," is rendered as *Ahnen Sachen*, "ancestors from Saxony."[1] The author pairs these with the observation that [[montezuma|Montezuma]] is portrayed as having red hair, as were the [[koryos|Koryos]] werewolf cult, and asks whether the Saxons were an ancient counterbalance to the Koryos, a related group, or both.[1]

## Argument from accumulation

The rhetorical structure of the list is cumulative rather than case-by-case: the author twice asks how many coincidences it takes before something ceases to be a coincidence, echoing a question posed by [[qanon|Q]].[1] He argues that it is far less plausible that Aztec and ancient German share this much vocabulary by accident than that the two peoples had contact during a period of history that has been obscured from view.[1] Readers wishing to pursue the material further are directed to Dodson's *Levels of Heaven and Hell* and *Extraterrestrial Linguistics*.[1]
