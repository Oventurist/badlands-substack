# London Economic Conference

The **London Economic Conference** of 1933 was a monetary and trade conference organized by the [[league-of-nations|League of Nations]] under the guidance of the [[bank-for-international-settlements|Bank for International Settlements]] and the [[bank-of-england|Bank of England]], which Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes as an attempt to impose a central bankers' dictatorship on the world.[1]

Begun in December 1932 and sold as the only way "to stabilize the world economy," the conference gathered 62 to 64 nations in a controlled environment chaired by the British Prime Minister and opened by the King himself.[1] Its Monetary Committee passed a resolution holding it essential "that independent Central Banks, with requisite powers and freedom to carry out an appropriate currency and credit policy, should be created in such developed countries as have not at present an adequate central banking institution," and reaffirming "the great utility of close and continuous cooperation between Central Banks," with the BIS to act "as an instrument for common action."[1] Echoing what the article calls the Bank of England's fixation with mathematical equilibrium, the resolutions justified a new central-bank-controlled gold standard as needed "to maintain a fundamental equilibrium in the balance of payments."[1] The underlying idea, in Ehret's reading, was to deprive nation states of the power to generate and direct credit for their own development — a process he compares to the Great Reset agenda.[1]

The conference collapsed because [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]] refused American cooperation.[1] Its resistance had already been cleared in Germany by the soft coup that ousted Chancellor [[kurt-von-schleicher|Kurt von Schleicher]] in favor of [[adolf-hitler|Adolf Hitler]] under [[hjalmar-schacht|Hjalmar Schacht]] in January 1933, and an assassination attempt on Roosevelt was thwarted on February 15, 1933 in Miami, where a woman knocked the gun from an anarchist-freemason's hand and Chicago Mayor Anton Cermak was killed instead.[1] By May 1933 Roosevelt called the conference's failure to address the real crisis "a catastrophe amounting to a world tragedy" and dismissed short-term stabilization as the "old fetishes of so-called international bankers," insisting that exchange rate fixing "is not the true answer."[1] The British declared that "the American statement on stabilization rendered it entirely useless to continue the conference."[1]

Ehret adds that while the conference's success might have made the Second World War unnecessary, the Malthusian, eugenics-driven "scientifically managed" priesthood outlined by figures such as [[john-maynard-keynes|John Maynard Keynes]] would have been just as deadly.[1]
