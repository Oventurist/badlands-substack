# AfD Ban Effort

The AfD ban effort refers to the campaign by German parliamentarians to exclude the [[alternative-for-germany|Alternative for Germany]] (AfD) party from elections, which gained momentum in late 2024 even as the party's polling support rose.[1]

## Polling context

Polling by Insa-Consulere published by the Bild newspaper in November 2024 put AfD support at 19.5 percent, a rise of 1.5 percentage points since October, placing the party firmly in second place behind the center-right CDU/CSU at 32.5 percent.[1] Party leader [[alice-weidel|Alice Weidel]] emerged as one of the most popular potential chancellor candidates at 17 percent, ahead of Chancellor [[olaf-scholz|Olaf Scholz]] of the SPD and Economy Minister Robert Habeck of the Greens.[1] AfD strength was especially pronounced in eastern Germany, where Weidel led with 27 percent support.[1]

## The ban motion

The rise came at a critical juncture, with 112 [[bundestag|Bundestag]] members from across the political spectrum pushing to exclude the party from the snap election scheduled for February 23.[1] The motion, citing the party's alleged "anti-democratic" nature, had already been submitted to the Bundestag president and could escalate to Germany's Constitutional Court; if successful it would bar the AfD from standing in elections and effectively remove it from the democratic process.[1]

Proponents of the ban, including figures from the ruling SPD, argued the measure was necessary to protect the integrity of German democracy, while AfD supporters and allies viewed it as an undemocratic attempt to silence a growing political force.[1] The item appeared as a bonus entry in the Badlands brief without accompanying editorial commentary.[1]

## June 2024: Wanderwitz gathers signatures

An earlier phase of the campaign was reported in June 2024, when [[christian-democratic-union|Christian Democrat]] MP [[marco-wanderwitz|Marco Wanderwitz]] said he had enough colleagues behind him to table a ban motion in the [[bundestag|Bundestag]], naming 37 MPs who would support it in an interview with the newspaper taz.[2] At that point the [[alternative-for-germany|AfD]] was the second most popular party in the country.[2] Wanderwitz said he was waiting on the Münster Higher Administrative Court, which in May had upheld the classification of the AfD as a "suspected right-wing extremist" organisation but had not yet published a written justification: "Once the reasons for the ruling are available, we will take a close look at it and then submit our updated and well-founded application for a ban."[2] The court had at least five months from the date of its decision to release its written report.[2] Should the Bundestag vote for a ban, Germany's Constitutional Court would hold the final say on its legality, and ReMix News noted that an actual ban could throw the German political system into turmoil and raise questions about democratic legitimacy.[2]

### Badlands commentary

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] treated the prospect of the German legislature banning the second-most popular party as a re-run of 1933, when the National Socialists staged the [[reichstag-fire|Reichstag Fire]] and blamed the Communist Party of Germany, then the second-largest bloc in the legislature.[2] He noted the irony of the [[social-democratic-party-of-germany|Social Democratic Party]] accusing the conservative AfD of National Socialism, and cited an October 2022 article in which Social Democrat Dorothea Marx said the object of a ban was to target individual AfD politicians and disarm them, since the party was known to be under intense surveillance by German intelligence agencies to a degree that hindered its ability to operate.[2] He extended the argument into a broader polemic about [[nato|NATO]] and the [[european-union|European Union]] as an empire exceeding Hitler's ambitions yet dependent on Russian energy, raising what he called the "Prussian Question" and asking whether [[vladimir-putin|Putin]] would need to "de-nazify" [[germany|Germany]].[2]
