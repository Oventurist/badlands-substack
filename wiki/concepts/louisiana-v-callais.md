# Louisiana v. Callais

**Louisiana v. Callais** is a United States Supreme Court case decided in late April 2026 in which the Court held, 6–3, that Louisiana's congressional map creating a second majority-Black district was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. The decision narrows the circumstances in which states may rely on race when drawing legislative districts under the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]].

## Background

The case arose out of extended litigation over Louisiana's congressional map following the 2020 census. After lower-court findings under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, the state redrew its map to add a second majority-Black district. Challengers argued that the revised map subordinated traditional districting principles to racial targets, violating the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] and [[fifteenth-amendment|Fifteenth Amendment]]. The dispute reached the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] twice, requiring two rounds of oral argument before resolution.

## The majority opinion

Writing for the majority, Justice [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]] held that compliance with Section 2 can justify the consideration of race but does not require states to adopt maps that prioritize racial outcomes. Section 2, "correctly understood," does not impose liability in a manner that conflicts with the Constitution, and it did not mandate Louisiana's redraw. The Court left Section 2 formally intact, but the ruling is expected to constrain how the provision is applied in future redistricting litigation.

## Thomas concurrence

Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] joined the ruling but wrote separately to say he would have gone further. In his view the Voting Rights Act should not be available at all as a vehicle for challenging how states draw congressional districts: the statute was enacted to protect access to the ballot, not to regulate the design of district maps. Thomas argued that redistricting decisions fall outside the statute's scope and that no claims of that kind should succeed in future districting cases.

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing in the Badlands Brief and on the Devolution Power Hour, characterized the decision as a signal that [[critical-race-theory|critical race theory]] "is dead – legally." She framed the ruling as a defeat for the progressive legal effort — associated in the corpus with Norm Eisen and Marc Elias — to expand the interpretation of civil rights statutes, describing the outcome as the end of race-based manipulation of electoral maps and a step toward districts of more "natural and organic" composition. She noted that political gerrymandering remains untouched, but that the use of race to manipulate seats by party is finished.
