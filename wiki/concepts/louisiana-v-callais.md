# Louisiana v. Callais

**Louisiana v. Callais** is a United States Supreme Court case decided in April 2026 in which the Court ruled 6–3 that Louisiana's congressional map creating a second majority-Black district was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander, narrowing how states may rely on race when drawing districts under the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]].[1]

## Question presented

The case focused on whether the state's revised congressional map violated the [[fourteenth-amendment|14th]] and [[fifteenth-amendment|15th]] Amendments by prioritizing race in its design.[1] The dispute followed extended litigation and two rounds of Supreme Court arguments after [[louisiana|Louisiana]] added the second majority-Black district.[1]

## Holding

Writing for the majority, Justice [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]] said compliance with Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act can justify consideration of race but does not require states to adopt maps that prioritize racial outcomes.[1] He wrote that Section 2, "correctly understood," does not impose liability that conflicts with the Constitution and did not mandate Louisiana's redraw.[1]

The ruling leaves Section 2 in place but is expected to constrain how it is applied in future [[2026-redistricting-battle|redistricting]] fights.[1]

## Thomas concurrence

Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] joined the Court's ruling but issued a separate opinion saying he would have gone further.[1] Thomas argued that the Voting Rights Act should not be used to challenge how states draw congressional districts at all, contending that the law is meant to protect access to the ballot rather than to regulate how states design district maps.[1] He wrote that redistricting decisions fall outside the statute's scope and that no claims under that section should succeed in future districting cases.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the decision as a signal that [[critical-race-theory|critical race theory]] is "dead – legally," and framed it as a defeat for what she described as a progressive effort, associated with figures such as Marc Elias and Norm Eisen, to expand the interpretation of civil rights statutes.[1] She argued the ruling ends race-based manipulation of electoral maps and should recast districts in a more "natural and organic" composition, while noting that political gerrymandering remains unaddressed.[1] She had discussed the broader Voting Rights Act ecosystem on the [[culture-of-change|Culture of Change]] program and on the Devolution Power Hour before the decision was released.[1]

## Oral argument of October 15, 2025

The Supreme Court heard nearly two and a half hours of oral argument on Wednesday, October 15, 2025, and appeared ready to strike down the 2024 congressional map that a group of voters had challenged as the product of unconstitutional racial gerrymandering under the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection clause.[2] The Court's conservative justices signaled that they were likely to undermine a key provision of the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act]], even if they might not ultimately strike it down altogether.[2]

The dispute dates to 2022, when [[louisiana|Louisiana]] adopted a new congressional map following the 2020 census.[2] Although roughly one-third of the state's population is Black, the 2022 map contained only one majority-Black district out of six, prompting Black voters to sue under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, which bars discrimination in voting practices.[2] Judge Shelly Dick agreed and ordered the creation of a second majority-Black district; the [[us-court-of-appeals-for-the-fifth-circuit|Fifth Circuit]] upheld that decision and gave Louisiana until January 15, 2024 to produce a compliant map.[2] Louisiana then drew a two-majority-Black-district map, which a group of "non-African American" voters challenged as unconstitutional racial gerrymandering; a three-judge district court agreed and blocked it for future elections.[2] In May 2024 the Supreme Court put the lower court's order on hold, allowing the 2024 map to be used that year, and later set the case for argument.[2]

[[naacp|NAACP]] attorney [[janai-nelson|Janai Nelson]] told Justice [[brett-kavanaugh|Brett Kavanaugh]] that the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s position "would swallow Section 2 whole" and warned of "a staggering reversal of precedent that would throw maps across the country into chaos," while urging that any adverse ruling be remanded so the lower court could use an alternative that satisfies both Section 2 and the Constitution.[2]

### Badlands commentary on the argument

Ashe in America wrote that the Voting Rights Act has been effectively gutted over the years, leaving only Section 2 (redistricting) and Section 11 (suppression and intimidation) as meaningful legal paths for remedy under the statute, and that this case concerns Section 2.[2] She described the argument as "rough," questioning whether government may discriminate on the basis of race in remedying race discrimination, criticizing Justice [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]]'s reasoning — including what she read as an inadvertent analogy between Blackness and disability — and mocking Nelson's contention that white Democrats do not vote for Black candidates.[2] She argued that preserving the Act despite the mootness of its original cause has produced "unintelligible civil rights laws," and recommended the full two-and-a-half-hour C-SPAN recording of the hearing as illuminating, noting that at the Supreme Court "you cannot just call everyone racist and claim dog whistles and get your way."[2]
