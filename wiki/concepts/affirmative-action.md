# Affirmative Action

Affirmative action, in the American higher-education context, refers to the practice of considering an applicant's race or ethnicity as a factor in university admissions decisions. For decades it functioned as the principal tool that the nation's most exclusive schools used to diversify their campuses, and university officials had long insisted that no substitute for racial preferences existed that could ensure a representative share of minority applicants — particularly Black students — gained admission to selective institutions.[1]

## Legal framework before 2023

Under decades of [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] precedent, a minority applicant's race could serve as an unenumerated "plus factor" that raised the applicant's chances of admission, provided it was not used as a mechanical quota. Both [[harvard-university|Harvard College]] and the [[university-of-north-carolina|University of North Carolina]] defended their admissions practices on precisely those grounds, and lower courts agreed with them, rejecting the lawsuits brought against the two schools.[1]

## The 2023 ruling

On Thursday, June 29, 2023, the Supreme Court found it unconstitutional to consider race in university admissions. The 6–3 decision eliminated racial preferences as an admissions tool and forced a reworking of admissions criteria throughout American higher education, where the pursuit of diversity had for decades been treated as an article of faith.[1] No longer able to give minority applicants an automatic boost, admissions offices were left to decide where racial diversity ranked among competing priorities such as academic performance, achievement in extracurricular activities including athletics, and preferences for alumni and donors.[1]

The litigation that produced the ruling was organized by [[edward-blum|Edward Blum]], a former stockbroker who has brought a number of cases against laws and policies that make distinctions based on race or ethnicity in areas such as voting and education.[1]

## Aftermath: schools reverse their arguments

In the weeks after the decision, a debate unfolded around the country as schools that had spent decades downplaying the weight given to race in court began insisting that, without considering race, minority admissions would plummet.[2] [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] noted that during oral argument [[harvard-university|Harvard]]'s counsel [[seth-waxman|Seth Waxman]] had struggled to reconcile the claim that race consideration was needed to maintain minority admissions with his assertion that it was not a major factor, producing only a "slight disparity" for Asian students that "had no effect with respect to outcomes."[2] As in past cases, the Court had been assured that race was merely one of a number of "tips" that was not substantial in decisions, yet once the Court barred its use, schools argued that the change would make a massive difference.[2]

## Public opinion

An ABC News/Ipsos poll conducted using Ipsos' KnowledgePanel found that a majority of Americans approved of the ruling: 52% approved of restricting the use of race as a factor in college admissions, 32% disapproved, and 16% said they did not know.[2] Approval was strongest among Republicans (75%) and independents (58%), while only 26% of Democrats approved.[2] The same polling found the country more divided on other high-profile rulings and increasingly viewing the Court as driven more by politics than by law.[2]

## Further commentary

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] framed the controversy through Matthew 6:24 — no man can serve two masters — identifying the two masters as equal opportunity and equity, which he defined as rigging the system for allegedly oppressed classes.[2] He argued the two are mutually exclusive, since a system cannot provide equal opportunity while being rigged in favor of some, and that a consistent legal system cannot coexist with rewriting the rules at will, which is what [[living-constitution|living constitution judicial pragmatism]] asserts.[2] On that reasoning he described affirmative action as, at best, a misguided attempt to help "oppressed" classes and at worst an insidious plot by the socialist left to foment a culture of mediocrity in the United States as a form of subtle warfare.[2]

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] wrote that critics on the left reacted to the ruling as though it were the bedrock of democracy, and that many objecting probably had not read what the ruling actually said.[2] He described the holding in plain terms: the Supreme Court struck down the use of race as a factor in the admissions process, deciding that holding an applicant's race against them — in this case non-Black applicants — violated the guarantee of equal protection under the law in the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]].[2] Champions of affirmative action, he argued, claimed it was a necessary step to combat racism, but what was originally meant to create less racist conditions had become a perpetuated form of racism itself.[2] He posed two questions: whether assuming a person's race determines their thoughts and experiences is simply a rebranding of the racist thinking affirmative action purports to fight, and whether skin color determines what a person can contribute or is instead a superficial diversity used by politicians to prevent true diversity of thought.[2]

## Commentary

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] placed the ruling inside a "War of Stories" framing, noting that while it made surface-level sense for the Court to issue the decision before its summer recess, the timing echoed the release of the [[roe-v-wade|Roe v. Wade]] reversal the previous June, which had arrived just before Independence Day and given a weary MAGA movement a needed boost. After two [[donald-trump|Trump]] indictments and much else, he argued, the movement had received another significant win just before the Fourth of July, which he speculated might be a teaser for [[agenda-47|Agenda 47]].[1]
