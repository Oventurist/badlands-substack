# Nazi Symbols in the Ukrainian Military

The presence of symbols made notorious by Nazi Germany on the uniforms of Ukrainian service members became a recurring controversy during the Russia–Ukraine war, and was the subject of a June 5, 2023 [[new-york-times|New York Times]] report.[1]

## The Times report

The Times reported that since Russia began its invasion, the Ukrainian government and [[nato|NATO]] allies had posted and then quietly deleted three seemingly innocuous photographs from their social media feeds: a soldier standing in a group, another resting in a trench, and an emergency worker posing in front of a truck.[1] In each case, the Ukrainians in uniform wore patches featuring symbols popularized by Nazi Germany that have since become part of the iconography of far-right hate groups.[1] The paper framed the photographs and their deletions as evidence of the Ukrainian military's complicated relationship with Nazi imagery, a relationship it traced to both Soviet and German occupation during World War II.[1] It added that the issue was especially delicate because President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] had falsely declared Ukraine to be a Nazi state in order to justify his invasion.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the article as evidence of a "turn" in Western coverage of Ukraine.[1] He contrasted battlefield and political realities — the catastrophic failure of the Bakhmut counter-offensives, continued Russian shelling of Ukrainian and NATO munitions, U.S. funding for Ukraine set to expire within 60 days, and Berlin rebuffing Ukraine's NATO accession efforts — against the emerging narrative, in which Baltic states claimed Zelensky was "provoking" war, the [[state-department|US State Department]] publicly opposed de-escalation, and the Times admitted Western media had ignored Nazi ideology within Ukrainian forces.[1] He described this as legacy media applying an [[overton-window|Overton window]] shift to Nazi imagery, inching the public toward the implied truism that "Yes, Nazis are fighting against Russians … and that's a good thing," and predicted few ordinary readers would accept it.[1]
