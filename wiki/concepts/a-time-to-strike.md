# A Time to Strike

"A Time to Strike," subtitled "What Are the Cascade of Labor Strikes Accomplishing, and for Whom?", is an opinion essay published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on October 11, 2023 and written by the contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]]. The piece surveys the wave of American labor actions of 2023 — the Hollywood writers' strike, the United Auto Workers walkout, and a broader surge of "worker activism" across retail, logistics, and healthcare — and argues that although the strikers' grievances are real, their demands are premised on an economic model that automation, offshoring and artificial intelligence have already dismantled. The article carries the standard Badlands disclaimer that contributions represent the opinions of their authors rather than the outlet itself.

## The 2023 strike wave

The essay opens by observing that amid new wars, a House "leadership crisis," escalating weaponization of government and what it calls an irreparably broken economy, it is easy to forget that 2023 produced labor strikes unprecedented in recent memory. Citing CNBC, the article reports that there had been 312 strikes involving roughly 453,000 workers so far in 2023, compared with 180 strikes involving 43,700 workers over the same period two years earlier — figures compiled by Johnnie Kallas, a PhD candidate at Cornell University's School of Industrial and Labor Relations and project director of the ILR Labor Action Tracker.

The [[writers-guild-of-america|Writers Guild of America]] receives the first extended treatment. After striking for almost 150 days, the writers reached a deal with the studios and voted to end the strike on Wednesday, September 27, 2023. Quoting reporting on the settlement, the article notes that the deal runs until May 2026 and includes a 5% minimum pay increase upon ratification, another 4% bump on May 2, 2024, and a further 3.5% increase on May 5, 2025. Writers won significant protections against artificial intelligence: AI cannot write or rewrite literary material, and AI-generated material will not be considered source material. For streaming, the guild negotiated a new residual based on viewership. The author observes that it is unclear from the reporting whether the guild secured protections for the "writer's room" and staffing minimums, demands featured in member testimonials on the WGA Contract 2023 website. As of Monday, October 9, 99% of members had ratified the agreement.

The [[united-auto-workers|United Auto Workers]] strike is presented as the second pillar of the wave. On the same day the writers and studios reached agreement, UAW picketers were joined for the first time in U.S. history by a sitting president when [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] appeared on the line in Wayne County, Michigan. Quoting PBS, the article lists the UAW's demands: a 46-percent pay increase; a four-day work week with overtime pay beyond 32 hours; union representation at new electric battery plants; and an end to the employment tiers that created two classes of employees — an older, better-paid group with more benefits and a younger tier hired after 2007. The author characterizes these demands as "detached from reality" under "#Bidenomics."

One trigger for the auto and steel walkouts, the article argues, was the widespread and ongoing reduction in force across the sector. As the UAW strike continued, automakers kept issuing pink slips, with another round of layoffs announced on Monday, October 9, involving Sodecia Automotive, General Motors and Ford. The author contends that Biden's economic policy and unprecedented spending have destroyed U.S. manufacturing, making his appearance on the picket line "astonishing" and "very telling." Against this, the piece quotes [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s recent remark that "The auto industry is being assassinated."

Beyond the two headline disputes, the article points to Axios coverage of rising "worker activism" during the "Summer of Strikes," spanning 84 Amazon workers, 1,400 electrical workers, 3,000 Starbucks workers, and 75,000 employees of [[kaiser-permanente|Kaiser Permanente]]. The author acknowledges that the labor efforts are well-coordinated, on message, and popularly supported, but insists that employers are responding to "black or red economics": it simply takes fewer people to deliver the same products and services in 2023.

## Expectation versus reality

The essay's central section contrasts what strikers want with what the author regards as achievable. The expectations — better pay, better conditions, better quality of life — recall a mid-century America in which an electrician or steelworker could own a home, support a stay-at-home spouse and children, and retire on a pension. The author calls the hope of returning to that world naive absent the destruction of what she terms "the global corporate communist machine" that produced current conditions.

Three supporting data points are marshalled. First, on household income, the [[bureau-of-labor-statistics|Bureau of Labor Statistics]] is quoted: Consumer Expenditure data show that dual-income households have been a majority among U.S. households for at least two decades, ranging from 52 to 58 percent between 1998 and 2017, where a dual-income household is defined as one in which one spouse works full time and the other at least part time. Second, on family formation, the article notes that U.S. birth rates are declining across every demographic, down 20% over the past decade, with many couples opting not to have children; Statista is quoted reporting around 3.66 million live births in the United States in 2021 and a decades-long decline attributed in part to postponed childbearing and contraception. Third, on retirement, the author asserts that although pensions remain a benefit in many industries, they are all insolvent "unless you change the definition of insolvent," referencing Fortune's December 2022 reporting on the $36 billion Teamsters pension bailout delivered through the American Rescue Plan.

These conditions, the article argues, did not arise organically: doubling the tax base while reducing the population and bankrupting the system resembles groundwork for the [[great-reset|Great Reset]] and a New World Order. The social outcomes among workers are identified as demoralization, disillusionment, and demands detached from reality — with UAW demands for four-day weeks, large pay increases and companies that prioritize workers at the expense of profitability traced to a "global communist vision" of a much less populated utopia.

## Millennials, social justice and work

A subsection turns to generational politics. The author cites Dave Ramsey's quip that "There are two types of 'millennial': Awesome and Sucks," allowing that she knows many of the former. Millennials, she writes, brought unbridled social justice into the workplace and into brands and products, and are now the first digital-native generation to reach the union negotiating table — a generation that may destroy entire industries through unrealistic expectations. The essay treats this as not really their fault: millennials have been "drowning in communism since birth," with media programming and academic education intentionally developed to shape their worldview, and the same influences that neglected to teach consequences now stand beside them on the picket line "by design." Corporations that embraced millennial demands and pumped social justice programs to the point of requiring mass reductions in force now find themselves across the picket line from those same employees — a situation the author suggests may be the point.

## Automation, McKinsey, and the future of work

The article's analytical core holds that Trump is correct that the auto industry is being assassinated, given optimization policies that streamlined and automated manufacturing and global sourcing policies that moved remaining jobs offshore. Global corporations have invested in [[automation|automation]], robotics and [[artificial-intelligence|artificial intelligence]] for over a decade, and the current disruption is the return on that investment. The discussion of the [[future-of-work|"future of work"]] inside global corporations has run for nearly ten years for a reason: there are going to be fewer jobs than people.

[[mckinsey-and-company|McKinsey & Company]] is named as the designer of this transition. The article quotes the firm's July 2020 report "The future of work: reskilling and remote working to recover in the next normal": "Even before the current crisis, AI, automation, and other technologies were already creating major shifts in required workforce skills. In 2017, our scenarios suggested that by 2030, up to 375 million workers (14 percent of the global workforce) would have to switch occupational categories." The author reasons that McKinsey was measuring this in 2017 because it was studying it, and studying it because it knew its own recommendations would produce those consequences — the world generating these labor-market impacts being the world the firm is designing. McKinsey and its peer professional services firms, she notes, deliver "solutions" to "problems" in every sector, geography and industry, including automotive and manufacturing, media and entertainment, healthcare, retail, forestry, aerospace and agriculture, with the top players in every industry as clients. The essay emphasizes that McKinsey is the strategic partner of the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]], calling it "essentially the world's strategy firm" whose influence touches every aspect of human life. A February 2021 McKinsey publication is cited as revising the figure eight months later.

Software, the author writes, can now replace entire skill sets that once required full-time employees; predictive analytics and generative AI have replaced whole disciplines because machines reach output faster and more accurately than humans; robots assemble where humans once stood. The optimization of traditional work will not stop.

## Conclusion: a first and final stand

The closing section returns to the layoffs continuing during the strike, asserting that as much as 62% of some manufacturing workforces have been eliminated even as the UAW demands a four-day week. The 18-month term of the WGA contract is read as strategic: as generative AI matures, the studios will hold significantly greater leverage over content creation. The author recounts wanting to stand with the writers at the strike's outset but being repeatedly stopped by their demands, and having initially framed their disruption as the same optimization every company underwent a decade earlier. She revises that judgment: corporate back offices were streamlined then, but back offices do not unionize and do not strike; their eliminations were celebrated, with CFOs raining "transformation dollars" and "redundant" workers receiving severance if they were lucky, and often nothing.

What distinguishes the present moment, the essay concludes, is that this strike is both a first and a final stand. The transformation of society is "endgame," it is here, and the final stand is likely too late — in the author's opinion the time to strike would have been 2017 at the latest. These particular strikes never stood a chance; had workers struck to protect liberty and the American way of life there might have been a course correction, but the cross-industry demands prove the "Summer of Strikes" to be self-interested and delusional, driven by greed in denial of an industry talent model that no longer exists. Strikers may win short-term concessions, but any deals will be short-lived because the bigger picture is a long game. The article ends with a warning to those who ignore the inevitable "in a world addicted to progress," and the sardonic sign-off, "And learn to code, I guess."
