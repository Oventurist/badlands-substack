# Maisha Namba

Maisha Namba is Kenya's third-generation biometric digital identity program: a unique personal identification number assigned to every Kenyan citizen, tied to a physical "Maisha Card" that serves as a digital identity credential. The program is presented by its backers as a means of accessing government services and conducting transactions.[1]

## Gates Foundation involvement

Badlands Media reports that the program received backing from [[bill-gates|Bill Gates]] and the [[gates-foundation|Gates Foundation]], with Gates's role in assisting the Kenyan government's development and rollout announced only after a series of "closed-door meetings" with President [[william-ruto|William Ruto]]. The *Kenyan Daily Post* reported that Gates, who is known for championing genetically modified foods, had met Ruto repeatedly since Ruto came to power, with most engagements shrouded in secrecy, and that the deal would connect the government to key technical experts and partners offering professional advice to guarantee a smooth rollout.[1]

Gates framed the initiative as one that would "improve efficiency, transparency, and accountability in the country's governance system, ultimately benefiting the citizens," though the article notes there has been little concrete explanation of how citizens would actually benefit.[1]

## Budget and delays

Kenya prepared for a US$6.8 million launch of the biometric digital ID system over several months. At the beginning of October 2023, however, Kenyan authorities postponed the launch due to "unfavorable circumstances."[1]

## Opposition and legal history

The project has met significant skepticism inside Kenya. Residents of the eastern Kenyan city of Garissa said they were not ready for the rollout because of poor infrastructure and network availability. Human rights groups have expressed concern about the possibility of discrimination and the erosion of privacy, arguing that the government is repeating the mistakes of Kenya's previous ID system, Huduma Namba, which was declared unconstitutional by the country's High Court in October 2021 for conflicting with the Data Protection Act.[1]

The government has promised that the third-generation ID card will not be mandatory and will not require individuals to register anew, relying instead on the existing civil registration system. Badlands argues that such voluntary-participation language is characteristic of programs with dramatic societal effects: assurances that no one is required to opt in persist only until it becomes increasingly difficult to operate in the system without the new technology — "an ever-steady encroachment," where today's assurances will not be tomorrow's.[1]

## Biometric vaccination for newborns

One component of the digital ID scheme is a [[biometric-vaccination-system-for-newborns|biometric vaccination system for newborns]] intended to replace birth certificates while tracking children from birth to five years of age to ensure they receive their vaccines.[1]

## Significance

The article treats Maisha Namba as a case study in how [[digital-identification|digital identification]] functions as the lynchpin of an emerging global [[technocracy|technocratic]] control system — one that would allow ruling classes to monitor and manipulate populations, regulate information flow, restrict access to resources and enforce compliance.[1]
