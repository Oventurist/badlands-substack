# 2025 Russia–Burkina Faso Nuclear Energy Agreement

The 2025 Russia–Burkina Faso nuclear energy agreement is a bilateral pact formalising expanded cooperation on peaceful nuclear energy between Russia and [[burkina-faso|Burkina Faso]], including joint projects in radiation technologies and the training of specialists from the West African country.[1] It was signed on a Thursday in June 2025 by [[aleksey-likhachev|Alexey Likhachev]], chief executive of Russian state nuclear corporation [[rosatom|Rosatom]], and Burkinabè Energy Minister Yacouba Zabré Gouba on the sidelines of the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum.[1] The signing was reported by [[rt|RT]].[1]

## Terms and background

Likhachev said the agreement marked "an important milestone in strengthening the partnership between Russia and Burkina Faso," adding that Moscow was "ready to provide advanced technologies and expert support to implement joint projects aimed at sustainable development and enhancing the region's energy security."[1] According to Rosatom's statement, the new pact builds on a roadmap signed the previous March between Rosatom and Burkina Faso's Ministry of Energy during the ATOMEXPO forum in Sochi.[1]

## Commentary

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] noted that the deal broke on the same day he interviewed retired General [[steven-kwast|Steven L. Kwast]] about what he called the broken "hero system" in Africa, and that the case study used in the conversation happened to be Burkina Faso and the rise of Captain [[ibrahim-traore|Ibrahim Traoré]].[1] He observed that 80 percent of Burkina Faso's population lacks access to electricity, making it one of the most destitute nations on earth, and that Traoré has refused aid from most external parties — including a reported $200 billion offer from Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] — on the grounds that the country would rather remain poor than owe future favours.[1] He argued the Russian deal was different in kind because it is bilateral and potentially mutually beneficial, and because it lets Burkina Faso send its brightest students to Moscow to study nuclear energy so they can eventually maintain their own facilities, concluding that it was a shame the West could not offer comparable assistance to Africa.[1]
