# Operation Absolute Resolve

Operation Absolute Resolve was the codename for the large-scale United States military action against [[venezuela|Venezuela]] conducted in the early morning hours of January 3, 2026, which culminated in the capture of Venezuelan President [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]].[1]

## The operation

The strikes centered on Caracas and other locations across the country and involved more than 150 US aircraft, airstrikes on military installations including the La Carlota airbase and Fuerte Tiuna, and ground raids in Caracas and nearby areas such as Miranda, Aragua and La Guaira.[1] Explosions were reported around 2 a.m. local time.[1] Casualty estimates varied widely and many reports relied on anonymous Venezuelan and Cuban sources; no US fatalities were reported.[1]

Maduro and his wife [[cilia-flores|Cilia Flores]] were captured by US forces and transported into the custody of the US District Court for the Southern District of New York to face federal charges including narco-terrorism conspiracy, cocaine importation conspiracy and additional counts contained in a superseding indictment.[1]

## Political aftermath

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] asserted US control or oversight of Venezuela's immediate future and signaled possible further actions, declaring "We are going to run the country" and framing the operation partly in terms of oil: he said American talent had built Venezuela's oil industry and that "the socialist regime stole it from us," and promised that large US oil companies would "spend billions of dollars, fix the badly broken infrastructure."[1] Trump dismissed the prospect that opposition figure [[maria-corina-machado|María Corina Machado]] would lead Venezuela, saying she lacked support and respect inside the country, and instead honored Maduro's designation of Vice President [[delcy-rodriguez|Delcy Rodríguez]] as acting president — while warning that if she did not give the US "total access" to rebuild the oil infrastructure, "she will pay a very big price — probably even bigger than Maduro."[1]

Mexico and numerous other countries condemned the action as a violation of the UN Charter and international law.[1] Venezuela's Supreme Tribunal of Justice named Rodríguez acting president, though her legitimacy was disputed.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands contributors read the operation as a negotiated exit rather than a genuine decapitation strike. [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that the indictment of Maduro's wife — something historically never done to the spouses of adversary heads of state — indicated a cooperating witness being taken into protective custody rather than the toppling of a dictator, and pointed to imagery of Maduro smiling, wishing DEA agents "Happy New Year," giving thumbs up in photos with [[drug-enforcement-administration|DEA]] agents, wearing a Disney-style hat, and being paraded through New York City in a van with its doors open.[1] He also noted that the [[cia|CIA]] apparently leaked operational details to *The New York Times* and *The Washington Post* in advance so articles could be pre-written.[1] He suggested Trump's claim that oil companies would pay to rebuild Venezuelan infrastructure could function as reparations tied to the 2018 lawsuits filed by [[pdvsa|PDVSA]] accusing global oil executives of corruption and theft of Venezuelan wealth.[1]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] situated the events within the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] framework, writing that Badlands had for months predicted Maduro would be granted a "narrative exit" from the cartel theater "in keeping with the Assad Template," with China and Russia posturing while the underlying action was Sovereign Alliance versus globalist cartel.[1] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] was more critical of audience reaction, comparing celebration of a scripted operation to leaving a James Bond film and cheering in the streets, and noting that "no war" was what Maduro himself had repeatedly called for.[1] [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] quipped that the best ending would be Trump awarding himself the $50 million bounty for Maduro's capture.[1]

Israeli journalist Hallel Rosen said on Israeli television that the Venezuela operation was "the last rehearsal before Iran," a framing echoed in Badlands commentary linking the action to the concurrent [[2026-iran-protests|Iran protests]].[1]

## Aftermath: arraignment, succession, and international reaction

In early January 2026, [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]] pleaded not guilty in a New York federal court to four counts — narco-terrorism, cocaine importation conspiracy, and possession and conspiracy to possess machine guns and destructive devices — with his wife [[cilia-flores|Cilia Flores]] pleading not guilty to related charges; both face decades to life in prison if convicted, and some legal analysts argued the prosecution is unlawful under international law.[2]

Following Maduro's removal, Vice President and Oil Minister [[delcy-rodriguez|Delcy Rodríguez]] was sworn in as interim president alongside 283 lawmakers elected the previous May, few of whom Reuters categorized as opposition because candidates directed by Nobel laureate [[maria-corina-machado|María Corina Machado]] had boycotted the contest.[2] Gunfire was reported at [[miraflores-palace|Miraflores Palace]] after the swearing-in, with some accounts describing security forces firing at a drone.[2]

Mexican President [[claudia-sheinbaum|Claudia Sheinbaum]] criticized the operation and reasserted Mexico's non-intervention policy, while President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] suggested a similar operation against Colombia "sounds good to me" and warned that something would have to be done about the cartels in Mexico.[2] Energy markets weighed whether sanctions relief could raise Venezuelan output; Morningstar analyst Joshua Aguilar said the firm's oil price forecasts would not change because Venezuela requires years of large capital investment, noting that "regime changes rarely lead to rapid investment and stabilizing supply."[2] State oil company [[pdvsa|PDVSA]] separately asked some joint-venture partners to cut output amid rising onshore inventories and diluent shortages.[2]

### Sovereign Alliance interpretation

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the operation resembled the Syrian transition — a managed, largely bloodless handover rather than a hostile regime change — carried out under the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] lens.[2] He pointed to Russia's Dmitry Medvedev calling Trump's actions "illegal" while conceding they had "a certain consistency" in defending national interests, and to Chinese social media framing the Venezuela operation as a "perfect blueprint" for Taiwan, as instances of [[narrative-shielding|Narrative Shielding]].[2] He noted Trump's stated willingness to apply the template from Iran and Mexico to Colombia, Cuba and Greenland, and Denmark's warning that a "Venezuela playbook" there would mean "the end of NATO."[2] [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] commented that "calling what happened a regime change isn't a bad thing," describing it as "one of the most impressive displays of power projection you could possibly do."[2]
