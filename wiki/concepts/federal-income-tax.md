# Federal Income Tax

The federal income tax is the levy on individual earnings instituted in the United States in 1913 following ratification of the [[sixteenth-amendment|Sixteenth Amendment]]. In the Badlands Media corpus it is treated as an instrument of control rather than a fiscal necessity.

## The constitutional objection

The essay A Week to Remember notes that "our founding fathers viewed income tax as being unconstitutional" and that "politicians had to later amend the Constitution to start taxing Americans' incomes in 1913." Setting that argument aside, the article presses a second objection: the same dollar is taxed repeatedly through income, property, sales, and capital gains taxes, amounting to "double, triple and quadruple taxation, certainly without representation." The author extends the point further — "in reality, all taxes are taxation without representation, because your government does not represent you … they do not work for you" — and illustrates the absurdity with the image of paying separate taxes to place purchases in a car trunk, remove them, and store them in a closet.

## Taxes as illusion

According to the source, "we don't really need to pay taxes to run the country, and our taxes don't actually pay for our government." Their function is presentational: "Taxes are used to create the illusion that Americans pay for their government, and the illusion that, without Americans paying their taxes, the government couldn't function." That illusion in turn sustains the belief that the government works for the people. The [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]], the article says, exists to make Americans "live in fear."

The essay dismantles common justifications by pointing to overlapping levies — if income tax funds roads and the [[united-states-postal-service|Postal Service]], why also gas taxes, car tabs, and postage? It recalls [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s argument that Amazon should deliver its own packages rather than be subsidised through USPS, noting that Amazon has thrived while running its own fleet.

## The historical argument

The article observes that before the federal income tax existed, America built its railroads, strung power lines nationwide, laid much of the highway and freeway system, and maintained a military, schools, hospitals, and sidewalks. Between the Civil War and the founding of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] in 1913, it argues, "America became an economic giant without having a Federal Income Tax." The author asks, "When was the last time you saw a new freeway built? Where is the money really going?" The simultaneous 1913 arrival of the income tax, the Federal Reserve, and the IRS is presented as no coincidence.

## Tax-free tips as a first step

The essay's central thesis is that Trump's June 10, 2024 pledge to exempt tips from taxation was the opening move toward abolishing the income tax altogether — and that the [[deep-state|Deep State]] recognised it as such. Taxing tips, the author argues, makes little sense in principle: a tip "is not income, it's a way people are shown appreciation," though some industries "have turned tips into wages" by underpaying staff and shifting the burden to customers.

The article predicts a staged dismantling rather than an abrupt one. Abolishing the income tax on January 20, 2025 would trigger "a panic … over the financial system," and legitimate programs would go unfunded. Instead the author expects tax-free tips, then lower overall rates, then elimination, with the government funded by tariffs and corporate taxes while federal jobs and programs are cut: "Less government, less government control." Income tax, the essay concludes, "does help to expand the size and control of the government, and it is used to control Americans, but it's not necessary to run the government."

## As treated in "America's Greatest Threat"

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]]'s America's Greatest Threat (April 24, 2024) advances a parallel argument. It holds that the founders "specifically stated in the [[united-states-constitution|Constitution]] that a federal income tax was unconstitutional," that the absence of such a tax kept the federal government small as intended, and that the [[sixteenth-amendment|Sixteenth Amendment]] ratified on February 3, 1913 removed the question from the electorate by permitting taxation without apportionment by population.

Carlson catalogues the layered incidence of taxation — federal and state income tax, sales tax, property tax, taxes on investments and savings — summarising that "a dollar is taxed when you earn it, when you spend it and when you save it," with [[inflation|inflation]] operating as the largest levy of all. Because the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] can print money at will, he argues, the government does not need the revenue; the tax exists to produce [[debt-slavery|debt slaves]], enforced by fear of the [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]]. He concludes with a personal admission that he believes "taxes are theft" yet files and pays before the deadline rather than risk imprisonment.
