# Nondelegation Doctrine

The nondelegation doctrine is a principle of American constitutional law holding that [[us-congress|Congress]] may not transfer its legislative power to executive agencies or private entities without supplying meaningful limits on how that power is exercised. It became a central question before the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] in March 2025 in consolidated cases concerning the [[federal-communications-commission|Federal Communications Commission]]'s administration of "universal service subsidy programs" and the mandatory contributions those programs extract from telecommunications carriers.[1]

## The 2025 FCC cases

Following two days of oral argument on [[environmental-protection-agency|EPA]] venue questions, the Court was scheduled to hear one oral argument covering two consolidated cases dealing with congressional funding of the FCC's universal service regime.[1] The questions presented were whether Congress violated the nondelegation doctrine by authorizing the Commission to determine, within the limits set forth in Section 254, the amount providers must contribute to the Fund; whether the Commission violated the doctrine by using the administrator's — that is, USAC's — financial projections in computing universal service contribution rates; and whether the combination of Congress's conferral of authority on the Commission and the Commission's onward delegation of administrative responsibilities to USAC violates the doctrine.[1] The Court additionally directed the parties to be prepared to argue why the case was not moot, "in light of the challengers' failure to seek preliminary relief before the Fifth Circuit."[1]

## Significance

Badlands host [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who covered the arguments live for Badlands Media, framed the FCC dispute as part of a broader 2025 confrontation over the reach of the [[administrative-state|administrative state]] and the authority of federal courts, noting that Badlands would carry the hearing in the afternoon alongside coverage of the EPA cases.[1]
