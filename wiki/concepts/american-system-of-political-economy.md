# American System of Political Economy

The American System of Political Economy is the school of national economic development associated with [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] and his economic advisor [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]], defined by protective tariffs, national credit, and vast internal improvements — and set explicitly in opposition to the British free-trade system.[1]

Carey described the clash in his 1851 *Harmony of Interests*: one system "looks to increasing the proportion of persons and of capital engaged in trade and transportation, and therefore to diminishing the proportion engaged in producing commodities," with diminished return to all labour; the other increases the proportion engaged in production, giving the labourer good wages and the owner of capital good profits.[1] "One looks to pauperism, ignorance, depopulation, and barbarism; the other in increasing wealth, comfort, intelligence, combination of action, and civilization. One looks towards universal war; the other towards universal peace. One is the English system; the other we may be proud to call the American system."[1]

## International diffusion

By the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia, the American System had produced what the article calls the greatest explosion of wealth in United States history and had become a model for nations seeking to break free of British colonial hegemony.[1] Germany under Chancellor [[otto-von-bismarck|Otto von Bismarck]] applied high protective tariffs and vast internal improvements under the Zollverein.[1] Czars [[alexander-ii|Alexander II]] and Alexander III and their advisors applied the model to Russia's modernization, vectored around the [[trans-siberian-railway|Trans-Siberian Railway]].[1] Japan under the Meiji Restoration applied it to escape feudalism and enter the modern age.[1]

Ehret argues that an age of London-financed revolutions, assassinations and wars mis-shaped the 1880s, 1890s and the twentieth century, preventing this system of win-win cooperation from evolving as intended, with only brief revivals under [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin Roosevelt]] and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]].[1] He identifies today's champions of the [[multipolar-world|multipolar world]] — [[xi-jinping|Xi Jinping]] and [[vladimir-putin|Putin]] — as governing by the best principles of the nineteenth-century American System.[1]

## Hamiltonian origins

The system originated with [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]]'s four major reports to Congress of 1791–92 as Washington's Treasury Secretary, and revolved around constitutional banking shaped by Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution, protective tariffs, and internal improvements.[2] Its four components were the consolidation of unpayable state debts into a single federal debt, the tying of new bonds to internal improvements and industry, the guidance of national finance through the [[first-bank-of-the-united-states|First Bank of the United States]] and successor national banks subservient to the Constitution and the General Welfare, and protective measures against foreign dumping supplemented by federal bounties.[2] German economist [[friedrich-list|Friedrich List]] was among the first to codify Hamilton's program under the name "American System of Political Economy," in his 1827 *Outlines of American Political Economy*.[2]

## Principles

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] premises the system on three general truths: respect for national sovereignty under a win-win system of cooperation rather than supranational controls under an unelected elite; large-scale, long-term infrastructure projects that uplift living standards, cognitive powers, and the productive powers of labor simultaneously; and the resulting increase of national capital and consumer consumption per capita and per square kilometer.[2] Taken together these variables tend to increase humanity's carrying capacity, conditional upon a factor Ehret says ivory-tower mathematicians and climate modellers detest: human creative reason.[2] He points to the late American economist [[lyndon-larouche|Lyndon LaRouche]]'s 1984 book *So You Wish to Learn All About Economics* as a scientific guidebook to the system, and identifies the opposing school with British imperial theoreticians premised on the [[malthusianism|Malthusian]] assumption that material limits to nature are insurmountable.[2]

[[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] articulated the underlying idea in 1860: "Man is not the only animal who labors; but he is the only one who improves his workmanship. This improvement, he effects by Discoveries, and Inventions."[2] His economic advisor [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]] amplified it in his 1872 *Unity of Law*, writing that the greater man's power of association, "the greater becomes his control of the forces of nature, and the more perfect his own power for self-direction."[2] Ehret reads both as recognizing that increasing rates of discovery of hitherto-unknown organizing principles of the universe allow the species to overcome limits to growth by leaping to new technologies and resources.[2]

## Suppression in America

Hamilton's murder by [[aaron-burr|Aaron Burr]] in 1804 derailed many of the system's designs, but did not destroy it.[2] In America the clash between the American and British systems defined all major conflicts from 1836, when [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] killed the [[second-bank-of-the-united-states|Second National Bank]] and brought the nation under the heel of British free trade, speculation, and cotton plantation economics.[2] Following protocols that Ehret says the IMF would impose on victim nations 150 years later, Jackson cancelled all internal improvements in order to "pay the debt" and deregulated banking, producing over 7,000 separate currencies issued by state banks and an economy that was chaotic, bankrupt, and prone to mass counterfeiting.[2] Whig defenders — [[john-quincy-adams|John Quincy Adams]], Lincoln, and [[henry-clay|Henry Clay]] — fought a rear-guard action until the 1840 victory of [[william-henry-harrison|William Harrison]], who died within three months with third national bank legislation unsigned on his desk.[2] Not until 1861 did a president arise who avoided assassination long enough to revive the system during an existential crisis of bankruptcy and foreign-sponsored civil war.[2]

## Revival under Lincoln

Ehret contrasts Hamilton's system, which tied the value and behaviour of money to the increasing powers of production of a society through manufacturing and internal improvements, to the opposing system of British free trade, which tied value to hedonistic impulses and the worshiping of money.[3] He argues that the means needed to break both systems of empire and slavery were located in the American System of political economy.[3]

Lincoln revived the system during the [[american-civil-war|Civil War]] in a three-step operation: the Banking and Currency Acts of 1862 and 1863, which placed thousands of state banks under federal charter and supervision; the [[national-bank-act-of-1863|Bank Act of 1863]], which set reserve requirements and capped interest rates to destroy usury; and the issuance of [[greenbacks|greenbacks]] under the [[legal-tender-act-of-1862|Legal Tender Act of 1862]], backed by [[five-twenty-bonds|5:20 bonds]] sold to ordinary citizens.[3] These measures were accompanied by a strong protective tariff to grow American industries, and financed the [[first-transcontinental-railroad|transcontinental railway]] begun in 1863.[3]

The postwar reversal came through the [[specie-resumption-act|Specie Resumption Act]], which killed the greenbacks, tied the republic's currency to gold, and contracted the credit available for large-scale, long-term infrastructure projects.[3]
