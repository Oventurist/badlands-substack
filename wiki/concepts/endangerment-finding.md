# Endangerment Finding

The endangerment finding is the 2009 determination by the [[environmental-protection-agency|Environmental Protection Agency]] that greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide endanger public health and welfare.[1] It is a landmark scientific and legal determination that provided the foundational basis under the [[clean-air-act|Clean Air Act]] for regulating emissions from vehicles, power plants, oil-and-gas facilities, and other sources.[1]

## Legal origins and scope

The finding was enabled by the Supreme Court decision in [[massachusetts-v-epa|Massachusetts v. EPA]], and in the Badlands framing it unlocked sweeping carbon mandates across energy, manufacturing, transportation, and logistics for more than a decade.[1]

## 2026 rescission

The [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] moved to rescind the finding by final rule, following a proposed rescission announced the previous July and an Inauguration Day executive order directing a review of its legality.[1] Officials including EPA Administrator [[lee-zeldin|Lee Zeldin]] described the rescission as the largest act of deregulation in U.S. history, projecting over $1 trillion in savings, largely from reduced vehicle compliance costs averaging more than $2,400 per light-duty vehicle.[1] The rescission initially targets motor vehicle emissions standards while paving the way for future rollbacks of power plant and stationary-source rules.[1]

Environmental organizations pledged immediate litigation based on settled science and Supreme Court precedent upholding the finding, and legal experts anticipated prolonged court fights during which some rules may go unenforced.[1] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the rescission as detonating the legal foundation of federal climate regulation.[1]

## Narrative aftermath (February 2026)

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the full repeal of the Obama-era endangerment finding — which branded greenhouse gases a threat to public health and served as the legal cornerstone propping up decades of [[environmental-protection-agency|EPA]] climate mandates — as the largest act of deregulation in American history.[2] With the finding gone, motor vehicle emission standards were eliminated and compliance programs, credit schemes and reporting obligations were, in his phrasing, ripped out root and stem.[2]

He described the response of what he calls the [[media-protectorate|Media Protectorate]] as immediate and predictable, with environmental groups wailing about millions endangered and Democratic strongholds vowing legal warfare.[2] The narrative frame crystallized overnight, casting [[donald-trump|Trump]] once more as an existential threat rolling back "settled science" in service to fossil-fuel oligarchs and steering America toward cataclysm under the guise of flourishing.[2] Burning Bright termed this the "Hegelian Nightmare" in its purest form: existential threats woven into the fabric of the Story, where progress itself is painted as planetary suicide, abundance is recast as greed, and the technologies that once promised a retro-futurist Golden Age are demonized as inverted world-enders.[2]

He argued that on the very day the rollback dominated surface-level coverage, a series of precise counter-deployments emerged — chief among them the [[ai-data-center-compact|AI Data Center Compact]] and a poll showing Democrats holding an edge on energy costs.[2] A same-week *Wall Street Journal* opinion piece, "Even Trump Can't Go Back to the Future," argued that structural energy shifts — rising demand from data centers and AI, and a 15-year move toward natural gas and renewables driven by cheap gas and plant retirements rather than regulation alone — mean market forces, capital cycles and physical infrastructure constraints shape outcomes more than executive action, concluding that nothing can stop the energy transition.[2]
