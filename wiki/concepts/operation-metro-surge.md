# Operation Metro Surge

Operation Metro Surge was an aggressive [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|ICE]] and [[customs-and-border-protection|CBP]] enforcement operation conducted in Minnesota as part of the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s mass deportation program.[1]

## Fatal shootings

The operation produced clashes in Minneapolis and St. Paul during which two U.S. citizen protesters were fatally shot: Renee Good on January 7, 2026, and Alex Pretti on January 24, 2026.[1] Homeland Security Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]] blamed "violent protesters" for the chaos, while Democratic senators accused federal officers of excessive force and criticized Noem for labeling the deceased "domestic terrorists."[1]

## Congressional scrutiny

The operation was a central subject of the [[senate-judiciary-committee|Senate Judiciary Committee]] hearing of March 3, 2026, at which Noem faced bipartisan criticism over both the enforcement tactics and unrelated departmental spending controversies.[1]

## Origins and scale

The operation began in December 2025 and produced more than 4,000 arrests in the Minneapolis–St. Paul area, along with statewide deployments of federal immigration agents.[2]

## Conclusion of the operation

In February 2026 federal authorities announced that the Minnesota immigration crackdown was ending, following an operation that had led to thousands of arrests and violent protests.[2] Border Czar [[tom-homan|Tom Homan]], who had gone to Minnesota to assess the surge and conduct federal, state, local and community stakeholder outreach, said he had worked to "deescalate the intense, unlawful, and dangerous situation that had unfolded here."[2] Homan reported that ICE's target list had been significantly reduced and many public safety threats removed from local communities; that an "unprecedented level of coordination" had been obtained with counties, which agreed to notify ICE before releasing criminal aliens from jail so custody could be assumed in a jail setting; that unlawful agitator activity had notably decreased; and that state and local law enforcement had stepped up against unlawful actions by agitators.[2]

Homan said he had proposed concluding the surge and that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] concurred, adding that officers and agents deployed to Minnesota would continue immigration enforcement in other locations across the country in service of border security and mass deportations.[2] He dismissed criticism from "the far left and extreme, organized agitator groups," thanked personnel from [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|ICE]] and [[customs-and-border-protection|CBP]], and said Minnesota communities were safer as a result.[2]

## January 2026: arrest milestone, strike call, and litigation

By January 19, 2026, the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] reported more than 10,000 arrests as part of the operation, which it described as involving thousands of federal agents — reports placed the deployment at roughly 2,000 to 3,000 or more — conducting arrests, raids and enforcement actions across the Minneapolis–Saint Paul metro area and statewide.[3] Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]] said DHS had "arrested over 10,000 criminal illegal aliens who were killing Americans, hurting children and reigning terror in Minneapolis because [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]] and [[jacob-frey|Jacob Frey]] refuse to protect their own people and instead protect criminals," and reported 3,000 arrests in the preceding six weeks.[3]

The surge depressed business activity in [[minneapolis|Minneapolis]], particularly in immigrant neighborhoods, where some businesses — notably day care and health transport establishments — reported revenue drops of up to 100%, and locals raised concerns that the operation was generating fear and economic instability.[3] A coalition of faith leaders, community groups and unions announced an "ICE Out of Minnesota: Day of Truth and Freedom" for Friday, January 23, calling for a statewide economic blackout and general strike — no work except emergency services, no school, no shopping — with demands including ICE withdrawal from Minnesota, punishment of the officer involved in the death of Renée Good, no additional ICE funding, and policies severing state ties with ICE.[3] Local and regional [[afl-cio|AFL-CIO]], [[seiu|SEIU]], [[american-federation-of-teachers|AFT]] and other labor unions publicly endorsed the effort.[3]

On January 12, 2026, Minnesota Attorney General [[keith-ellison|Keith Ellison]] and the cities of Minneapolis and [[saint-paul|Saint Paul]] filed a federal lawsuit (Case No. 0:26-cv-00190) against DHS and federal officials, naming Noem along with ICE and CBP officials as defendants, alleging that Operation Metro Surge and its deployment of armed agents violated constitutional rights and seeking declaratory relief, a temporary restraining order and an injunction against certain enforcement tactics.[3] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that Ellison's suit followed grand jury indictments concerning the Minnesota government's alleged enablement of foreign criminal networks, and asked whether officials were "trying to sue their way out of being indicted."[3] She also observed that [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] press releases in the same period were dominated by migrant-crime, money-laundering and fraud prosecutions, and urged the federal government to spend more time on public officials enabling corruption and less on speech and tax-evasion cases.[3]

## January 2026 escalation

The [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] launched Operation Metro Surge in early January 2026, deploying approximately 2,000 federal agents from [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|ICE]], [[customs-and-border-protection|Customs and Border Protection]] and [[homeland-security-investigations|Homeland Security Investigations]] to Minneapolis and St. Paul.[4] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] subsequently ordered an additional 1,000 CBP agents to the Twin Cities, potentially bringing the total to roughly 3,000 federal personnel conducting immigration enforcement amid what Badlands described as a violent left-wing insurgency.[4] Trump posted on [[truth-social|Truth Social]]: "FEAR NOT, GREAT PEOPLE OF MINNESOTA, THE DAY OF RECKONING & RETRIBUTION IS COMING!"[4]

White House deputy chief of staff for policy [[stephen-miller|Stephen Miller]] addressed the deployed officers directly on Fox News' Will Cain Show, telling them they hold federal immunity in the conduct of their duties and that anyone who obstructs them commits a felony.[4]

## Late January 2026: leadership change and continuing protests

Despite a reported thaw in state-federal relations — a private call between President [[donald-trump|Trump]] and Governor [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]] that both sides described favorably — protests continued in [[minneapolis|Minneapolis]] alongside ongoing federal immigration operations under Operation Metro Surge.[5] Trump announced that Border Czar [[tom-homan|Tom Homan]] would be sent to [[minnesota|Minnesota]] to take charge of federal immigration enforcement, on the same day that reports of Border Patrol commander [[gregory-bovino|Greg Bovino]]'s removal from Minneapolis circulated and were denied by [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]] spokesperson [[tricia-mclaughlin|Tricia McLaughlin]] and the [[white-house|White House]].[5] The officer-involved fatal shooting of [[alex-jeffrey-pretti|Alex Pretti]] dominated online discussion that Monday.[5]

## Federal enforcement package in Minnesota (early 2026)

By early 2026 the operation formed one element of an intensified set of federal investigations and enforcement actions in Minnesota covering immigration enforcement, potential obstruction by state officials, and alleged discriminatory housing practices.[6] The [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]], led by Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]], had by then arrested more than 10,000 undocumented immigrants in the Twin Cities under Operation Metro Surge, including thousands in recent weeks, many charged with serious crimes.[6] The operation drew widespread attention after the fatal shooting of U.S. citizen protester [[renee-nicole-good|Renee Nicole Good]] by an ICE officer, which triggered protests across the region.[6]

In a related move, the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] issued subpoenas to Governor [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]], Minneapolis Mayor [[jacob-frey|Jacob Frey]], Attorney General [[keith-ellison|Keith Ellison]] and other state and local officials as part of an investigation into whether they conspired to hinder federal immigration enforcement.[6] Separately, the [[department-of-housing-and-urban-development|Department of Housing and Urban Development]] opened a probe into [[minneapolis|Minneapolis]] housing programs, alleging that race-based prioritization for certain ethnic groups violates the [[fair-housing-act|Fair Housing Act]] and the Civil Rights Act, and the federal actions highlighted broader concerns about welfare fraud and corruption tied to state policies.[6]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the hysteria surrounding the story rests on accusations that the subpoenas are political retaliation, but noted that they were issued by a grand jury rather than by the administration — an independent examination of the facts within the current judicial construct — concluding that "no one is above the law."[6]
