# Missouri v. Biden

Missouri v. Biden is a federal lawsuit alleging that officials of the United States government unlawfully coordinated with social media platforms to suppress the speech of Americans, in violation of the First Amendment.[1]

## Role in the 2023 weaponization hearing

The case featured prominently in the July 2023 hearing of the House [[house-weaponization-committee|Select Subcommittee on the Weaponization of the Federal Government]], which was convened to examine the federal government's role in censoring Americans, Big Tech's collusion with out-of-control government agencies to silence speech, and the ongoing litigation itself.[1] [[d-john-sauer|D. John Sauer]], special assistant attorney general for Louisiana and an attorney for the plaintiffs, testified at the hearing alongside [[robert-f-kennedy-jr|Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]], Breitbart journalist [[emma-jo-morris|Emma-Jo Morris]] and [[maya-wiley|Maya Wiley]].[1]

## July 2023 injunction and appeal

U.S. District Judge [[terry-doughty|Terry Doughty]], a [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] nominee, issued a 155-page opinion holding that federal officials likely violated the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] in how they had communicated with [[twitter|Twitter]], [[meta-platforms|Meta]], and other technology giants about removing or restricting content related to the pandemic, the 2020 election, and other political subjects.[2] The resulting order would block federal agencies and officials from communicating with social media companies about content appearing on their platforms.[2]

On Wednesday, July 5, 2023, the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] said it was appealing the ruling and filed a notice of appeal with the [[fifth-circuit-court-of-appeals|5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals]] in New Orleans, framing the injunction as a restriction on the Biden administration's efforts to stem the spread of misinformation.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the judge in the case had denied the plaintiffs' motion to enjoin and constrain the [[food-and-drug-administration|FDA]] from colluding with social media companies to censor speech, meaning the agency could still work with platforms to ban users sharing dissenting medical research.[2] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the administration's appeal as evidence of its public disdain for the First Amendment while praising Doughty's ruling as a "white pill."[2]

## Immediate consequences of the injunction

One day after [[terry-doughty|Judge Terry Doughty]] set limits on the Biden administration's communications with tech firms, the [[state-department|State Department]] cancelled its regular monthly meeting with [[facebook|Facebook]] officials, telling the company that all future meetings were "canceled pending further guidance."[4] Hours later the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] filed a notice of appeal and said it expected to request a stay of the district court's decision "expeditiously."[4] Coverage of the disruption noted that these meetings had previously flagged suspected foreign influence operations for the platforms to investigate, and that similar sessions involving [[cisa|CISA]] and other companies were expected to be affected.[4] Doughty's opinion described the government's conduct as a massive attack on the First Amendment, possibly the most extensive in the nation's history, and found evidence of "a massive effort by Defendants, from the [[white-house|White House]] to federal agencies, to suppress speech based on its content."[4]

## The Independence Day temporary restraining order

The order issued by Judge [[terry-doughty|Terry Doughty]] of the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Louisiana on Tuesday, July 4, 2023 restricted the Biden administration from communicating with social media platforms about broad swaths of online content, a ruling that could curtail efforts to combat what the government called false and misleading narratives about the coronavirus pandemic and other issues.[5] The New York Times described the order as carrying significant [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] implications and as a major development in the legal fight over the boundaries of online speech.[5] Doughty wrote that parts of the government, including the [[department-of-health-and-human-services|Department of Health and Human Services]] and the [[fbi|Federal Bureau of Investigation]], could not talk to social media companies for "the purpose of urging, encouraging, pressuring, or inducing in any manner the removal, deletion, suppression, or reduction of content containing protected free speech."[5] The ruling was seen as a victory for Republicans who had accused platforms such as [[facebook|Facebook]], [[twitter|Twitter]] and YouTube of disproportionately removing right-leaning content in collaboration with government, while Democrats maintained the platforms had failed to police misinformation and hateful speech.[5]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that the Missouri Attorney General had been releasing evidence of the federal government working with private companies to censor Americans for months before the ruling.[5] She catalogued the eight explicit prohibitions in the order, which covered HHS, NIAID, the CDC, the U.S. Census Bureau, the FBI and the [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] — including administrators, staff, deputies and named principals — several members of the Executive Office of the President including press secretary [[karine-jean-pierre|Karine Jean-Pierre]] and what appeared to be the entire [[white-house|White House]] strategic communications team, [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]] and Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] explicitly, [[cybersecurity-and-infrastructure-security-agency|CISA]], and the [[state-department|State Department]].[5] The judge nevertheless denied the temporary restraining order as to the [[food-and-drug-administration|FDA]], the Election Assistance Commission, the U.S. Department of Commerce, several individuals, and the [[disinformation-governance-board|Disinformation Governance Board]] and [[nina-jankowicz|Nina Jankowicz]], and also denied the plaintiffs' motion to certify the case as a class action.[5]

Quoting the opinion, Ashe highlighted Doughty's statement that "if the allegations made by plaintiffs are true, the present case arguably involves the most massive attack against free speech in United States' history," and that plaintiffs were "likely to succeed on the merits in establishing that the government has used its power to silence the opposition."[5] She placed the ruling against a broader backdrop of collapsing institutional trust, citing [[fox-news|Fox News]]'s settlement with [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]] and the reported firing of [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]] as part of that deal.[5] Even that loss proved useful, she argued, because it revealed Fox's motives to newly awakened viewers and exposed the "safest and most secure election" narrative as a lie — pointing to Dominion director of product strategy and security [[eric-coomer|Eric Coomer]]'s private admissions that the company's systems were "just riddled with bugs," that "our products suck," and that almost all of Dominion's technological failings were due to installation errors.[5] Americans had been censored for saying exactly what Coomer said privately, she wrote, and Missouri v. Biden was the case taking on that censorship machine.[5]
