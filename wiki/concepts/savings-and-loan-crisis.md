# Savings and Loan Crisis

The savings and loan crisis of the 1980s was the wave of thrift insolvencies and taxpayer-funded bailouts that Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] describes as the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression and "the biggest bank robbery in US history."[1] In Lange's account the crisis was not an accident of policy but a planned operation: a nationwide money laundering scheme enabled by [[deregulation|deregulation]] of the thrift industry, run for the benefit of the [[shadow-government|shadow government]] and paid for by the American taxpayer.[1]

## Origins

When [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]] took office in 1981 he inherited an economy struggling with high inflation, which [[paul-volcker|Paul Volcker]] at the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] was fighting by raising interest rates.[1] Savings and loans, restricted to residential lending funded by customer deposits, were badly squeezed by those rates.[1] Reagan made regulatory relief one of four "pillars" of his 1981 economic recovery agenda, establishing the [[task-force-on-regulatory-relief|Presidential Task Force on Regulatory Relief]] chaired by Vice President [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] and creating OIRA to review new rules.[1]

The resulting changes let thrifts make commercial and unsecured business loans, invest heavily in commercial real estate, and finance such property 100 percent with no money down, under far more lenient standards than before.[1] At the same time federal [[deposit-insurance|deposit insurance]] on individual accounts was raised from $40,000 to $100,000 in 1980, guaranteeing that the new "entrepreneurial" owners of S&Ls took no personal risk when gambling with depositors' money; the government became the ultimate safety net and, in Martin Mayer's framing, the industry became a giant Ponzi scheme.[1]

## Mechanics of the looting

Lange argues the crisis was not primarily outright embezzlement but small groups of friends who found a way to loot the Treasury — making loans to each other, investing in each other's companies, and financing each other's ventures without personal risk.[1] A signature technique was the [[land-flip|land flip]], in which a parcel was sold repeatedly among associates at escalating prices, the inflated valuation then serving as collateral for a much larger loan from another thrift that was never intended to be repaid.[1] Because real estate prices are easy to manipulate and the sector is less tightly regulated than banking, property transactions doubled as an ideal [[real-estate-money-laundering|money laundering]] channel.[1]

## Cases

The Bush family's own involvement was extensive. [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]] and partner [[armando-codina|Armando Codina]] bought a Miami office building through a loan from [[broward-federal-savings-and-loan|Broward Federal Savings and Loan]] that a third partner, [[j-edward-houston|J. Edward Houston]], defaulted on; the thrift's failure cost taxpayers roughly $235 million while Bush and Codina settled for $505,000 and kept the building.[1] [[neil-bush|Neil Bush]] sat as a director of [[silverado-savings-and-loan-association|Silverado]] in Denver while approving loans to his own benefactors [[william-walters|William Walters]] and [[kenneth-good|Kenneth Good]], whose defaults helped produce a bailout expected to cost $1 billion.[1] [[sunrise-savings-and-loan|Sunrise Savings]] of Boynton Beach failed in 1985 at a cost of $680 million after Vice President Bush personally reprimanded a regulator investigating it.[1]

Other central figures included Louisiana organized-crime associate [[herman-k-beebe|Herman K. Beebe]], who at various times controlled 55 state banks and 29 thrifts and was linked to as much as $10 billion in reckless loans; [[mario-renda|Mario Renda]], who directed roughly $5 billion a year in deposits into 130 thrifts, every one of which later failed; and [[robert-corson|Robert Corson]], described in federal law enforcement records as a known money launderer, whose [[vision-banc-savings|Vision Banc Savings]] went insolvent four months after purchase.[1] Texas had the most S&L failures of any state.[1]

## Cover story

Lange contends the public narrative of the scandal was deliberately concentrated on [[charles-keating|Charles Keating]] and the [[keating-five|Keating Five]] campaign-finance affair, a "red herring" that kept the country debating campaign money for years while the underlying money laundering went unexamined; none of the five members of Congress suffered consequences.[1]

## Purpose

Several writers cited in the article treat the episode as a premeditated conspiracy to move funds out of the country for use by the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]], with many of the dodgiest figures connected to Mafia families, the agency, or both.[1] Institutions such as [[palmer-national-bank|Palmer National Bank]] — seeded with $2.8 million from Beebe and holding the account of the [[national-endowment-for-the-preservation-of-liberty|National Endowment for the Preservation of Liberty]] — channeled money to [[oliver-north|Oliver North]]'s [[iran-contra-affair|Iran-Contra]] network, supporting the claim that thrift deregulation helped pay for covert operations Congress would not fund.[1] Estimates of the money that vanished from S&Ls during the 1980s range from $20 billion to $50 billion or more, with no one certain of the true figure.[1]


## Reference in "Profiting From War"

"Profiting From War" describes [[jeb-bush|Jeb Bush]] as involved in the Savings and Loan scandal, which it characterizes as having stolen taxpayer money through a massive federal bailout while his father [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]] was president.[2] The article separately notes that [[neil-bush|Neil Bush]] was best known for the corrupt practices at Colorado's [[silverado-savings-and-loan-association|Silverado Savings & Loan]], where he served as a director during the 1980s.[2]
