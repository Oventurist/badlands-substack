# Paramount–Warner Bros. Discovery Merger

The Paramount–Warner Bros. Discovery merger is a proposed $111 billion acquisition of [[warner-bros-discovery|Warner Bros. Discovery]] by [[paramount-global|Paramount]], announced against a backdrop of heavy Gulf sovereign-wealth financing and fierce opposition from [[hollywood|Hollywood]] creatives.[1]

## Origins: 2023 merger talks

Talks between the two companies date to December 2023, when *Variety* confirmed that Warner Bros. Discovery CEO [[david-zaslav|David Zaslav]] had met Paramount Global CEO [[bob-bakish|Bob Bakish]] at a lunch meeting in New York to discuss a possible merger, with Axios first reporting the discussions.[3] Zaslav also spoke with Shari Redstone, whose National Amusements Inc. owns a controlling stake in Paramount Global, about a potential combination.[3] Terms could not be learned; both companies enlisted bankers, but the status of the talks was described as very preliminary and representatives of both firms declined to comment.[3]

At the end of the third quarter of 2023, Paramount Global reported long-term debt of $15.6 billion, considerably less than WBD's $43.5 billion, while WBD was the larger company by market value with a $28.4 billion market capitalization as of the close on December 20 against Paramount's $10.3 billion.[3] The two companies were looking to pool assets spanning TV, film, sports and streaming to gain greater scale and operational efficiencies, with particular interest in combining the Max and Paramount+ premium streaming services to better compete with [[netflix|Netflix]] and [[disney|Disney]]'s Disney+ and Hulu.[3]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] responded to the 2023 talks by restating a macroeconomic maxim he had offered a year earlier about Fortune 500 consolidation: "Consolidation precedes collapse."[3] He argued the trend was accelerating, and that blockbuster mega-deals tossing around massive numbers imply economic viability but obscure the underlying reality — a smaller company seeking to merge with a larger one does so not because it wants to but because it has to, the product of an accounting shell game in which firms "worth" tens of billions operate on single-digit margins and then reap market backlash to sub-par offerings.[3] He predicted many more such headlines heading into 2024.[3]

## Financing

The deal is being heavily financed by Gulf sovereign wealth funds, with [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]]'s [[public-investment-fund|Public Investment Fund]] committing approximately $12 billion as part of a $24 billion equity package alongside Qatari and Abu Dhabi investors.[1] The funds are structured as passive stakes to ease U.S. regulatory review.[1] The Saudi commitment is described as the latest chapter in Riyadh's aggressive, multi-billion-dollar push into Hollywood under Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]]'s [[vision-2030|Vision 2030]] diversification plan, which also includes a PIF-led $55 billion acquisition of [[electronic-arts|Electronic Arts]] in 2025, full control of MBC Group, a $1 billion studio partnership with Arena SNK, and funding for domestic film production and festivals such as the Riyadh Comedy Festival, Soundstorm, and the Red Sea International Film Festival.[1]

## Opposition

On April 13, 2026, more than 1,000 writers, actors, and directors — including Bryan Cranston, Joaquin Phoenix, Tiffany Haddish, Lily Gladstone, Denis Villeneuve, and J.J. Abrams — signed an open letter opposing the acquisition.[1] Organized by advocacy groups including the [[committee-for-the-first-amendment|Committee for the First Amendment]] and the Future Film Coalition, the letter warned that the deal would accelerate industry consolidation, producing fewer films, narrower story diversity, reduced job opportunities for creators, higher production costs, and less choice for global audiences.[1] Paramount defended the deal, saying it would preserve independent creative leadership and increase theatrical output, while Saudi officials and backers framed the broader strategy as soft-power rebranding, domestic entertainment for a young population, and political influence in Washington.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the most important front in the info war is American culture, and that the Cultural Arm of what he calls the System of Systems has always been its most seismic weapon because it translates raw power into perceived legitimacy in the subconscious of the observer, codifying a counterfeit morality through fiction that burrows beneath conscious skepticism.[1] He wrote that people stopped believing the news long ago but still crave the dream and the archetype, which is why the machine poured its darkest energies into a cultural fifth-generation offensive.[1] In his reading, the Trump-allied Ellisons' consolidation of a legacy studio powerhouse through Paramount Skydance would alone be a strategic threat to legacy Hollywood, but the addition of tens of billions in Saudi and allied Gulf sovereign capital — extending into sports and gaming — makes the panic pattern unmistakable and a [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] maneuver.[1] He concluded that the signatories do not fear consolidation in the abstract but this consolidation, and do not dread foreign capital on principle but capital that refuses to genuflect to their inverted morality.[1]

## Senate demand for FCC investigation (March 2026)

On March 23, 2026, a group of Democratic U.S. senators led by [[cory-booker|Cory Booker]] of New Jersey — and including [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] and Elizabeth Warren — sent a letter to the [[federal-communications-commission|FCC]] demanding a full and independent investigation into the $111 billion merger.[2] The senators raised national-security and foreign-influence concerns over the financing, which includes roughly $24 billion from Middle Eastern sovereign wealth funds — the [[saudi-public-investment-fund|Saudi Public Investment Fund]], the Qatar Investment Authority, and the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority — and $1 billion in equity from China's [[tencent|Tencent]].[2]

The letter noted that the investments are structured as non-voting equity, often routed through firms such as Apollo Global Management, apparently to sidestep mandatory review by the Committee on Foreign Investments in the United States (CFIUS).[2] Senators warned that these stakes could give foreign governments leverage over editorial decisions, content licensing, and strategic priorities at major U.S. news outlets owned by the merged entity, including CBS News and CNN, and specifically flagged risks tied to Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] and to Chinese national-security laws compelling technology companies to cooperate with Beijing's intelligence services.[2] They urged the FCC to reject Paramount Skydance's claim that the foreign-investment portion requires only minimal scrutiny, calling instead for rigorous review under the Communications Act in coordination with the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]], CFIUS, and intelligence agencies.[2] Paramount Skydance downplayed the concerns, asserting that the financing structure poses no governance or control issues.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the senators' intervention as the establishment telegraphing what it truly fears: that Middle Eastern partners already flowing into gaming and sports are now positioning within the final major theater of narrative control.[2] In seeking to stop this convergence, he argued, the establishment reveals itself as a "Protectorate" that wants permanent cultural and geopolitical fracture rather than normalization.[2]

## Netflix deal and Paramount's hostile bid (December 2025)

The contest for [[warner-bros-discovery|Warner Bros. Discovery]] came to a head in December 2025.[4] On Friday, December 5, [[netflix|Netflix]] announced an $83 billion agreement, approved by both boards, to acquire a large part of the company, excluding its cable channels.[4] On Monday, December 8, [[paramount-global|Paramount]] mounted a hostile takeover bid, going around the Warner board directly to shareholders with an all-cash offer of $30 per share — valuing the company at roughly $108 billion including debt — and covering the entire business, including the Warner Bros. studio, HBO Max and cable channels such as CNN.[4]

Paramount justified the maneuver by asserting that the board was "pursuing an inferior proposal" that would face "a challenging regulatory approval process," while chief executive [[david-ellison|David Ellison]] said the offer would "create a stronger Hollywood" and serve "the creative community, consumers and the movie theater industry."[4]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] laid out two scenarios: a Netflix win yielding a hyper-centralized behemoth pairing the streaming platform with Warner's intellectual-property library and further narrowing the range of permissible narrative, or a Paramount close in which [[jared-kushner|Kushner]]-linked Gulf capital, tech sovereignty via Ellison, global sports assets and roughly $6 billion in synergies would crack Hollywood's unipolar structure under what he called multipolar gravity.[4]

## Renewed pursuit in September 2025

In September 2025, five weeks after [[skydance-media|Skydance Media]] closed its $8 billion takeover of [[paramount-global|Paramount Global]], [[david-ellison|David Ellison]] was reported to be weighing a deal then worth more than $70 billion to acquire [[warner-bros-discovery|Warner Bros. Discovery]] in its entirety — moving even before the merged Paramount Skydance had finished laying off upwards of 2,000 employees.[5] Variety analysis concluded that the motive for the speed was to beat rival suitors to the punch.[5]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the prospective merger as a front in a broader cultural inversion, arguing that the Ellisons were "storming the Media and Entertainment Industrial Complexes" and that a successful bid would bring [[hbo|HBO]], [[cnn|CNN]], Cartoon Network, DC, Harry Potter and Game of Thrones under new stewardship, on top of the [[cbs|CBS]], MTV, BET and Nickelodeon assets already secured through Paramount.[5] He linked the maneuver to the [[ufc|UFC]]'s nearly $8 billion Paramount broadcasting deal beginning in 2026 and to whispers of pursuits of TikTok's U.S. operations, whose timing alongside an impending Trump–Xi Jinping meeting he said made "a mockery of coincidence."[5]
