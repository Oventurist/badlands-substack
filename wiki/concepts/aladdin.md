# Aladdin (BlackRock)

**Aladdin** is the risk-management and portfolio-analysis platform operated by the asset manager [[blackrock|BlackRock]]. In Badlands Media commentary it is cited as evidence of how long [[artificial-intelligence|artificial intelligence]] systems have been operating in the background of the global economy and how much control such systems have already accumulated.[1]

## Treatment in Badlands commentary

The April 2023 essay "Don't Bring Shiny Rocks to Digital Gunfights" invoked Aladdin at the point where its author argued that the moment of need for AI countermeasures may already lie in the past.[1] Directing readers to a video highlighting the tool, [[patriots-in-progress|Patriots in Progress]] wrote that viewers "should quickly see how long AI has been in the works and how much control some AI tools have already amassed."[1]

The reference functions in the essay as a bridge between two claims: that AI-generated data will progressively overwhelm the public's capacity to distinguish reality from fabrication, and that mitigation must begin immediately rather than at some hypothetical future threshold.[1] The proposed mitigation is the imposition of "severe physical costs" on machine-generated data by pairing it with auditable energy expenditure recorded on [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]].[1]

## Predictive simulation and the housing thesis

In a November 2024 essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] described Aladdin as a supercomputer BlackRock built in the 1990s that allows the firm to run massive computations and an enormous number of simulations, arguing that at sufficient scale simulation "can almost predict future events with pinpoint accuracy."[2] He offered the tool as one of two possible explanations for BlackRock's purchases of residential property at historically high prices: either the firm was told in advance by "the people who control the world" that interest rates would be raised in 2022, or it deduced the coming rate rise from Aladdin's simulations.[2] On either reading, foreknowledge of a rate shock made buying rental housing rational despite elevated prices, since higher rates would push would-be buyers into the rental market.[2]

## Wenatchee supercomputer account (Carlson)

Badlands contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] has written that Aladdin runs on a supercomputer built in the Eastern Washington farming community of Wenatchee in the 1990s, tucked in among the town's apple orchard warehouses.[3] In his description Aladdin is a Monte Carlo simulation, meaning the machine can run endless scenarios in seconds and thereby predict future events or likelihoods to the point of "almost literally predicting the future."[3] Carlson credits this predictive capacity with transforming [[blackrock|BlackRock]] from a fairly insignificant investment company into the largest holder of assets in the world, passing the centuries-old "blue blood" families in the process — families he suspects were themselves pumping money into BlackRock, which he offers as an explanation for why the Rockefellers and Rothschilds do not appear on lists of the world's richest people.[3] He cites the system as evidence that technology far in advance of what the public is shown has been quietly shaping the world for decades.[3]

## Origins, Monte Carlo method and commercial licensing (Carlson, 2023)

In an April 12, 2023 essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] gave a fuller account of Aladdin's origins: [[blackrock|BlackRock]] built a supercomputer in the 1990s in [[wenatchee-washington|Wenatchee, Washington]], a city of roughly 35,000 people situated among apple orchard warehouses and processing facilities, a location he assumed was chosen for isolation combined with access to adequate power grids.[4] On that machine the firm created Aladdin, a software system that "can essentially predict future outcomes" by means of a [[monte-carlo-simulation|Monte Carlo simulator]] running massive numbers of scenarios to identify the most likely result.[4] BlackRock first used it internally to pick stocks and other investments, and in 1999 made the software available to other companies for a fee.[4]

Carlson described a widening range of licensees. Insurance companies used Aladdin to estimate the risk of premature death, informing whom to insure and what premiums to charge, while other investment firms used it to allocate capital, with the effect that the financial system as a whole became progressively more predictable.[4] Investment banks, believing they understood their risks better, grew comfortable taking on more risk in pursuit of greater reward.[4]

## Aladdin and the 2008 crisis

In Carlson's telling this worked for a period — markets grew substantially — until [[2008-financial-crisis|2008]], when many investment banking firms were left holding risky portfolios as everything collapsed.[4] He compared the dynamic to modern weather forecasting: accuracy breeds overconfidence, so that a forecast of no rain leaves people without a jacket or umbrella when a storm arrives.[4] Over-reliance on artificial intelligence, he argued, is what caught the firms "with their pants down," compounded by [[too-big-to-fail|"too big to fail"]] expectations of a government bailout and by bankers' growing conviction that caution merely ate away at profits.[4]
