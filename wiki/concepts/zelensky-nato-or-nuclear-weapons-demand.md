# Zelensky NATO-or-Nuclear-Weapons Demand

In October 2024, Ukrainian President [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] told European leaders that Ukraine must either join [[nato|NATO]] or obtain nuclear weapons, a demand that startled allies and followed a lackluster response to his "victory plan" from US officials in Washington the previous month.[1]

## The statement

Zelensky made the comments while presenting the victory plan before the European Union's European Council in Brussels.[1] He referenced a recent private conversation with Republican presidential candidate [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], telling lawmakers that he had said Ukraine needs "some kind of alliance" or would be "forced to pursue nuclear weapons."[1]

"In a conversation with Donald Trump I said – this is our situation: What way out do we have? Either Ukraine will have nuclear weapons, which for us will be a defense, or we'll need to have some sort of alliance, besides NATO. But today we know of no other alliance," Zelensky said.[1] He continued: "NATO countries today are not at war. NATO countries are not fighting. In NATO countries people are still alive. Thank God. That is why we choose NATO, not nuclear weapons. And Donald Trump heard me. He said you have a just argument."[1]

Zelensky invoked the [[budapest-memorandum|Budapest Memorandum]], the 1994 agreement under which Ukraine gave up its Soviet-era nuclear arsenal in exchange for security guarantees from Russia, the United States and the United Kingdom.[1] He asked rhetorically, "Which nuclear states suffered? None except Ukraine… Who gave up their nuclear weapons? All of them? No. Only Ukraine… Who is fighting today? Ukraine."[1]

## Reactions and commentary

Former Russian president and Security Council deputy chairman [[dmitry-medvedev|Dmitry Medvedev]] claimed that Ukraine was working on a dirty bomb, saying the Zelensky government "has everything necessary for that: resources, technology and specialists."[1]

Badlands Media's commentary mocked the demand as Zelensky, who "has no leverage," making demands of the author of *The Art of the Deal*.[1] The analysis linked the nuclear framing to the parallel Iran–Israel storyline, arguing it would appear absurd for Ukraine to be granted a nuclear weapon after years of alarm over the prospect of Iran obtaining one, and anticipated the Ukraine and Israel theaters of war formally converging in the mainstream narrative.[1]
