# Critical Infrastructure

"Critical infrastructure" is the federal designation covering the sectors whose disruption would gravely damage national security, public health, or the economy — water and wastewater, energy, natural gas, communications, banking, manufacturing, and, since 2017, election systems. In the United States the [[cisa|Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency]] holds the coordinating role across all of them.[1]

## Sector interdependency

Testimony at the February 6, 2024 [[house-committee-on-homeland-security|House Homeland Security Committee]] hearing on the water sector repeatedly emphasized that the sectors are interlocked. [[charles-clancy|Charles Clancy]] of [[mitre-corporation|MITRE]] said he was "particularly concerned about the interdependencies between several of the different critical infrastructure sectors. You hit energy, water goes down shortly thereafter. Same thing with natural gas … if you have a significant attack on one, you can cause cascading of failures and others."[1] [[kevin-morley|Kevin Morley]] echoed the concern for cascading degradation of drinking water and wastewater services.[1] [[marty-edwards|Marty Edwards]] added a hardware dimension: the same programmable logic controllers sit in water treatment plants, electrical substations, and manufacturing plants.[1]

## Centralization and complexity

The Badlands account advanced a general thesis from the hearing: centralized technical control of critical infrastructure has created new complexities, and "complexity breeds and hides corruption" — either corruption of security protocols that leaves infrastructure vulnerable, or corruption in the conventional sense of crony capitalism, sweetheart deals, and non-competitive contracts.[1] It pointed to the exchange in which witnesses said the [[biden-administration|Biden administration]]'s water cybersecurity sprint pushed "a government specific answer" with no resourcing behind it as a possible instance of both.[1]

The article extended the water sector's structural problems — foreign-made shared supply chains, [[operational-technology|operational technology]] monoculture, and two decades of what it called an authoritarian push to centralize infrastructure — to energy, banking, communications, and elections, and asked whether the resulting vulnerabilities are "a bug … or a feature."[1]

## Local control as counter-proposal

Morley argued for directing resources to local communities, local systems integrators, and local contractors rather than attempting to reach 50,000 separate entities from Washington, a position Representative [[august-pfluger|August Pfluger]] restated as making the effort "more local based and regional based."[1] The Badlands author noted that these community-control solutions closely resemble proposals that have been dismissed or demonized when advanced by activists in other sectors.[1]
