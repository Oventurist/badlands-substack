# Utah Constitutional Sovereignty Act

The Utah Constitutional Sovereignty Act (Utah SB0057) is a state law signed by Governor [[spencer-cox|Spencer Cox]] on January 31, 2024, that establishes a process by which the state may overrule or decline to enforce federal directives it deems unconstitutional intrusions on state sovereignty.[1]

## Provisions

The Act establishes a framework under which the Utah Legislature, acting by concurrent resolution, may prohibit the enforcement of a federal directive within the state by government officers if the Legislature determines the directive violates the principles of state sovereignty.[1] The statute describes the ways in which a federal directive may be found to violate those principles, limits who may request such a concurrent resolution, requires the Legislature to consult the state attorney general on the potential litigation impact of any resolution, and requires notice to representatives of tribal governments.[1]

## Reception and constitutional questions

Cox defended the measure in a statement, saying that "balancing power between state and federal sovereignty is an essential part of our constitutional system" and that the legislation "gives us another way to push back on federal overreach and maintain that balance."[1] Critics countered that the law conflicts with the Constitution's Supremacy Clause, under which federal law takes precedence over state law.[1] Robert Keiter, a law professor at the University of Utah's SJ Quinney College of Law, told CNN he was skeptical the Act was constitutional, saying it "sends the message, and the Utah legislature is famous for sending messages of this sort, that it's unhappy with the federal government," expressed "in a way that is constitutionally problematic."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the legal analysts quoted in coverage of the law conspicuously failed to mention the [[tenth-amendment|Tenth Amendment]], which reserves to the states or the people all powers not delegated to the federal government.[1] She contended that where the federal government exceeds its constitutional authority, states have both a right and a duty to assert sovereignty, and that Utah's need to legislate the principle in 2024 was itself "evidence that the federal government is absolutely out of control."[1] She rejected the characterization of the Act as merely symbolic, noting that the same commentary invoked the Texas border case and the Colorado Fourteenth Amendment ballot challenges as live examples of states asserting such authority, and closed with a quotation from James Madison's Federalist No. 10.[1]
