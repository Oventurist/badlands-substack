# Manifest Destiny

**Manifest Destiny** is the American doctrine holding that the young republic possessed a continental destiny to expand and to serve as a model of liberty. In the Badlands Media essay "Avoiding WWIII," [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] argues that the doctrine should not be discarded because "evil perverts justified evil things using them," since great moral heroes also acted against imperialism and the Anglo-American deep state in its name.[1]

## Republican origins

Ehret traces the earliest expression of Manifest Destiny to John Winthrop's 1630 "City on a Hill" speech, in which America's identity was to serve as a beacon and role model of liberty for other nations to be inspired by.[1] The theme was later amplified by [[george-washington|George Washington]] and [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], who explicitly wanted America's first army and congress to be called "Continental" rather than the "congress of the 13 colonies" or "army of the 13 colonies."[1] When Washington led the Continental Congress, the Americas were dominated by hereditary systems of empire led by French, English, Russian and Spanish oligarchical systems that controlled everything but the 13 colonies.[1]

Washington understood that harmony with Natural Law did not make the republic immune from becoming another evil empire, and so warned against the dual evils of foreign entanglements abroad and party politics at home.[1] [[john-quincy-adams|John Quincy Adams]] extended the idea by drafting the [[monroe-doctrine|Monroe Doctrine]], which he believed could work only if America ventured "not abroad, in search of monsters to destroy."[1]

## The perverted version

Ehret contends that the forces shaping American expansion during its first 125 years too often failed this test. The USA grew from 13 backward colonies in 1776 to 45 industrially advanced states by 1900, but the anti-slavery voices of Benjamin Franklin, John Jay, John Quincy Adams, [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]], Charles Sumner, William Seward and [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]] were repeatedly subverted by an anglophile [[deep-state|Deep State]] parasite class running both Wall Street in the north and the southern slave power.[1] Where abolitionist figures such as Franklin and Alexander Hamilton encouraged the sharing of knowledge, technical skills, science and technological progress with blacks and natives without forced religious conversion, the Deep State sought expansion only through "the conquerors whip."[1]

The southern perversion of Manifest Destiny, promoted by [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]], Jefferson Davis and Albert Pike, envisioned expanded black slavery and Native Americans crushed under a "superior" white race and confined to plantations or reservations.[1] Jackson's 1830 Indian Removal Act and the resulting [[trail-of-tears|Trail of Tears]] emptied valuable lands that were handed to southern cotton planters, deepening the free-versus-slave tension that produced the Civil War of 1861–65.[1]

Legitimate defenders of Manifest Destiny, Ehret argues, grew territory through diplomacy — the Louisiana Purchase of 1804, the Oregon Territory in 1848, and the Alaska Purchase of 1867 — while the "America" of Wall Street and Virginia's slave power preferred to pick fights with neighbors, as in the Mexican War of 1846–48 and the 1893 overthrow of Hawaii's monarchy.[1]

## Eurasian revival

Ehret argues that a new form of Manifest Destiny has migrated eastward. Russia's 21st-century version aims to extend civilization into the Siberian Far East and Arctic and beyond Central Asia, Mongolia, Japan and China, merging since 2018 with the [[polar-silk-road|Polar Silk Road]].[1] China's version is the [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]], unveiled in 2013.[1] The essay concludes that it is a "rich irony" that the spirit of John Quincy Adams' security doctrine is alive in the world — "just not in the U.S."[1]
