# Money Laundering

Money laundering is a financial crime that involves concealing the origin of money obtained from illegal activities — such as drug trafficking, corruption, embezzlement or gambling — by converting it into funds with an apparently legal source.[1] Criminals, including terrorist organizations, attempt to disguise the proceeds, sources or nature of their illicit activities through the practice.[1]

## Mechanics

The process is conventionally described in three stages: the underlying illegal activity places money in the launderer's hands; the money is then passed through a complex system of banking transfers or other transactions that generate an appearance of legitimacy; and the now-cleaned proceeds re-enter the financial system. Money laundering facilitates a broad range of serious underlying criminal offenses and ultimately threatens the integrity of the financial system itself.[1]

Large-scale laundering requires banks. Financial institutions are needed to make the transfers and transactions that legitimize illicit cash, which in turn threatens the integrity of those institutions.[1] It also requires front companies, a large volume of transactions to obscure the origin of funds, and operators with the knowledge, skill and experience to evade banking and anti-money-laundering law. The process is made far easier when banks are themselves complicit in the underlying criminal activity.[1]

## The intelligence dimension

Badlands Media contributor Joe Lange argues that the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] has run the largest money laundering operation in the world, servicing a [[shadow-government]] that profited from global drug trafficking and weapons sales.[1] In this account the agency did not merely tolerate launderers but created them: [[manuel-artime]] recruited the accountant [[ramon-milian-rodriguez]] in 1972 and arranged CIA training for him in accounting, after which Milian Rodriguez laundered billions of dollars for the [[medellin-cartel]].[1] Purpose-built institutions such as [[castle-bank-and-trust]] in the Bahamas and the [[nugan-hand-bank]] in Australia served as conduits for covert operational funds and drug proceeds alike.[1]

Recurring jurisdictions in the Lange account are Panama, Curaçao, Switzerland, Hong Kong and the Bahamas — all places where Milian Rodriguez testified he moved cartel money.[1] Curaçao has repeatedly been named by the United States as a "Major Money Laundering Jurisdiction" in its International Narcotics Control Strategy Report, and is described there as a regional financial center and transshipment point for drugs from South America.[1] Switzerland's banking secrecy made it a hub used by [[richard-secord]] and Oliver North during the [[iran-contra-affair]].[1]

Laundering also occurred inside the United States. Milian Rodriguez testified that he laundered hundreds of millions of dollars in cocaine profits through US banks operating in Panama, and named Citicorp — the predecessor of [[citigroup]] — and [[bank-of-america]] as institutions whose representatives he dealt with off the official employment rolls.[1] He further testified that the cartel held some $11 billion in assets inside the United States as of 1983.[1]

## War as a laundering mechanism (Q drop #117)

Chapter 17 of the [[q-for-dummies|Q for Dummies]] series defines money laundering plainly as the process of cleaning the path from which money is derived — funneling illegally obtained money through a front so that the earnings appear legitimate.[2] It then applies the concept to Q drop #117's question about the single biggest event capable of generating payouts of billions from many nation states, answering: war.[2]

The chapter cites Ukraine as the contemporary example, arguing that billions raised and sent there are siphoned off by the [[deep-state|Deep State]], and answers Q's follow-up question of who audits the money by asserting that the same actors do, so that no true accountability for the funds exists.[2] It characterizes the arrangement as one gigantic money laundering operation built on American taxpayers, and closes on Q's final question — what money buys — with the answer: power.[2]

## Advertising and modern art as laundering vehicles (Carlson, 2024)

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]]'s September 17, 2024 Badlands Media essay argues that advertising is an ideal laundering mechanism because its effect on sales cannot be quantified, making billions in spending impossible to audit.[3] He applies the same logic to modern art — where value is purely subjective — and to social media and [[youtube|YouTube]] revenues, comparing the arrangement to the car wash used to launder Walter White's methamphetamine proceeds on *Breaking Bad*.[3] The funds requiring laundering, in his account, are money created out of nothing by the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] together with the proceeds of gun running, drug running and human trafficking he attributes to the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]].[3]
