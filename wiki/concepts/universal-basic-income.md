# Universal Basic Income

Universal basic income (UBI) is a policy under which a government provides recurring unconditional cash payments to individuals. In the United States the approach has been advanced principally in Democratic-run states, where legislatures have proposed handouts to low-income and homeless residents structured as UBI programs.[1]

## Oregon proposal (2023)

In early 2023 the Oregon legislature took up a bill that would pay homeless and low-income people $1,000 a month, establishing a People's Housing Assistance Fund Demonstration Program administered through the state's Department of Human Services.[1] According to Bill Track 50, the legislation would "provide 12 monthly payments of $1,000 to individuals who are experiencing homelessness, are at risk of homelessness, are severely rent burdened or earn at or below 60 percent of area median income."[1] The bill would also require a study of who received the money, broken down by demographic categories including race, veteran status and risk of domestic violence.[1] Fox News described the measure as the latest in a series of blue-state efforts to establish universal basic income programs.[1]

## Commentary

In the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[absolutetruth1776|AbsoluteTruth1776]] argued that the officials behind the Oregon bill would rather incentivize homelessness than make an honest attempt at correcting its underlying causes or help recipients find sustainable ways to improve their situation.[1] He contended that $1,000 per month is "just enough to make sure these people can continue supplying themselves with a substance of their choice," called the measure a terrible gesture that could only come from people who want to see the country burn, and urged that the homeless instead be offered jobs, housing and a new start.[1]

## Technocratic antecedents

Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] traces modern universal basic income advocacy to the "energy credits" proposed by [[howard-scott|Howard Scott]]'s [[technocracy-inc|Technocracy Inc.]] in the 1930s, which were to be assigned to all people regardless of employment once automation had rendered most jobs worthless.[2] Under that scheme no one could accumulate more credits than another, invest, start a business for profit or save, since any of these would produce inequality.[2] Ehret notes a parallel proposal in the Social Credit system of [[c-h-douglas|Major C. H. Douglas]], who argued that credits should be provided to all workers so they could afford the products of the firms employing them.[2]

He connects both to [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s remarks at the 2017 World Government Summit, where Musk said that "the much harder challenge is, how are people going to have meaning? A lot of people derive their meaning from their employment. So if there's no need for your labor, what's your meaning? Do you feel useless?" before adding that "there is a pretty good chance we end up with a universal basic income, or something like that, due to automation."[2] Ehret presents this as the technocratic management of what he calls useless eaters.[2]
