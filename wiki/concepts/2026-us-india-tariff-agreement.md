# 2026 US–India Tariff Agreement

The 2026 US–India tariff agreement was a trade recalibration announced by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] between January 30 and February 2, 2026, cutting the US reciprocal tariff on Indian imports from 25% to 18%, effective immediately, following a Monday phone call with Indian Prime Minister [[narendra-modi|Narendra Modi]].[1]

## Terms

The move also eliminated an additional 25% punitive tariff imposed in 2025 over [[india|India]]'s purchases of Russian crude oil, reducing the effective US tariff burden on Indian goods from as high as roughly 50% to 18%.[1] Modi publicly confirmed the new 18% rate.[1] As part of the agreement, India committed to halting purchases of Russian crude and shifting imports toward US suppliers and Venezuelan crude.[1] Trump further stated that India had agreed to reduce tariffs and non-tariff barriers on US products, describing the goal as movement toward zero and resolving the 2025 US–India trade dispute tied to Russian energy imports.[1]

## Venezuelan and Chinese dimension

In parallel, Trump said the United States welcomes Chinese and Indian investment in [[venezuela|Venezuela]]'s oil sector, making the remarks to reporters aboard Air Force One around January 31–February 1.[1] He stated that [[china|China]] "is welcome" to make deals in Venezuela and that India is "coming in" to buy Venezuelan oil. Venezuela holds the world's largest proven oil reserves, though production has long been constrained by sanctions and political instability; Trump characterized the approach as permitting third-country investment and purchases as part of a broader energy realignment.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the agreement through the [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] framework, arguing that India functions as a "bridge state" — a sovereign-coded relay that massages deals and offers the public a pragmatic on-ramp to an order already forming.[1] On the surface, through what he termed the "NeoCon goggles" imposed by the media, the deal reads as classic Trumpian hardball in which India capitulates on Russian oil to choke Moscow's energy lifeline; yet in the same breath Trump welcomed Chinese investment in Venezuelan oil fields.[1] The result, in this reading, is not isolationist decoupling but "sovereign acceleration": threading energy and capital flows that bind India more tightly to both American renewal and Chinese partnership while using antagonistic framing toward Russia as a translation layer.[1]
