# Government in the Sunshine Act

The Government in the Sunshine Act, enacted as Public Law 94-409, is a United States open-meetings statute signed into law in 1976 during the fallout from the [[watergate|Watergate]] scandal involving former President [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]], who had conspired with what Badlands Media described as various shady actors to spy on and record meetings in the Oval Office.[1] The statute is commonly referred to simply as "the Sunshine Act."[1]

## Text and scope

The act provides, with ten specified exemptions, that "every portion of every meeting of an agency shall be open to public observation," a formulation codified at 5 U.S.C. 552b(b) and quoted in case number 99-1383, *Natural Resources Defense Council v. NRC*.[1] In effect the act requires officeholders to disclose to the public the communications that occur during meetings "of an agency," with agency meetings understood as any communications between agency officeholders relating to agency business.[1]

Badlands Media contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that the reach of the act is broader than is commonly assumed: while agency business ordinarily occurs within agency walls or among agency personnel, it also encompasses any meeting between an official and any other person when the subject matter can reasonably be construed as agency business.[1] His illustrative case was a Secretary of Defense discussing how to manage an agency issue with a friend whose advice, opinions or insight materially influences the Secretary's job — a conversation Deschamps held to be official business of the US government and therefore within the purview of the Sunshine Act, such that the people must know what was discussed so they can intervene as needed.[1]

## Trusteeship rationale

Deschamps traced the statute's principle to a much older concept of trusteeship, which he said has been almost totally lost on most people in the modern age, a loss he blamed on a century of dumbed-down education.[1] He wrote that in the era of the founding fathers the need for public oversight of elected officials was so vital that its absence would have been unthinkable, yet more than 250 years later most citizens do not even consider that they have no window into the affairs of their elected officials and therefore no way to tactically manage intrusions into their lives as free people.[1]

To make the point concrete he offered a pair of domestic analogies: a parent would not accept being excluded from a discussion between a spouse and a child's teacher about what the child is taught, and a parent who saw a hired babysitter attempting to feed poison to their child would have the right to intervene and impose their will, because the babysitter's stewardship rests on a trust that unilateral action would violate criminally.[1] Citizens' rights with respect to government, he argued, are no different, and the people can and should recall their rights out of the hands of any elected official who violates the spirit of a valid trustee.[1] He grounded the underlying right in free will, writing that as free will-endowed beings people have the right to self-direct their lives, so that anything affecting a person, their rights, or their property is something they have the right to know about and negotiate over — an unalienable, God-given right that must be reflected in the legal system of any nation claiming to be lawful.[1]

## Alleged circumvention

Deschamps contended that the [[chatham-house-rule|Chatham House Rule]] functions as the mechanism allowing government officeholders to conspire with people they should not, because it does not conform to the spirit of the Sunshine Law.[1] In his reading, the rule incorrectly asserts that information may be received and exchanged so long as the identity of the speaker is not revealed when the information is later used, whereas the Sunshine Act exists not to give officeholders privacy but to let voters keep an eye on their elected officials and officeholders.[1] He applied the same reasoning to the annual meetings of the [[bilderberg|Bilderberg Group]], calling them a violation of the sacred trust between citizens and their trustees and describing them as illegal, unlawful, a breach of trust and a violation of the spirit of officials' oaths of office — meetings that have nonetheless been held every year since 1954 without major pushback from citizens, government regulators or oversight committees.[1]

Deschamps nevertheless treated the statute as tactically useful, arguing that because the principle of the Sunshine Act is valid and most ordinary people believe as much, exposing violations of that principle can help awaken the public to the conclusion that their officials cannot be trusted.[1]
