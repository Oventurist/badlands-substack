# January 2026 Government Shutdown Standoff

The January 2026 government shutdown standoff was an appropriations impasse in the United States Congress in which Senate Democrats refused to advance a Republican-backed six-bill spending package unless provisions funding the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] were removed, driving the federal government toward a partial shutdown at a Friday funding deadline at the end of January 2026.[1]

## The dispute

The contested package included an additional $10 billion for [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|Immigration and Customs Enforcement]], on top of prior allocations made under the [[one-big-beautiful-bill-act|One Big Beautiful Bill Act]] of the previous year.[1] Agencies facing a lapse in funding included DHS — encompassing ICE, FEMA, TSA and the Coast Guard — as well as the Pentagon and the Departments of Labor, Transportation, and Housing and Urban Development.[1] Prediction market [[polymarket|Polymarket]] priced the probability of a shutdown by January 31, 2026 at roughly 79%.[1]

## Political drivers

The impasse grew out of national uproar over federal immigration enforcement tactics in Minneapolis, where two U.S. citizens were fatally shot by federal agents in successive confrontations.[1] Sen. [[ted-cruz|Ted Cruz]] (R-TX) described those killed as individuals who were "from all appearances... not violent criminals."[1]

Senate Minority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] (D-NY) called White House and Republican proposals "unrealistic," and demanded reforms including a prohibition on federal agents wearing masks during operations and a requirement that agents obtain judicial search warrants before entering homes.[1] Schumer said the five non-DHS bills could pass immediately if separated from the DHS title, and warned that Republicans would bear responsibility for any shutdown.[1] Republicans declined to strip the DHS language, citing the difficulty of securing House votes for a revised bill while that chamber was in recess.[1]

## De-escalation measures

In response to the pressure, the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] pursued several de-escalatory steps: it removed CBP official [[gregory-bovino|Gregory Bovino]] from the Minneapolis operation and replaced him with border czar [[tom-homan|Tom Homan]]; President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] personally telephoned Minnesota Gov. [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]] and Minneapolis Mayor [[jacob-frey|Jacob Frey]]; and DHS Secretary [[kristi-noem|Kristi Noem]] agreed to testify before the [[senate-judiciary-committee|Senate Judiciary Committee]] after months of refusal.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] placed the standoff in a sequence of immigration-driven funding fights, noting that before 2025 the longest shutdown had been the 35-day lapse in 2018, also over immigration enforcement, and that the December 2025 shutdown ran longer at 43 days, centering on eligibility changes that removed migrants from SNAP and Medicaid.[1] She argued that this contradicted longstanding assurances that illegal migrants could not access entitlements, and drew a parallel to Georgia senators simultaneously denying that non-citizens vote while objecting to voter registration data being shared with DHS for immigration enforcement.[1] She characterized shutting down services for Americans to protect the interests of "criminal invading migrants" as treason in principle, while stating a general preference for government shutdowns, and concluded that the problem is accountability when the authorities of accountability are themselves the parties requiring it.[1]

## House consideration of the Senate funding bill

The partial shutdown began at 12:01 a.m. on January 31, 2026, after [[congress|Congress]] failed to enact all required appropriations before the funding deadline.[2] The Senate subsequently passed a bipartisan spending package funding most federal agencies through the end of the fiscal year while extending [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] funding on a short-term basis.[2]

The legislation returned to the House, where Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] faced divisions within the Republican conference.[2] A bloc of House Republicans sought to attach the [[save-act|SAVE Act]] — which would require proof of US citizenship to register to vote in federal elections — to the spending bill, an approach opposed by Democrats and not included in the Senate package.[2] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] urged House Republicans to pass the Senate-approved measure and reopen the government, while Democratic leaders rejected adding the SAVE Act and pressed instead for changes to immigration enforcement policy, including unmasking federal agents, ending roving patrols, and increasing oversight of DHS operations.[2] House leadership indicated a reopening vote was expected once members returned to Washington, with negotiations focused on whether the package would proceed cleanly or carry additional policy conditions.[2]
