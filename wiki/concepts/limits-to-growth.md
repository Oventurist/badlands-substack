# Limits to Growth

"Limits to growth" refers to the thesis, revived by the [[club-of-rome|Club of Rome]] in 1968, that computer modelling demonstrates fixed limits to humanity's growth.[1] Badlands Media reporting characterizes this as a neo-Malthusian revival resting on the closed-system premises of [[british-east-india-company|British Imperial]] economics — the [[law-of-diminishing-returns|law of diminishing returns]] of [[john-stuart-mill|J. S. Mill]] and [[david-ricardo|David Ricardo]] — which had already been refuted by [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]].[1]

The Club of Rome's models were incorporated into the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] during the 1973 event that produced the [[davos-manifesto|Davos Manifesto]] and [[klaus-schwab|Klaus Schwab]]'s doctrine of [[stakeholder-capitalism|stakeholder capitalism]].[1]

Against the thesis, [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] cites the material record: humanity rose from about one billion people in 1800 to nearly eight billion today, and average US life expectancy rose from roughly 40 years in 1800 to 78 years, while per capita productivity tended to increase along with political emancipation — at least until the financial coup of 1971 in trans-Atlantic society.[1] He also cites [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]]'s 1961 address to the American Academy of Sciences, in which Kennedy said [[thomas-malthus|Malthus]] had been "expressing not a law of nature, but merely the limitation then of scientific and social wisdom."[1] Higher energy-flux-density technologies of the kind described by [[lyndon-larouche|Lyndon LaRouche]] are presented as the practical refutation of fixed limits.[1]

## Origins and reception

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] calls *Limits to Growth* (1972) the document that became the bible and blueprint of the anti-humanist movement that birthed today's [[green-new-deal|Green New Deal]] agenda, noting that it holds the record as the most widely read book on ecology, having sold 30 million copies in 32 languages.[2] A retrospective marking its fortieth anniversary observed that it helped launch modern environmental computer modelling, began the globally focused environmental debate, and more than any other book introduced the concept of anthropocentric climate change to a mass audience.[2]

The book was the culmination of a two-year study by a team of MIT statisticians under the nominal heading of [[jay-forrester|Jay Forrester]] and [[dennis-meadows|Dennis Meadows]].[2] The [[club-of-rome|Club of Rome]]–MIT study was not begun in the USA but at Montebello, Quebec in 1971, when Club backer [[pierre-trudeau|Pierre Trudeau]] allocated taxpayer money to start the project; a network of Rhodes Scholars and Privy Councillors around [[alexander-king|Alexander King]], [[maurice-strong|Maurice Strong]], [[maurice-lamontagne|Maurice Lamontagne]], Michael Pitfield and Governor General Roland Michener presided over the meeting, and when the Canadian funds had served their role the project continued on funding from the Volkswagen Foundation, whose Nazi-supporting past Ehret says should have made some of the MIT statisticians uncomfortable.[2] The model it produced predicted an apocalyptic end of the world by 2000.[2]

## Carrying capacity and criticism

The study's authors gave mathematical form to [[thomas-malthus|Thomas Malthus]]' crisis point of over-population under the name "carrying capacity."[2] [[lyndon-larouche|Lyndon LaRouche]] replied in [[there-are-no-limits-to-growth|*There Are No Limits to Growth*]] (1982) that the study was conspicuously fraudulent on two counts: Meadows and Forrester greatly understated known quantities of natural resources, and they projected consumption using systems of simultaneous linear equations, which builds into the projections the assumption that absolutely no technological progress is occurring — an assumption contradicted by five hundred years of European civilization.[2] Ehret adds that Meadows has more recently described a future in which at most one to two billion people would be granted permission to exist by a ruling oligarchy.[2]
