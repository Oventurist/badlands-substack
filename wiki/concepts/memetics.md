# Memetics

Memetics is the study of how cultural units — memes — replicate, mutate, and spread through populations by a process analogous to biological natural selection. The concept was developed by biologist [[richard-dawkins|Richard Dawkins]], who claimed that culture can follow a process of replication and amplification similar to the Darwinian model.[1]

## Application to the information war

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that memetics has come to define much of the internet and much of the information war, and that the biological concept applies to information far more readily than to biology.[1] The argument rests on the observation that information distilled into tiny, often-visual, readable formats spreads where more exhaustive and expansive efforts fail.[1]

## The "core truth" thesis

The commentary advanced a claim it attributed to observation rather than to Dawkins: that for something to be truly memetic it must communicate a core truth, and that this core truth "need not be burdened by facts."[1] As illustration it cited memes circulating over a decade of information-war activity that were not true in the traditional sense — such as an image of Hillary Clinton visiting Jeffrey Epstein's cell in a Groucho mask — yet which conveyed an idea American culture found impossible to let go of, suggesting a darker truth nesting below conscious regard.[1]

## Political application

According to the same analysis, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] understands and embraces memetics perhaps more than any other leader in world history, and leveraged the internet's force-multiplier effect on memetics during his initial rise in 2016.[1] Trump's opponents noticed the strategy but cannot replicate it intentionally because they do not understand its baseline foundation.[1] The Brief applied this framework to [[keir-starmer|Keir Starmer]]'s viral Pride remarks about the United Kingdom having "the gayest parliament… anywhere in the world," arguing that the outgoing prime minister's legacy was secured by the internet's memetic response, and that the meme of Britain being intentionally made into a "fake and gay mecca" lands differently amid revelations about [[grooming-gangs|grooming gangs]].[1]
