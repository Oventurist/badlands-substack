# Kabbalah

Kabbalah is a tradition of Jewish mysticism which Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] treats as distinct from Judaism proper, arguing on the [[breaking-history|Breaking History]] program that "the Kabbalah is not Judaism."[1]

## Historical account

In the Badlands telling, the form of Judaism practiced in pre-Biblical and Biblical times was based on the Torah, the first five books of the Old Testament.[1] After the death of Christ — which the account attributes to the [[sanhedrin|Sanhedrin]], the supreme rabbinical court — the Roman province of Judaea fell into chaos, culminating in the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 AD and the diaspora of the Jewish people from the Holy Land, with many of those people becoming the early Christians.[1]

By the fourth century AD, following the Council of Nicaea in 325, Emperor Constantine declared Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire.[1] At the same time, the remnants of the Sanhedrin — the Pharisees who had rejected Christ — were reorganizing Jewish theology with new ideas, writing the Talmud, which they claimed recorded oral traditions predating Christ, and later, in the 13th century, the Zohar, which was the origin of Kabbalah.[1]

The account emphasizes that unlike Christianity and Islam, Judaism has no central doctrine outside the Torah, so modern Judaism spans a wide spectrum of belief; while many sects focus on the Torah and Talmud to varying degrees, some — particularly those originating in Eastern Europe among Ashkenazi Jews, in the region of the Pale of Settlement — focus on the Zohar and Kabbalah.[1]

## Practical Kabbalah

The first Jewish mystics, some 800 years ago, believed they had overlooked important teachings from the distant past and looked back to the earliest forms of organized religion, before Abraham some 6,000 years ago, for insight.[1] These Kabbalists believed King Solomon had used the Kabbalah to communicate with divine beings who gave him the knowledge and power to construct the First Jewish Temple in the 10th century BC.[1]

The core concept of what is called "Practical Kabbalah" is that mystics can likewise communicate with these deities through rituals and wield their power as Solomon did.[1] In the 1550s, Rabbi Isaac Luria — whose teachings later became the foundation of Hasidic Judaism in the 18th century — held that pursuing Practical Kabbalah, understood as magic or sorcery, without the [[third-temple|Third Temple]] constructed or the ashes of burned [[red-heifer|red heifers]] could result in something very detrimental such as the unwitting summoning of demons; Practical Kabbalah was consequently prohibited among commoners.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry described this as possibly the most important commentary he had written for the newsletter, citing extensive research and a 1921 article in The Atlantic by Albert T. Clay.[1] He concluded that the activists of the [[temple-institute|Temple Institute]] are mystics who embrace Practical Kabbalah, and that while they may believe they are about to summon their messiah, his Christian faith and instincts led him to believe they are in fact attempting to summon the Antichrist.[1]
