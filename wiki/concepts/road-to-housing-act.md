# ROAD to Housing Act

The **ROAD to Housing Act** is a housing policy package that, as of December 2025, was folded into the text of the fiscal year 2026 [[national-defense-authorization-act|National Defense Authorization Act]] rather than being advanced as freestanding legislation.[1] Its inclusion in a 3,086-page defense authorization bill unveiled by House Republican leaders on Sunday, December 7, 2025, drew criticism from commentators who viewed the maneuver as an attempt to move unrelated domestic policy through a must-pass national security vehicle.[1]

## Inclusion in the FY2026 NDAA

House GOP leadership planned to bring the annual defense policy bill to a vote late on Wednesday, December 10, 2025, after releasing the bill text the preceding Sunday evening.[1] The package immediately drew public pushback from hard-line conservatives over the inclusion of Ukraine funding and the omission of a ban on a [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] digital currency, among other issues.[1] Badlands commentary singled out the housing title specifically, asking why Congress was "sneaking in the 'ROAD to Housing Act' inside the 2026 NDAA."[1]

## Nested legislation

According to the Badlands analysis, the ROAD to Housing Act was not merely one additional bill inside the NDAA: the housing act itself was described as containing 28 further acts nested within it, effectively making the defense authorization a vehicle for dozens of separate legislative measures.[1] Badlands contributor Jon Herold characterized the practice in blunt terms, and the episode was presented as an example of the legislative bundling that allows individual provisions to pass without standalone debate or recorded votes.[1]

## Significance

The controversy illustrates a recurring theme in Badlands coverage of [[congress|Congress]]: that omnibus and must-pass legislation is routinely used to enact policy that could not survive scrutiny on its own merits.[1] Because members face pressure to support the annual defense authorization, riders attached to it are difficult to strip out without jeopardizing the underlying bill.[1]
