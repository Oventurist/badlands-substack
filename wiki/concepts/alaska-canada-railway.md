# Alaska-Canada Railway

The **Alaska-Canada Railway** is a 2,570 km rail project intended to connect Alaska for the first time with Canada and the lower 48 states. [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] reports that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced federal support for the long-overdue project on September 26, 2020, announcing it in a tweet the same day.[1]

## Significance

Ehret describes the potential re-emergence of the railway, driven under a pro-Pacific and pro-Arctic development model, as the first genuine display of that paradigm in North America in decades.[1] If it survives the oncoming Environmental Impact Assessments and the Federal Government of Canada — which he characterizes as run by anti-development Rhodes Scholars and technocrats committed to depopulation and world government — it would open bountiful resources locked in the inaccessible Arctic, create tens of thousands of direct jobs and millions indirectly, and vector North America's economic destiny toward the growing Asian markets led by China.[1] Most importantly, in his framing, it would break the west free of the twofold trap of anti-development environmentalism on one side and pro-militarization right-wing views on the other, moving toward win-win cooperation with Eurasian partners.[1]

## Implementation

The Alaska-Alberta Railway Development Corporation was tasked by Trump with building and managing the $17 billion project, and features programs to tie North America into the Asian market and to integrate a North American transport system whose once-proud rail network has fallen derelict since WWII and the age of "highways and cars."[1] Whether such programs can proceed now that globalist arrangements such as NAFTA and the TPP have been jettisoned — giving nation states authority to exercise a dirigistic role in long-term economic planning — remains to be seen.[1] The railway is conceptually linked to the [[bering-strait-tunnel|Bering Strait Tunnel]] and the [[polar-silk-road|Polar Silk Road]].[1]

## The missing 1,000 km gap

Anyone wishing to take rail north from the United States can currently travel only as far as British Columbia, because a 1,000 km gap separates all existing rail from Alaska.[3] Ehret calls it incredible that, in light of the post-war battles for continental development, this gap has remained in place for decades, with the northernmost line reaching only Dease Lake, British Columbia, built over fifty years ago by [[wac-bennett|Premier W.A.C. Bennett]] and long derided as "the railway to nowhere" with a price tag of only one dollar.[3] He describes the line as a relic of a sabotaged northern vision that was always designed to connect Canada and the USA while opening the north for development, and attributes the sabotage of the wider BC rail system to Bennett's son, Bill Bennett.[3] The Alaska–British Columbia–Yukon conferences of the 1960s were instrumental in advancing Bennett's plan for an eventual link between the two rail systems.[3]

The connection would run 2,570 km, moving freight, oil, grains and other goods from Anchorage to the Yukon, the Northwest Territories and northern [[alberta|Alberta]], and involves roughly 1,800 km of new construction between Dease Lake and the Yukon and Alaska.[3] Ehret presents it as one of the greatest projects in the Americas and a necessary component of the Bering Strait rail corridor programme proposed by the governments of Russia and China, which would extend corridors from the Eurasian New Silk Road through the Siberian development belt into the Americas.[3]

## Studies, A2A and obstacles

In the year 2000 the Alaskan government spent $6 million on a feasibility study, followed in 2015 by a similar study funded by the Province of Alberta.[3] It was in 2015 that the [[alaska-alberta-railway-development-corporation|Alaska-Alberta Railway Development Corporation]] (A2A) was created as a private initiative to advance a plan the federal government had long committed to blocking; its CEO [[sean-mccoshen|Sean McCoshen]] projected more than 18,000 Canadian jobs and a more efficient trans-Pacific shipping route linking Alberta to world markets.[3] Ehret attributes the earlier lack of headway to the monetarist rules underpinning globalization, which he says place supposedly "free markets" on the throne and nation states in the dungeon of economics.[3]

Ehret contrasts the project with the militarization of the North American Arctic, noting Canada's April 2024 Arctic defence doctrine embracing long-range ballistic missiles and participation, with Japan and New Zealand, in the [[aukus|AUKUS]] nuclear submarine programme.[3] He argues that the railway, together with the [[bering-strait-tunnel|Bering Strait Rail Tunnel]], offers a break from British geopolitics, the rebuilding of industrial cities ravaged by decades of globalized outsourcing, and the construction of new permanent cities — fulfilling the dreams of Bennett and [[john-diefenbaker|John Diefenbaker]].[3]

## Arctic strategic context

In a later essay Ehret returned to the railway, writing that Trump's executive order reviving the Alaska-Canada Railway was directly tied to a strategic vision for Arctic cooperation, in opposition to the closed-system warhawks promoting a militaristic program against Russia and China in the Arctic.[2] He placed the project alongside Russia's revival of the [[bering-strait-tunnel|Bering Strait crossing]], China's [[polar-silk-road|Polar Silk Road]] and the merger of the [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]] with the [[eurasian-economic-union|Eurasian Economic Union]] as elements of an [[open-systems-vs-closed-systems|open-system]] development dynamic offering economic opportunity to all Arctic nations and an escape from military confrontation.[2]

## Framing around the 2025 Alaska summit

Writing ahead of the [[2025-trump-putin-alaska-summit|August 2025 Trump–Putin meeting in Alaska]], Ehret repeated that in the early hours of September 26, 2020 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had announced the revival of the 2,570 km Alaska–Canada rail connection, a "gigantic continental project long thought dead and buried," moving freight, oil, grains and other goods from Anchorage to the Yukon, the Northwest Territories and northern [[alberta|Alberta]].[4] He argued that completing [[wac-bennett|W.A.C. Bennett]]'s rail vision in the twenty-first century is a necessary component of the Bering Strait Rail Corridor programme proposed by the governments of Russia and China, which would extend transport corridors from the Eurasian New Silk Road through the Siberian development belt into the Americas, connecting down to the west coast and New York.[4] Other than Trump's 2020 announcement of federal support, he wrote, the only North American Arctic discussions of note in recent decades had centred on anti-Russian militarization and anti-ballistic missiles.[4]
