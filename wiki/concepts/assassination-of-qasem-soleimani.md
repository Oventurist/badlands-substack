# Assassination of Qasem Soleimani

On 3 January 2020, [[qasem-soleimani|Qasem Soleimani]], an Iranian major general, was targeted and killed by a United States drone strike near Baghdad International Airport in [[iraq|Iraq]] while on his way to meet Iraqi Prime Minister [[adil-abdul-mahdi|Adil Abdul-Mahdi]].[1] A Hellfire missile destroyed his convoy shortly after his arrival at the airport, killing him and nine others.[1] Badlands Media describes the strike as "cutting the head off the snake" in the Middle East and as possibly the biggest single event to promote peace in the region in decades.[1]

## Background

The strike came three days after protesters attacked the United States embassy in Baghdad on December 31, 2019, an event that prompted President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to warn that [[iran|Iran]] would be "held fully responsible for lives lost, or damage incurred, at any of our facilities" and that "they will pay a very BIG PRICE."[1] Speaking to reporters at Mar-a-Lago on New Year's Eve, Trump said he did not want war but that a conflict "would go very quickly" and would not be a good idea for Iran.[1]

Soleimani was in Iraq on a not-so-secret trip to meet politicians and military allies, which reporting indicates also had a covert component: coordinating an intensifying campaign of rocket strikes against some of the 5,000 American troops based in the country.[1] In mid-October 2019 he had secretly met Shiite militia leaders to coordinate attacks on US forces in hopes of triggering an American reprisal that would redirect Iraqi public anger from Iran to the United States, delegating the campaign to [[abu-mahdi-al-muhandis|Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis]], a head of the [[popular-mobilization-forces|Popular Mobilization Forces]] and commander of [[kataib-hizballah|Kataib Hezbollah]].[1] Al-Muhandis was killed in the same strike, meaning Trump removed Soleimani, al-Muhandis and other militant leaders in a single operation.[1]

## Significance in the Badlands account

Badlands Media argues that Soleimani was the one man connected to all Islamic terrorism in the Middle East going back several decades, that he was among [[alwaleed-bin-talal|Al Waleed bin Talal]]'s biggest puppets, and that he — rather than Iran's supreme leader, characterised as a figurehead — was the real power in Iran.[1] In this reading, Soleimani's task under Al Waleed's control was to keep fomenting terror and the perceived need for war as a mechanism for laundering money, with the entire war on terror described as a massive money-laundering operation.[1] Trump publicly stated that Soleimani should have been killed many years earlier; the article attributes the failure of previous administrations to act to Al Waleed's control over four preceding presidents.[1]

Soleimani commanded the [[quds-force|Quds Force]], which funded organisations designated as terrorist groups by the United States, including [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]] in [[lebanon|Lebanon]] and Hamas in Gaza, and trained militant groups in Iraq that attacked American forces with sophisticated explosives.[1] One such device, the explosively formed penetrator, could shatter even armoured vehicles; roughly 861 US troops were wounded and 196 killed by Iranian-made EFPs over the preceding decade according to a Military Times report citing the US military.[1]

## Reaction

Rather than universal approval, the strike drew criticism from figures associated with the [[obama-administration|Obama administration]] and the press.[1] The Federalist published a piece headlined "Ben Rhodes Attacks Trump To Obscure Obama's Complicity In Iran's Forever War," describing Soleimani as an Obama administration partner and the leading terrorist of the leading terror force of the world's leading terror regime.[1] Former US Ambassador to the United Nations [[samantha-power|Samantha Power]] condemned the action, complaining that Trump was surrounded by sycophants, had purged Iran specialists, and had abolished National Security Council processes to review contingencies.[1] The New York Times' Max Fisher wrote that assassinating Soleimani would be "a major, overt act of war," while Washington Post columnist David Ignatius described "an eerie feeling" reminiscent of the 2003 invasion of Iraq.[1]

Badlands Media attributes the panic to two issues in particular — the Iran nuclear deal and the pallets of cash sent to Iran — and argues that the [[cia|CIA]] and the Obama administration feared exposure of a money-laundering operation and were hoping for war with Iran as a distraction.[1] Contrary to establishment predictions, no war broke out; the author contends the strike freed Iran from control by Al Waleed, Soleimani and the CIA.[1]
