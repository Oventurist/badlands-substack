# Ku Klux Klan Act

The Ku Klux Klan Act, commonly called the KKK Act, is a Reconstruction-era federal civil-rights statute that prohibits conspiracies to intimidate persons or to violate their civil rights.[1] In January 2026 the statute returned to national prominence when Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]] said that the storming of [[cities-church|Cities Church]] in St. Paul, Minnesota by left-wing activists could constitute a violation of the Act, alongside the [[face-act|FACE Act]].[1]

## Use as political messaging

Badlands Media contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that she was herself sued in March 2022 for allegedly violating the KKK Act, fought the suit and won, but argued that the outcome of her case was less important than the pattern it illustrated.[1] She observed that the Act "makes for sensational headlines" and carries a unique moral stigma because of its association with the Ku Klux Klan and racial terror — a stigma she said is not accidental, since legal scholars openly acknowledge that invoking the statute can function as political messaging.[1]

She cited a 2015 NYU Social Justice Law Review passage — quoted by the plaintiffs and their experts in her own case — stating that "another reason to bring a KKK Act claim is for the purpose of political messaging," because liability under the Act "carries the additional stigma of conspiracy and its association with the KKK's legacy of politicalized racism," creating "the opportunity to brand a particular ballot security group or party committee with these deeply embarrassing associations" and thereby strengthening deterrence and driving settlement.[1] Ashe in America cited the same passage in her appellate defense to show that the statute was being used as a reputational weapon rather than a civil-rights safeguard, noting that the plaintiffs had cited the paper as authority for the propriety of their conduct.[1]

## Contemporary assessment

In her assessment, the KKK Act in the 21st century is "less about stopping violent conspiracies and more about attaching moral contamination to political opponents" — in her words, "character assassination with federal branding."[1] She argued that the statute had been deployed repeatedly in this fashion, but rarely against figures such as former CNN host [[don-lemon|Don Lemon]], writing that "empathetic mockingbirds rarely get clap backs like 'Klan.'"[1]

Ashe in America wrote that seeing the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] "boomerang the KKK Act onto the same political class that spent years weaponizing it" satisfied a certain instinct, while maintaining that the Act "should probably be abolished" — though she suggested that before abolition becomes realistic, certain people need to become better acquainted with what the statute has become in 2026.[1]

## Use against Don Lemon (2026)

In January 2026 [[don-lemon|Don Lemon]] was criminally charged with conspiracy against rights under the Civil Rights Act of 1871 in connection with a protest at [[cities-church|Cities Church]] in St. Paul, Minnesota.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the irony of the charge "hilarious," describing the Reconstruction-era statute as a law passed to combat organized and often violent efforts to intimidate citizens and suppress protected constitutional rights, especially voting and First Amendment activity, and noting that it created both criminal penalties and civil remedies for conspiracies interfering with federally protected rights.[2]

She recounted having been accused civilly under the act — with no criminal claim for which to provide remedy — and winning in July 2024 after defending herself, while observing that she won nothing beyond the right to say so factually.[2] From this she drew the maxim that "the process is the punishment": an accusation under the act functions as character assassination that persists in background checks and public opinion regardless of the verdict, a condition she termed "manifest injustice."[2]
