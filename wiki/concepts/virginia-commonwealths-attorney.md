# Virginia Commonwealth's Attorney

In Virginia, local prosecutors are titled "Commonwealth's Attorneys" (CAs) and occupy an unusual constitutional position.[1] Unlike prosecutors in most states, who sit within the executive branch, Virginia's CAs are categorized in the state Constitution as "Constitutional Officers," placing them outside executive control.[1]

## Accountability gap

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] describes this designation as effectively creating a fourth branch of government: Commonwealth's Attorneys answer to no one except the voters, once every four years.[1] Even where overwhelming evidence of corruption exists, there is no mechanism to recall a CA, remove him from office, or remove him from a specific case in which a blatant conflict of interest is present.[1] In most other states a governor holds the power to remove prosecutors from individual cases, as occurred in the Jussie Smollett matter; Virginia provides no equivalent remedy.[1]

## Litigation challenge

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry recounted that his family sued the state in July 2019 to challenge this constitutional provision, after a judge was forced to consider a plea deal arising from his brother's death in which the accused would have received a full confession credit in exchange for one year in county jail rather than prison.[1] The prosecutor — later exposed as a career con artist with a criminal record and no prosecutorial experience — told the property owner where the death occurred that the plea was the family's best chance at justice, citing a purported conversation with the judge.[1] The property owner's letter of outrage prompted the judge to appear in open court, deny any such conversation, note that it would have been unlawful and career-ending, reject the plea deal and recuse himself.[1]

The resulting lawsuit sought removal of the prosecutor from the case, citing statements he had made privately that he cared only about re-election and not about the victim's family.[1] A second judge acknowledged the argument as compelling but denied the motion, citing the plain language of the Virginia Constitution; he too subsequently rejected a plea deal and recused himself, delaying the prosecution into the following calendar year and handing it to a successor prosecutor.[1] The case finally went to trial in January 2022 and produced a conviction, five years after the death.[1] The original prosecutor was defeated at the polls by a historic margin, lost his law license, and never qualified for the state pension he had been seeking.[1]

The 2026 [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] investigation of Fairfax County Commonwealth's Attorney [[steve-descano|Steve Descano]] revived attention to this accountability gap.[1]
