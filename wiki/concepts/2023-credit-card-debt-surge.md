# 2023 Credit Card Debt Surge

The 2023 credit card debt surge refers to an unexpected explosion in U.S. revolving consumer credit reported by the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] in May 2023, which contradicted the prevailing expectation that a credit crunch was throttling household borrowing.[1]

## Background

One month before the March data was released, analysts at [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] had warned that consumer credit was about to get much tighter following one of the most depressing Senior Loan Officer Opinion Surveys (SLOOS) on record, which showed near-record tightening in lending standards paired with a historic plunge in credit demand.[1] Consistent with that warning, growth in U.S. credit card debt appeared to grind to a crawl: revolving credit rose by just $5 billion, down sharply from $12.8 billion in January and from a $13.7 billion trailing twelve-month average, and the smallest single monthly increase since April 2021.[1] The conclusion drawn at the time was that whether the slowdown reflected weak demand or a sudden squeeze in supply, "one of the most powerful economic lifelines is grinding to a halt."[1]

## The March 2023 print

The subsequent release upended that reading. Against Street expectations of a modest increase from February's $15 billion to roughly $17 billion, the Fed reported that new credit card debt in March totaled $26.514 billion — smashing expectations by almost $10 billion and marking the second-fastest pace of credit card debt growth on record, arriving just as interest rates hit all-time highs.[1]

## Commentary

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] read the figures as confirmation that "credit, borrowing, and leverage are the order of the day."[1] He traced the shift to the normalization of credit cards in the late 1970s, combined with an explosion of cheap imported goods, which he argued replaced a culture of saving for purchases with an addiction to immediate consumption financed at nearly double the sticker price in interest.[1] In his account this was not accidental but part of a much older effort to induce otherwise self-sufficient people to trade independence for convenience, ease, and material splendor, producing a population that continues to "spend-spend-spend" even as the Fed raises rates, consumer goods double in price, and the housing market deflates — roughly a century after the Great Depression instilled the opposite habits.[1]

Deschamps further argued that the bankers' answer to the resulting fragility would not be a return to sound money but the deliberate triggering of another global bankruptcy — one in a long line of "great resets" — ushering in a more permanent and freedom-compromising financial system built on [[central-bank-digital-currency|Central Bank Digital Currencies]] and universal basic income tokens.[1]
