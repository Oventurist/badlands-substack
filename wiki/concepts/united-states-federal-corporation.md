# United States as a Federal Corporation

The claim that "the United States is a federal corporation" is a recurring argument in constitutionalist and sovereignty-oriented commentary, and forms the evidentiary centrepiece of the [[a-startling-contradiction-at-the|Badlands Media article "A Startling Contradiction at the Heart of 'Our' Legal System"]] (May 16, 2023) by [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]].

## The statutory citation

The argument rests on Title 28 of the [[united-states-code|United States Code]], section 3002(15)(A), part of the Federal Debt Collection Procedures Act's definitions section, which the article reports as defining the "United States" as "a Federal corporation." The article asserts that a scan of the remainder of the Code yields no other definition of the *nature* of the United States; other provisions define the United States geographically, which the author regards as immaterial to the question of its legal character. In the absence of any counter-citation drawn from the Code, the article says the conclusion to be drawn is "unclear," and treats the citation as sufficient to unsettle conventional accounts of American government.

The article frames the question in civic terms: Americans are told variously that they live in a democracy and that they live in a republic, but it asks how either proposition can be legitimately proved by citation.

## Chain of authority

The article is careful to distinguish its position from the stronger claim that all civic authority taught in schools is fictitious. It advances the principle that no governmental body, agency, or entity — governmental or commercial — can exceed the authority of that which created it. On this reasoning, a corporate "United States" installed at some point in history does not negate the chain of authority that has always existed above it: federal authority derives from the states, state authority derives from the people, and, per the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]], ultimate authority derives from God. Corporations, the article notes, cannot exist before nations or "body politics."

The article supports the people-to-states-to-federal ordering by quoting the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] in [[chisholm-v-georgia|*Chisholm, Ex'r v. Georgia*]], which described a state as deriving its authority from "the voluntary and deliberate choice of the people."

## Maladministration and "linguistic shell games"

Because a corporate United States was, in the article's telling, installed "against the knowledge or consent of the people," the author characterises it as "an extraordinary act of maladministration" that "has been covered up almost completely." The article concedes that the full mechanics are not understood and that "the detective work into these matters remains ongoing."

Related to the corporation claim is the article's concept of "linguistic shell games" — games in which the meanings of words are surreptitiously redefined so as to attach hidden meanings to familiar terms, producing a "dual" language in which a speaker's words are presumed by the system to mean something other than what the speaker intends. On this account there exist multiple entities calling themselves "the United States": a corporate one and a federal one, both of which the article says ultimately derive their authority from the people and from God.

This framing is contested: mainstream legal scholarship treats 28 U.S.C. § 3002(15)(A) as a narrow definitional provision applicable to debt-collection procedure rather than a statement about national sovereignty. The Badlands article presents the citation as evidence of a broader concealment, and links the public's ignorance of it to the operational latitude of the [[deep-state|Deep State]].
