# Hudson River School

The Hudson River School was the nineteenth-century American movement of landscape painting which, in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]], arose directly out of [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]]'s [[national-academy-of-design|National Academy of the Arts of Design]] and out of the earlier school led by Morse's teacher [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]].[1]

## Aesthetic principles

Ehret argues that the school's canvases are consistently more than landscapes and more than the sum of their parts, aiming not to paint literally but to penetrate to the essence of the soul of Creation using light, shade and perspective.[1] Their subject matter is the category of universal sentiments explored by [[friedrich-schiller|Friedrich Schiller]] and Alexander von Humboldt in their theories of art and freedom, as distinct from the ephemeral emotions of day-to-day experience.[1] The article uses the school to rebut the modern claim that classical realism deadens creativity and that abstract surrealism is the only route to creative freedom.[1]

## Painters and works

Albert Bierstadt's *Among the Sierra Nevada Mountains of California* is cited as a work in which the painter was consciously expressing the Divine in the light and grandeur of immense landscapes.[1] [[thomas-cole|Thomas Cole]], a direct student of West and collaborator of Morse, mastered the high-fidelity copying of nature but rarely painted a landscape without conveying a transcendent idea; his series on the course of life traces a man from infancy through adolescence, adulthood, maturity and death, intended not as romantic sentiment but to bring the profound truths of mortality and the purpose of life before the audience.[1] Frederic Edwin Church's *Cotopaxi*, composed in 1862 at the heart of the American Civil War, contrasts the destructive heat of a volcano with the light of the rising sun, expressing what Ehret identifies as hope — the higher power of the rising sun superseding temporal destruction.[1] At the National Academy's inception its thirty founding talents included Cole, Asher B. Durand, Frederick Agate and Martin E. Thompson.[1]
