# The Truth Cannot Be Extinguished

"The truth cannot be extinguished" is the central thesis of "The Truth," a January 11, 2023 essay by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]]. The piece argues that truth is self-sustaining and indestructible, and that the information war in which the [[great-awakening|Great Awakening]] milieu believes itself engaged therefore cannot ultimately be lost.[1]

## The lion metaphor

Carlson opened with the aphorism that "the truth is like a lion. It doesn't need to be protected; just let it loose and it will protect itself."[1] He contrasted this with lies and [[disinformation|disinformation]], which he said require "constant nurturing" and consume enormous resources to make the masses believe and keep believing them.[1] Truth, by contrast, can be minimized — its fuel cut or its oxygen removed — and "billions and trillions of dollars" can be spent suppressing it, but it will always survive, "in many cases just barely," and "always find a way to persist."[1]

## Truth as singular and objective

The essay rejects relativism explicitly. Carlson wrote that whether or not a person believes the truth, it remains the truth; it can be rejected, ignored, minimized, or hidden from others, but not altered — and "if you can alter the truth, then it never really was the truth to begin with."[1] He stated flatly that "your truth can't be different from my truth. There is only one truth," adding that truth "isn't an ice cream flavor" nor like the popular view of religions as multiple paths to one endpoint.[1] In his framing there is "right" and varying degrees of wrong, and "anything that is not 100% the truth is a lie."[1]

## Why people accept or reject truth

Carlson canvassed possible explanations for why some people reach the truth and others do not, treating the question as one of [[red-pilling|red-pilling]]. He allowed that intelligence helps but is not decisive, since "there are plenty of smart people who live their lives believing lies."[1] He gave greater weight to psychology, personality and ego damage: some can abandon prior beliefs painlessly, while others "would rather go on believing lies than accept a truth that forces them to admit they were wrong."[1] For others, the barrier is effort — refusing to look past the first search result or to assess whether an author has a paid or ideological agenda, illustrated by his remark that it "shouldn't shock anyone" that [[pfizer|Pfizer]] employees "tend to be pro-vaccine."[1]

He further argued that accepting truth can require sacrifice — loss of employment, relationships, social status, or access to public venues such as sports, movies and restaurants — and that many people rationally weigh those costs and choose the lie, in a dynamic he compared to cult members deliberating whether to leave.[1] Testing the point, he asked how people would respond to the claim that "the world is run by Satanic pedophiles that want to create a one-world government," predicting that a few would accept it at once, most would need time, and many would reject it as crazy or too painful to acknowledge.[1]

Carlson's profile of the person most likely to accept truth comprised four qualities: innate intelligence and discernment; self-confidence sufficient to trust oneself and stand alone; courage and willingness to sacrifice; and maturity, "allowing the belief in God to open your eyes to the truth."[1]

## Personal narrative

The essay is substantially autobiographical. Carlson recalled an uncle so devoted to [[john-f-kennedy|JFK]] that he kept a plaster bust of the president's head in his den, and confessed that as a boy he disliked JFK precisely because his uncle loved him — an opinion he acknowledged was based on "ridiculous reasoning."[1] He suggested the same dynamic may explain part of the hostility to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]: that some people resent that others find him appealing.[1]

Tracing his political development, Carlson wrote that as a young adult he considered [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] an embarrassment to the office, but that he later "fell for the 9-11 narrative hook, line and sinker," approving — "like all of [[congress|Congress]] and the Senate" — of war against countries unconnected to the official [[september-11-attacks|9/11]] account and of surrendering personal rights for safety.[1] He disliked [[barack-obama|Obama]] enough to turn his back on politics, yet voted for [[mitt-romney|Mitt Romney]] and [[john-mccain|John "No Name" McCain]], concluding that "a person can be smart and stupid at the same time" and that age does not reliably confer understanding.[1]

Trump both "scared and excited" him; Carlson feared he would turn the world upside down, which he now regards as "the only thing."[1] The [[qanon|Q]] drops, he wrote, were "like a bucket of cold water being thrown on me," after which his appetite for truth became insatiable and he began telling people about the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]], the Illuminati bloodlines and [[false-flag-operation|false flags]].[1] He came to believe he had merely overwhelmed his listeners, who "would go right back to sleep again after being overwhelmed by the media's obfuscation," though he noted that "fewer and fewer people think I'm crazy today."[1]

## Implications for the information war

Carlson concluded that because the truth cannot be defeated, "the war we find ourselves in cannot be lost," offering this as solace to the discouraged without diminishing those who fight and sacrifice in it.[1] He urged readers not to be passive but to "seek the truth, spread the truth, expose the lies, hold the liars accountable," while trusting that with hearts and egos in the right place "we can't screw this up."[1] The essay closes with counsel to be patient — the war "will be won in its own time," people "will wake up at their own pace," and the process "can't be forced" — and with an exhortation to pray for strength, endurance, enlightenment, and for opponents and those who have done wrong.[1]
