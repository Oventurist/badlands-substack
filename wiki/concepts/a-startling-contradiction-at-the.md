# A Startling Contradiction at the Heart of 'Our' Legal System

"A Startling Contradiction at the Heart of 'Our' Legal System," subtitled "If the Law Doesn't Belong to the People, Then What Is It For?", is an opinion article published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on May 16, 2023, written by the contributor known as [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]]. The piece argues that the American legal system rests on a self-contradictory premise — that ordinary citizens are simultaneously presumed incompetent to interpret the law and yet fully liable for obeying it — and that this contradiction is compounded by the statutory definition of the United States as a "federal corporation." As with all Badlands Media features, the article carries a disclaimer that it represents the opinions of the contributing author and not necessarily the views of Badlands Media itself. At the time of archiving it had accumulated 120 likes.

## The central contradiction

The article opens with what it calls a simple question: why do we need lawyers? The author stresses that the question is not intended to disparage members of the legal profession who are sympathetic to Americans and to "true American values," which the article identifies with the preamble to the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]]. Rather, the question is posed to expose a structural absurdity. Citizens are told, on the one hand, that "commonfolk" lack the competence to interpret law and therefore require qualified professionals to intervene on their behalf; on the other hand, those same citizens are expected to obey the law voluntarily and competently, and are punished for transgressing it.

These two assumptions, the article contends, are diametrically opposed and cannot coexist. "If we're sufficiently competent to abide by the law on our own, then we shouldn't need legal professionals to handle our matters for us; conversely, if we necessarily require interpreters to intervene on our behalf to operate lawfully, then we shouldn't be held liable for knowing how to abide by it ourselves." The author states the underlying principle in italics: "You cannot have a free society if the very rules that govern that society are indecipherable by the citizens of that society."

To illustrate, the article observes that lawyers and judges themselves have likely breached laws or codes without any knowledge of having done so, and asks whether it is even possible for anyone to know the law as presently written in its entirety. It offers the example of agents of the [[internal-revenue-service|Internal Revenue Service]], asking whether they have memorized the entire revenue code — Title 26 of the [[united-states-code|United States Code]] — and, if not, how they can enforce it in real time. Attempting to do so, the author writes, would be "just as absurd" as playing chess without knowing how the pieces move. Until the law becomes both decipherable and accessible to those obligated to follow it, the article says, it remains "a twisted game of Russian roulette," in which any act or omission might be argued to be illegal by a sufficiently "weaselly" legal professional.

In a parenthetical aside the article cites the claim, drawn from Andrew Łobaczewski's *Political Ponerology*, that genuine clinical psychopaths account for roughly 4% of the population. The author clarifies that humane or common-sense outcomes remain possible within the legal system, but insists no guarantee of them exists and that opportunities for morality to prevail are "dwindling by the minute."

## A quick caveat

Under a section headed "A Quick Caveat," the article salutes those who entered the legal profession "for the right reasons, not knowing the true nature of the game." It argues that the system as currently constituted punishes those who wish to do good, yet that good and moral people both inside and outside it are "the only barrier we have against absolute chaos." The author observes that the system could not survive if it did not at least superficially appear to deliver order and justice, and that this appearance is made possible by conscientious people working within it. The stated target of the critique is therefore the system itself rather than the individuals inside it — excepting those who knowingly and intentionally misuse it to cause harm. Throughout history, the article adds, "the fact that we have any 'rights' whatsoever is due to the sacrifice of thousands upon thousands of heroes whose names we'll never know."

## True rule of law

The article maintains that the [[rule-of-law|rule of law]] does not exist when citizens cannot articulate the laws under which they are required to operate. Restoring America and other nations, it argues, requires "a renaissance in civic awareness." When the purpose of law is subverted to serve any aim other than the defense of legitimate rights, the author writes, "the law itself becomes the greatest source of tyranny."

Ordinary people — even those of extreme intelligence, good faith and work ethic — cannot stay current with the "tens of thousands of pages of new 'law' being enacted on a regular basis." The article's proposed remedy is collective rather than individual: readers are urged to help build a shared database of the legal system's "most blatant absurdities," making it impossible for anyone of sound mind to continue regarding the present arrangement as valid or tenable. The author offers the metaphor of a boat with many holes: if the holes can be located and collectively plugged, the vessel stays afloat. The [[deep-state|Deep State]], the article asserts, "requires our collective ignorance to operate unopposed."

## The United States as a federal corporation

The article's central factual claim concerns the statutory definition of the United States. Citing Title 28 of the United States Code, section 3002(15)(A), it reports that the United States is defined there as a "federal corporation," and asserts that no other definition of the *nature* of the United States appears elsewhere in the Code — other provisions define the United States geographically, which the author treats as immaterial. In the absence of a counter-citation from the Code, the article says, the conclusion to be drawn is "unclear." This forms the basis of the wider argument about the [[united-states-federal-corporation|corporate United States]].

The author notes that federal corporations presuppose a federal government, and that there must be a "chain of authority." According to the Declaration of Independence, the article says, the authority of the government of America ultimately comes from God; readers are referred to a companion Badlands piece, "God and the Declaration of Independence." Corporations cannot exist before nations or "body politics."

## From the people, to the states, to the United States

Developing the chain-of-authority argument, the article states the principle that no governmental body, agency, or other entity — governmental or commercial — can exceed the authority of that which created it. It cautions that this does not mean the entire civic structure taught in schools was a lie: at some point a corporate "United States" was installed, but that installation "doesn't negate the entire chain of authority that has *always* existed above it." The authority of the federal government derives from the states, which in turn derive theirs from the people.

To support this, the article quotes the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] decision in [[chisholm-v-georgia|*Chisholm, Ex'r v. Georgia*]], dated in the piece as February 1794:

> "A State does not owe its origin to the Government of the [federal] United States, in the highest or in any of its branches. It was in existence before it. It derives its authority from the same pure and sacred source as itself: The voluntary and deliberate choice of the people ... A State is altogether exempt from the jurisdiction of the Courts of the United States, or from any other exterior authority, unless in the special instances where the general Government has power derived from the Constitution itself ..."

From this the author concludes that the voluntary and deliberate choice of the people is the source of state authority, from which all else flows, and that the installation of a corporate "United States" without the knowledge or consent of the people constitutes "an extraordinary act of maladministration" that "has been covered up almost completely." The article explicitly concedes that "we do not yet know the full details of how all this works — the detective work into these matters remains ongoing," and recommends that readers consult the Badlands comment sections, describing the site's commenters as among "the most well-read and astute" available.

## Linguistic shell games and the role of the people

The article argues that multiple entities call themselves "the United States," and that the system plays "linguistic shell games" — defined in the piece as games in which the meanings of words are surreptitiously redefined so as to attach hidden meanings to familiar terms. This creates a "dual" language in which a speaker may believe their words mean one thing while the system presumes, often without disclosure, that they mean something else. There is, the author says, a corporate United States as well as a federal United States, and all such entities ultimately derive their authority from the people and from God. Remove that foundation, the article contends, and none of the rest can stand.

In a section titled "Fixing Things at the Top and the Bottom," the article frames this civic education as a complement to political action from above: while [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and "the Q team" — a reference to [[q-anon|Q]] — fix things "upstairs," ordinary people can assist at ground level by informing themselves about the legal system and stepping into their "true position as the de jure civilian authority." The author appends an explicit disclaimer that no specific action is being recommended, and describes the situation as "all hands on deck," arguing that learning and speaking the truth has greater impact than most realize.

## Conclusion

The article closes by acknowledging that many readers already know the claim that the United States is a corporation, while reassuring newcomers that "we all start somewhere." Its stated hope is to frame the problem persuasively enough that readers can discuss it with friends and family; the divergence between the "law" and the "narrative," the author writes, is so profound that merely sharing the Title 28 citation may suffice to wake "sleeping" people. The author offers himself as an example, noting that American Hypnotist was "a hardcore 'lefty' until his late twenties," when these and related facts were first brought to his attention. The piece ends with the formulation: "The law belongs to the people—but only through knowledge and vigilance can the people hope to keep it."
