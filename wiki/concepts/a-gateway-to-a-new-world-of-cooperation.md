# A Gateway to a New World of Cooperation

"A Gateway to a New World of Cooperation," subtitled "Analyzing a Major Week in Tianjin," is a Badlands Media article published on September 3, 2025 by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]. It interprets the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit held in Tianjin, China, on August 31 and September 1, 2025 as the visible emergence of an alternative security and financial architecture, and argues that the West should convene an emergency peace summit modelled on historical precedents rather than drift toward nuclear war.

## The Tianjin summit

The article reports that Presidents Narendra Modi, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin — described as representing the three founding nations behind the [[shanghai-cooperation-organisation|SCO]] — stood together in Tianjin alongside other world leaders whose states account for nearly half the world's population and 20 percent of world GDP. Together, the author writes, they represented "a new financial/security architecture premised on immutable principles" set out in the [[tianjin-declaration|Tianjin Declaration]].

Ehret summarises those principles in five points: total respect for the sovereign equality of nations, as against what he calls the "completely arbitrary" [[rules-based-international-order|Rules Based International Order]]; the rejection of unilateral coercive measures; the creation of a new SCO Development Bank to provide productive credit for all members; resistance to what the declaration frames as the rehabilitation of Nazism growing throughout the West; and the position that a just settlement of the Palestinian question is the only way to ensure peace and stability in the Middle East.

The article asserts that the foundations of an alternative security architecture with a vital financial component are "coming online," and suggests this is "perhaps why convenient fires were also lit" in the same period — citing bloody confrontations launched by Azerbaijan against SCO member Armenia, and fighting between Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, both SCO members hosting Russian bases and Chinese Belt and Road–connected infrastructure. While conceding that the identity of "the arsonists lighting these fires" is not yet known, the author writes that "the timing and character of conflicts smells of Anglo-Intelligence operations."

According to the source, the common theme of the summit and of the broader [[multipolar-alliance|multipolar alliance]] is the common good, common security, and coordinated action to improve the conditions of life of peoples and sovereign nations as outlined by the [[un-charter|UN Charter]] — which Ehret distinguishes sharply from the "rules based international order." He laments that even relatively sane Western voices appear unaware of what he calls the epoch-making decisions being made, and states that the multipolar powers have made clear they would prefer cooperation with the trans-Atlantic community over war, even though events have forced them to prepare for the latter. "If there were ever a time for humility and self-reflection amongst the leaders of the west," he writes, "that time is now."

## The nature of peace summits of the past

The article argues that in pre-nuclear epochs, peace conferences were typically arranged only after years of devastating war. It cites the 1648 Peace of Westphalia, which emerged from the Thirty Years' War and established the framework of the modern sovereign nation-state system at the heart of international law; the Congress of Vienna of 1815, described as a weak attempt following two decades of Napoleonic war; the League of Nations created after "the meat grinder of WW1"; and the [[united-nations|United Nations]] and [[bretton-woods-system|Bretton Woods System]] brought online after the Second World War. Some of these conferences, the author allows, were shaped by honest agendas and some were not, but in every case all parties agreed that a new system was necessary to avoid a plunge into war.

Ehret adds that human memory is weak and that "the Byzantine web of power politics in which the Anglo-American deep state operates is not easily transformed by conferences." Sitting on the precipice of another plunge into war and economic collapse, he argues, the existence of atomic weapons has made the errors of the past intolerable. While some RAND Corporation analysts and representatives of the military-industrial complex may regard a new world war as an exciting and even winnable prospect according to their computer-modelled scenarios — the article links to reporting on a Palantir executive telling Americans to prepare for a three-front war with Russia, China and Iran — the author insists that "such a war could never be won." Next-generation hypersonic missile technologies unveiled by both Russia and China, including underwater drones, have in his view demonstrated that a first-strike monopoly by [[nato|NATO]] is "a pipe dream," and that no attack on Russia or China could occur without retaliation destroying every major city in the West.

## Two options

The article poses the alternatives starkly. One option would be for Russia, China, India and the other multipolar states to abandon their sovereignty and their desire for a system based on win-win cooperation, and to accept a Great Reset managed by "a nest of technocratic unipolar priests" running a world government above elected regimes. This, Ehret writes, would require erasing or rewriting the UN Charter, which premises international law on national sovereignty, non-interference and mutual cooperation, and would require states to accept "new systems of draconian population reduction under the rubric of 'decarbonization'" in exchange for a promised order of peace, degrowth and sustainability. Based on the profiles, words and performance of multipolar leaders over the past decade, the author concludes, "they won't do this."

## The need for a different kind of reset

The second option, which the article endorses, is a summit convened before nuclear war rather than after it, oriented not toward an anti-humanist Great Reset or transhumanist new world order but toward solving objective problems on which all peoples can agree. Ehret grounds this in Franklin Roosevelt's Four Freedoms of 1941 — freedom from want, freedom from fear of war, freedom of speech and freedom of conscience — which he presents as the backbone of the Atlantic Charter (in its original form, before what he calls the Biden–Johnson edits), the UN Charter, and the original design of the Bretton Woods system. He notes that the same universal freedoms were restated in the UN Declaration of Human Rights of 1947 and in the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence that emerged from the Bandung Conference of 1955, which set the tone for the Non Aligned Movement.

At Bretton Woods, the article recalls, a new financial system was created in two weeks on banking principles that demanded fixed exchange rates to block unbounded speculation, which had been used as economic warfare against poor nations for centuries. It was premised on emitting long-term credit for large-scale projects in order to internationalise the New Deal in an era envisioned as one of multipolar cooperation and win-win development. The UN as envisioned by Roosevelt or Henry Wallace, Ehret writes, was never meant to be a world government but "a platform for dialogue and harmonization of security and economic interests among civilizations." Although the Bretton Woods institutions — the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]] and the [[world-bank|World Bank]] — and the U.S. government itself were, in his account, hijacked after the Second World War by deep state operatives representing "the worst oligarchical inclinations of humanity," he argues the noble origins of these institutions, which John F. Kennedy and to a lesser extent [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] attempted to revive, should not be ignored.

## Existing multipolar development architecture

The article contends that the discussion it calls for has already begun among leaders representing the majority of the world's population, as seen in Tianjin. It states that the development programs unveiled by China's productive financial architecture and its growing array of allies have already unleashed over $3 trillion in large-scale infrastructure investment through the [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]]. This is described as increasingly tied to the [[eurasian-economic-union|Eurasian Economic Union]], which is preparing the groundwork for a fully integrated financial architecture founded on real-world values as outlined on numerous occasions by [[sergey-glazyev|Sergey Glazyev]].

Ehret observes the irony that Roosevelt's Four Freedoms are now being advanced by Eurasian powers "considered by many to be the most 'un-American' groups on earth." He argues there is no shortage of cooperative projects to build across Asia, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Americas that could create real employment, rebuild decayed factories and infrastructure, and establish trust among nations long set against one another. The piece closes with an appeal to "sane forces" among Western citizens and policymakers to organise and bring their nations into harmony with this discussion, warning that "the clock is ticking fast, and if we miss this opportunity now, the only peace the world may ever find will accompany a long nuclear winter."
