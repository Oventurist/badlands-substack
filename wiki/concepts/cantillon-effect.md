# Cantillon effect

The Cantillon effect describes the uneven distribution of the benefits of newly created money, in which those positioned closest to the source of monetary expansion capture purchasing power before prices adjust for everyone else. A July 11, 2024 Badlands Media essay on the exploitation of trust invokes the idea through the coinage "Cantillionaires," the class of actors who have mastered the game by perpetually sitting closer and closer to the source of debasement.[1]

## The argument

The essay's frame is that money is trust: until January 3, 2009 humanity had only trust-based options for money — seashells, glass beads, precious stones, precious metals and government-issued paper currencies — and the physical nature of these monies forced people to trust that others would not exploit them through dishonest scales, closed trade routes, sanctions, counterfeiting, double spending, theft or market manipulation.[1] On that reading, [[fiat-currency|fiat currencies]] exploit trust by design, and the present system amounts to one massive, intentional, continuous exploitation of trust.[1] The essay quotes Robert Breedlove's formulation that "inflation is theft."[1]

Because the incentives of the current system are backwards, the essay argues, this exploitation of trust yields an effectively infinite amount of purchasing power that the Cantillionaires can wield to defend their position and further their interests.[1] Extending a scriptural theme from an earlier Badlands piece on fifteen years of indictments, it suggests that "the love of trust" could be substituted for "the love of money" in the verse identifying the love of money as a root of all kinds of evil, since history shows such actors are overly determined — often tyrannical — to obtain, keep and gain more trust by any means necessary.[1]

## Proposed remedy

The essay's answer is trust-less money: [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]], which allows any two willing parties to transact directly without a trusted third party and so, in its phrasing, allows populations to regain their trust.[1] This connects the monetary argument to the national-security thesis of [[softwar|Softwar]] and to the reading of [[trust-the-plan|"TRUST the plan"]] as a description of how trust was exploited rather than a call to extend more of it.[1]
