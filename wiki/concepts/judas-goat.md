# Judas Goat

**Judas goat** is a term drawn from slaughterhouse practice, defined figuratively as "any person or thing used as a decoy to lure people into being caught, arrested, etc."[1] In commentary published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], the phrase was adopted as an analytical metaphor for a political figure deliberately elevated by an opponent so that the faction following that figure would be led into exposure or ruin.[1]

## Use in Badlands Media commentary

In the January 18, 2023 article "The Chosen One," contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] applied the Judas goat metaphor to [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], asking whether [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had "installed his very own Judas Goat as head of the insurgency."[1] The argument held that Trump, described as a "Master 5D chess player," had manipulated media coverage during the 2020 Democratic presidential primary so that Democratic voters would select the opponent Trump most wanted to face and most wanted installed in office.[1]

Under this reading, the value of a Judas goat is not electoral defeat but institutional exposure: a weak, compromised, or unpopular leader placed at the head of an opposing movement drags that movement into scandals it cannot survive.[1] Lange paired the term with a second definition-driven metaphor, the **trap** — "a position or situation from which it is difficult or impossible to escape" — to describe what he called "the biggest sting in world history."[1]

## Relationship to related narrative concepts

The Judas goat framing sits alongside other interpretive devices used in the same community, including [[trust-the-plan|"trust the plan"]] and the [[qanon|QAnon]] maxim that there are "no coincidences," both of which treat visible political chaos as choreography rather than accident.[1] Lange summarized the underlying claim with the phrase "Think Mirror," arguing that investigative pressure once directed at Trump would be redirected at Biden because "turnabout is fair play."[1]

The concept is explicitly presented as unprovable opinion; Lange wrote that he could not prove the leverage he described and left the conclusion "for you to decide."[1]
