# Always Fight Back

"Always Fight Back: Lessons from Lawfare in the US District of Colorado" is a first-person essay published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on August 13, 2024 and written by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who was a named defendant in the civil case [[naacp-v-useip|NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.]] The piece recounts the author's successful pro se defense at trial in the [[us-district-court-for-the-district-of-colorado|United States District Court for the District of Colorado]] and uses the case as a lens through which to argue that so-called "election denial" and door-to-door canvassing are constitutionally protected activities rather than voter intimidation.

## The case and its outcome

According to the source, three organizations — the Colorado/Montana/Wyoming Area Conference of the [[naacp|NAACP]], the [[league-of-women-voters|League of Women Voters of Colorado]], and [[mi-familia-vota|Mi Familia Vota]] — filed suit on March 9, 2022 against the [[useip|United States Election Integrity Plan]] (USEIP) and several individual defendants. The complaint contained two counts under Section 11(b) of the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]] and one count under the [[ku-klux-klan-act-of-1871|Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871]], alleging that the defendants were "planning to, threatening to, and actually deploying armed agents to knock on doors throughout the state of Colorado," with "the purpose and effect of intimidating Coloradans from voting, trying to vote, helping others to vote, supporting or advocating for certain political beliefs."

Trial commenced on July 15, 2024. The author writes that the court cut the planned five-day trial short after three days, entering a Judgment Based on Partial Findings under Rule 52(c) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure — meaning the defense prevailed without having to present its case in chief — and awarding costs to the defendants. The essay quotes Judge [[charlotte-sweeney|Charlotte Sweeney]]: "In sum, plaintiffs have failed to introduce any evidence that can remotely be perceived as intimidating or threatening on behalf of the three defendants." The author characterizes the litigation, which ran for 208 days from the filing of the operative affidavit through trial, as an "unprecedented attack on the First Amendment by the leftist lawfare machine, under the guise of 'fighting voter suppression.'"

## Historical argument: questioning elections as an American tradition

A substantial portion of the essay is devoted to a historical survey intended to show that questioning election outcomes is normal, protected, and historically productive of reform. The article recounts the 1800 presidential contest between [[thomas-jefferson|Thomas Jefferson]] and incumbent John Adams, which went to the House of Representatives and was resolved only after 36 rounds of voting, inspiring the Twelfth Amendment. It notes the 1824 election of John Quincy Adams, likewise decided in the House after 36 ballots. The 1860 election is described as unprecedented because [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]]'s name was removed from the ballot in ten Southern states, with South Carolina voting to secede weeks after his victory — a parallel the author draws to modern ballot-removal efforts. The 1864 wartime election introduced mail-in ballots, which many at the time argued were vulnerable to manipulation.

The essay then turns to [[tammany-hall|Tammany Hall]] and William "Boss" Tweed, who rose to power in New York in 1863 and whose corruption was widely known by 1871. It quotes Tweed biographer Kenneth D. Ackerman: "It's hard not to admire the skill behind Tweed's system ... The Tweed ring at its height was an engineering marvel, strong and solid, strategically deployed to control key power points: the courts, the legislature, the treasury and the ballot box. Its frauds had a grandeur of scale and an elegance of structure: money-laundering, profit sharing and organization." The author observes that Tweed punished those who questioned his electoral practices and died in prison in 1878, concluding that "election denialism is often a response to election fraud."

Further examples include the disputed 1876 election between Samuel Tilden and Rutherford B. Hayes, resolved by a fifteen-member congressional commission and the February 1877 compromise that ended Reconstruction by withdrawing federal troops from the South; and the 1888 election, by which time "ballot fraud was so common, it developed its own vocabulary," with "colonizers," "floaters," and "repeaters" described as distinct categories of fraudulent voting, especially in Indiana where no voter registration existed. Grover Cleveland's call for citizens to rise above "lethargy and indifference" and "restore the purity of their suffrage" is credited with catalyzing a ballot-reform movement that brought the secret ballot to 38 states by 1892.

In the modern era, the article cites the 1948 Chicago Tribune headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" as evidence of media fallibility, noting that only one in three Americans approved of Harry S. Truman and that Truman himself believed he had lost on election night. It then discusses the 2000 election, in which broadcast media prematurely called Florida for [[al-gore|Al Gore]] and the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ultimately halted the Florida recount on equal-protection grounds, awarding the presidency to [[george-w-bush|George W. Bush]]. That controversy produced the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]], signed in 2002, which the author argues "gave birth to the elections industry" and left U.S. elections "increasingly centralized and dependent on private vendors with little meaningful oversight."

The essay stresses that since HAVA no presidential election has occurred without allegations of irregularity from one side or the other: John Kerry raised voter-access concerns in 2004; ACORN acknowledged in 2008 that of 1.3 million new voters registered, only about 450,000 were legitimate; and the Pew Research Center's "2012 Survey of the Performance of American Elections" found nearly 20 million voters experienced at least one problem in 2012. After [[donald-trump|Donald J. Trump]]'s 2016 victory over [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]], the author notes, Democrats alleged Russian collusion and launched the Mueller investigation, which found no evidence of collusion; Clinton wrote what the author calls "an election-denying book"; Democrats produced the documentary *Kill Chain: The Cyber War on America's Elections*; and litigation such as *Curling v. Raffensperger* continued in Georgia. Specific to the Colorado case, the article points out that plaintiff League of Women Voters published "The 2016 Presidential Election was Rigged" on November 23, 2016, and that plaintiffs' counsel [[free-speech-for-people|Free Speech For People]] launched an impeachment campaign on Inauguration Day, January 20, 2017, announced in a Washington Post article titled "The Campaign to Impeach President Trump Has Begun."

## Canvassing and the First Amendment

The article argues that canvassing is likewise not inherently intimidating, noting that all three plaintiff organizations engage in political canvassing as a matter of course and that the ACLU publishes practical guidance for canvassers on their rights. The author's trial brief, filed before trial, is cited as containing the relevant history and case law. The essay's summary formulation is that election denying is not intimidating, canvassing is not intimidating, and election deniers engaging in canvassing is also not intimidating — "It's only a problem when it involves 2020 and President Trump." While conceding that holding such a view in the media is protected by the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]], the author contends it is another matter for three civil rights organizations, two law firms, and at least six attorneys to advance it in court without evidence, and concludes the claims were frivolous because no evidence of intimidation emerged in discovery, in two and a half years of motions practice, or at trial.

## Origins of the plaintiffs' legal theory

The essay traces the plaintiffs' theory to a 2015 article in the NYU Social Justice Law Review titled ["Voters Strike Back: Litigating against Modern Voter Intimidation"](https://socialchangenyu.com/review/voters-strike-back-litigating-against-modern-voter-intimidation/), which the plaintiffs' expert witness cited in his official report. That paper argued that "section 11(b) was a deliberate attempt to expand the existing laws against voter intimidation, including by eliminating any legal requirement of racial targeting," and that voters today "are deterred from voting through subtler tactics, such as aggressive poll-watching, anonymous threats of harm, frivolous and excessive voter registration challenges, and coercion by employers."

The author highlights passages she regards as objectively unethical and closely mirrored by the plaintiffs' conduct: that "another reason to bring a KKK Act claim is for the purpose of political messaging," since "liability under the Act carries the additional stigma of conspiracy and its association with the KKK's legacy of politicalized racism"; that the Act "creates the opportunity to brand a particular ballot security group or party committee with these deeply embarrassing associations," which "could strengthen the law's deterrent effect and help drive settlement"; and that "success is defined as stopping the defendant's conduct." The lone plaintiff representative who testified at trial, the author writes, admitted that stopping the defendants' conduct was the overriding goal.

## The coached witness

The essay's central factual indictment concerns the only witness who claimed both to have been approached by USEIP and to have been intimidated by whoever came to her door. Under oath, the article states, she admitted she had named "USEIP volunteers" only at the suggestion of plaintiffs' counsel. Judge Sweeney is quoted: "It was upon suggestion apparently from plaintiffs' counsel that USEIP was the group canvassing in her county that she got – at which point she adopted that position and included it in her affidavit." The author notes that the affidavit, signed under penalty of perjury, was filed with the court in December 2022 to defeat the defense's motion for summary judgment, and describes the finding as "a damning rebuke of both the civil rights organizations and their lawyers."

## Conclusion

The article closes on the personal cost of the litigation — reputation, career, family, and sanity — observing that an award of costs "doesn't even begin to cover it," while affirming that complete vindication in federal court restored the author's faith in justice. Its final admonition is that "justice doesn't just happen. You must show up and fight for it."
