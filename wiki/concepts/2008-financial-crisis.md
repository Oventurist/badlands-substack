# 2008 Financial Crisis

The 2008 financial crisis originated in the American housing market, where, as Joe Lange describes it, Americans were told real estate prices would rise forever and that income was not needed to buy a house.[1] Rapidly rising prices created a phony wealth effect and euphoria while [[citibank|Citibank]] and other Wall Street firms packaged delinquent and underwater mortgage loans into securities, rated them AAA to signal little or no risk, and sold them worldwide, including to pension funds.[1]

Lange argues the scheme was not new but a revival of the template pioneered by [[charles-e-mitchell|Charles E. Mitchell]] at [[national-city-bank|National City Bank]] before the [[stock-market-crash-of-1929|1929 crash]], made global by Citibank's international branch network.[1] He attributes the crisis directly to the 1999 repeal of the [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall Act]], which permitted the merger of Citibank and Travelers — owner of Salomon Brothers and Smith Barney — to form [[citigroup|Citigroup]].[1]

Citigroup received what Lange calls the biggest bailout in world history: according to *Borrowed Time* by James Freeman and Vern McKinley, more than $517 billion all told, some $40 billion more than the roughly $476 billion in cash and guarantees described in a 2011 congressional report.[1] The authors argue the bank had teetered on the edge of ruin repeatedly across its 206-year history, only to be rescued each time by friends in high places.[1]

## Cited in Structures of Control

Part 3 of Badlands Media's *Structures of Control* series describes the 2008 financial crisis as "orchestrated by supernodes like [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]]," arguing that it revealed how financial networks consolidate power under the guise of stability.[7] The essay pairs it with the COVID-19 pandemic as an emotional conditioning loop: manufactured crises synchronized across media, politics and economics to engineer fear and submission and to lock society into survival-mode thinking (see [[manufactured-5d|manufactured 5D]]).[7]

## Cited as a driver of generational dependence (February 2025)

A February 11, 2025 Badlands Media essay cited the 2008 financial crisis as a case study in what it called [[generational-dependence-syndrome|Generational Dependence Syndrome]].[2] The author argued that the collapse of small banks during the crisis forced populations toward "too big to fail" institutions, further concentrating financial power.[2] This consolidation was said to have entrenched generational reliance on centralized banking systems and reshaped societal perceptions of financial security, with families increasingly equating stability with alignment to those monolithic institutions while decentralized alternatives were marginalized or discredited.[2] In the essay's framework, the episode illustrates how economic policy functions as one arm of a self-reinforcing feedback loop of dependency alongside media and education.[2]

## Effect on the Atlantic City casino sector

A June 15, 2024 Badlands feature by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] noted that the 2008 financial crisis caused a sudden decline in [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s hotel and casino business, and that within five years he was seeking to exit the casino space altogether.[3] Trump's 2013 attempt to sell the Trump Plaza Hotel and Casino in Atlantic City was blocked by [[carl-icahn|Carl Icahn]], the senior lender on its mortgage; the property closed in 2014 and was demolished in 2021.[3]

## Catalyst for digital assets

Badlands Media identified the 2008 financial crisis and the ensuing "Great Recession" as the primary catalysts for the rise of new digital asset classes over the following ten to fifteen years.[4] As more people became aware of the power held by the central banks, solution-oriented innovators sought to reclaim some of that power through decentralization and digitization — the impulse that produced [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]], [[ethereum|Ethereum]], and eventually more than 21,000 cryptocurrencies.[4]

## As a case study in systemic consolidation

The "Structures of Control" series presents the 2008 crisis as a demonstration of how crises are leveraged to consolidate power across interconnected systems of finance, government, and global institutions.[5] While smaller banks collapsed under the weight of toxic assets, institutions deemed "too big to fail" such as [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] and [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan Chase]] emerged more powerful, acting as critical hubs controlling capital flows and influencing global economic policy.[5] Central banks including the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the [[european-central-bank|European Central Bank]] coordinated massive bailouts that, in this account, reinforced the dominance of those financial supernodes rather than merely stabilizing markets.[5]

The stated outcome was an acceleration of centralization within a tightly controlled network of multinational financial institutions now able to dictate policies shaping governments, corporations, and individual behavior.[5] Regulatory measures such as Dodd-Frank, though intended to prevent future crises, are said to have further entrenched these institutions by disadvantaging smaller competitors.[5] Ripple effects including austerity measures and rising inequality are described as strengthening reliance on centralized financial systems while eroding local and national sovereignty.[5]

Applying network theory, the article argues that nodes with the highest connectivity hold the most influence, and that the surviving institutions became "supernodes" — central command points directing capital, information, and power, which dictated the terms of connectivity rather than merely participating in it.[5] The collapse of [[lehman-brothers|Lehman Brothers]] is characterized as an orchestrated disruption whose shockwaves were meticulously managed to safeguard overall network stability.[5]

## Carlson's Aladdin-overconfidence account (2023)

Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] has attributed the 2008 collapse in part to the spread of predictive risk software.[6] After [[blackrock|BlackRock]] began licensing its [[aladdin|Aladdin]] system to outside firms in 1999, insurance companies and investment houses alike came to rely on its [[monte-carlo-simulation|Monte Carlo]] scenario modelling, and the financial system became, in Carlson's phrase, more and more predictable.[6] Investment banking companies grew comfortable taking more risk because they believed they understood that risk better, and the added risk promised more reward.[6]

For a while it worked and financial markets grew substantially, but in 2008 many of those firms found themselves holding risky portfolios when everything collapsed.[6] Carlson compared the failure to increasingly accurate weather forecasting, which leaves people without a jacket or umbrella on the day the storm arrives: an over-reliance on AI, he wrote, caught these firms with their pants down.[6] He added that the banks partly expected rescue under [[too-big-to-fail|"too big to fail"]] reasoning, but that the deeper cause was disillusionment — a belief that they could do no wrong and that caution merely ate away at profits.[6]
