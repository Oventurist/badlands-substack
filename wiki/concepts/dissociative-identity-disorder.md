# Dissociative Identity Disorder

Dissociative identity disorder (DID) is the diagnosis formerly known as multiple personality disorder (MPD), central to the clinical material presented in the Badlands Media article "Gazing Into the Abyss pt.2: a Therapist's Tale."[1] The name change from MPD to DID reflected a revised view that the patient is one fragmented person rather than several different people sharing one body, though the article notes that most people with the condition experience it more as MPD described it.[1]

## Parts and alters

The terms "parts" and "alters" are used to describe fractured, compartmentalized aspects of the primary or "host" identity that have acquired a life and memory of their own.[1] This fragmentation is said to occur when trauma is so severe that the host identity could not continue functioning if it retained awareness of what happened.[1] Illustrative cases include a wheelchair-using woman whose alters ranged from a gentle adult to a playful five-year-old with giant dolls to a mute, terrified three-year-old, and a young man misdiagnosed as schizophrenic who carried a child alter alongside alters representing each member of the band Guns N' Roses.[1]

## Link to organized abuse

At a workshop on dissociation organized by clinical psychologist [[alison-miller|Alison Miller]] using sexual abuse prevention funds, she learned that many people suffering from MPD had been severely abused throughout childhood by organized groups, including Satanic and other "dark-side" religious cults.[1] More disturbing still, a number of them were still involved with those groups without conscious awareness, because it was dissociated parts of them that attended the rituals.[1] Miller records that she was skeptical at the time, but her skepticism soon waned.[1] Because alters can be separately conditioned, perpetrators are described as able to install "programs" that operate outside the host's awareness, enabling covert contact, reporting on therapy sessions and self-harm.[1]

## Professional contestation

Acceptance of the dissociative disorder diagnosis was not widespread, and during the [[memory-wars|"memory wars"]] of the 1990s it came under sustained attack.[1] The [[false-memory-syndrome-foundation|False Memory Syndrome Foundation]] and similar organized political groups pressed the media with the claim that unethical therapists were suggesting to helpless clients that they had been sexually abused, or worse, by family members.[1] Leading therapists in the field of dissociative disorders, including Bennett Braun and Judith Peterson, were targeted with lawsuits and, in Peterson's case, criminal charges, while insurers tended to settle out of court in a way that implied guilt.[1] The journal *Treating Abuse Today* was forced out of business by picketers representing supposedly innocent accused parents.[1]

## Prognosis

The article emphasizes that recovery is possible: two of Miller's four earliest such clients reintegrated their alters, recovered from their trauma, and now live productive lives away from the group that abused them.[1]
