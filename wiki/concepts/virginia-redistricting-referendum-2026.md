# Virginia Redistricting Referendum (2026)

The **Virginia redistricting referendum of 2026** was a proposed state constitutional amendment, approved by Virginia voters on April 21, 2026, that would have temporarily shifted congressional map-drawing authority away from the bipartisan redistricting framework Virginians established by ballot measure in 2020.[1] It was struck down by the [[virginia-supreme-court|Virginia Supreme Court]] on May 8, 2026, before it could take effect.[1]

## Background and content

The referendum reached the ballot after months of legal challenges.[1] The congressional map associated with the amendment was projected to favor Democrats in as many as 10 of Virginia's 11 U.S. House districts, making it one of the highest-stakes fronts in the national [[2026-redistricting-battle|2026 redistricting battle]] ahead of the [[2026-midterm-elections|midterm elections]].[1] Proponents framed the temporary suspension of the bipartisan framework as an effort "to promote fairness."[1]

## Clearance for the April 21 vote

On February 13, 2026, the [[virginia-supreme-court|Virginia Supreme Court]] cleared the way for the April 21, 2026, referendum, ruling that voters could decide the plan even as legal challenges continued.[2] The measure, passed by the Democratic majority in the [[virginia-general-assembly|General Assembly]], would have amended the state constitution to permit [[mid-decade-redistricting|mid-decade redistricting]] of U.S. House districts, a departure from the traditional post-census process.[2] If approved, the redrawn map could have allowed Democrats to expand their share of Virginia's 11-member U.S. House delegation from six seats to as many as ten, making the referendum a national focal point in the broader battle over control of Congress.[2] A lower court had previously blocked the referendum on procedural grounds, but the Supreme Court reversed that decision and allowed the special election to proceed while litigation continued.[2]

## Invalidation

In a 4–3 decision, the Virginia Supreme Court held that the amendment process used by the [[virginia-general-assembly|General Assembly]] violated the state constitution because the legislature approved the amendment after early voting for the prior general election had already begun.[1] The court read the constitutional phrase "next general election" to encompass the early voting period rather than Election Day alone.[1] Republicans argued the sequence disenfranchised voters who had already cast ballots before the measure was finalized.[1] Democrats announced plans to appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands writers treated the saga less as a partisan win than as evidence of systemic electoral illegitimacy.[1] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the crisis was avoidable, that the court — not the Democratic-controlled Commonwealth pressing its case — bore responsibility for creating it, and that the affair pushed the public toward recognizing that elections are "corruption all the way down."[1] She cited [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]]'s position that the most logical response is to cancel the midterms until real elections are possible, adding that "we cannot have real elections until we are honest about how fake they've been."[1]
