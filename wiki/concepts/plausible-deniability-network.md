# Plausible Deniability Network

The "plausible deniability network" is the term Badlands Media uses for the layered system of government agencies, quasi-governmental institutes, and nominally private non-governmental organizations through which Western intelligence services conduct influence and regime-change operations at arm's length.[1] The concept is the organizing frame of the first installment in a Badlands series on "Shadow Networks" in the Deep State's web of subversive influence.[1]

## Origins

The article traces the underlying practice to 1953, when the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and British [[mi6|MI6]] orchestrated the overthrow of Iran's democratically elected Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh in Operation Ajax (TPAJAX) — described as the first major documented instance of Western intelligence using paid and staged protests as the core mechanism of regime change.[1] Domestically, the same logic produced [[cointelpro|COINTELPRO]], the [[fbi|FBI]]'s covert and often illegal program that infiltrated and steered organic movements such as anti-war protesters, the Black Panthers, and the National Indian Movement, and altered their messaging.[1]

## The layering principle

A "plausible deniability layer" is an institution created to carry out work too controversial for the body above it. The article notes that one of the original reasons for creating the CIA was to perform operations too controversial for the [[state-department|State Department]] to be associated with.[1] Once the State Department layer was itself compromised, many CIA functions migrated to [[usaid|USAID]], an agency under the State Department umbrella.[1]

The [[church-committee|Church Committee]] and the Pike Committee investigations of the 1960s and 1970s exposed assassination plots, human experimentation, domestic surveillance, mail interception, propaganda dissemination, media manipulation, and other abuses, rendering the CIA "radioactive" and causing foreign governments to recoil from any CIA-adjacent organization.[1] Because the functions themselves were considered essential to Western hegemony, a new approach was required.[1]

## The NGO layer

By the 1980s, [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]]'s CIA director [[william-casey|William Casey]] won bipartisan support for a series of new institutions to carry on the CIA's former work at the NGO level rather than the agency level, ostensibly as Cold War instruments.[1] The governing idea was that the more deniability layers existed, the harder it would be to trace an operation back to the CIA, the [[pentagon|Department of Defense]], or the State Department.[1] The [[national-endowment-for-democracy|National Endowment for Democracy]] (NED) was the result; its long-time president Carl Gershman explained that it would be "terrible for democratic groups around the world to be seen as subsidized by the C.I.A.," and that this was "why the endowment was created."[1]

Beneath NED sat a set of specialized surrogates: the [[international-republican-institute|International Republican Institute]] (Republican politics), the National Democratic Institute (Democratic politics), the [[solidarity-center|Solidarity Center]] (unions and the workforce, affiliated with the [[afl-cio|AFL-CIO]]), and the Center for International Private Enterprise (business and chambers of commerce).[1] Outside the NED umbrella but within the same network sit the [[united-states-institute-of-peace|United States Institute of Peace]] (nonviolent action), the [[global-engagement-center|Global Engagement Center]] (propaganda and messaging), and [[freedom-house|Freedom House]] (academic legitimation of regime-change efforts).[1] All are described as existing inside a triangle formed by the State Department, the CIA, and the DoD, with USAID acting as the network's funding mechanism.[1]

## Modern operation

The article argues that the CIA today is not directly involved in such operations, instead relying on more disarming organizations, methods, and language, and on a global network of individuals and organizations in nearly every country touched by USAID — what [[george-soros|George Soros]] calls "open societies."[1] The declassified Contra-era manual [[psychological-operations-in-guerrilla-warfare|Psychological Operations in Guerrilla Warfare]], published less than a year before NED's founding, is presented as documentary evidence of the tradecraft that was subsequently rebranded as "[[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]]."[1]
