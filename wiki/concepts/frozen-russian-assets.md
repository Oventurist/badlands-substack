# Frozen Russian Assets

Frozen Russian assets are the sovereign and related Russian funds immobilized by Western governments following the escalation of the Ukraine conflict in February 2022, estimated at roughly $300 billion.[1] Some €200 billion ($213 billion) of that total is held by the Brussels-based clearinghouse [[euroclear|Euroclear]].[1] Ukraine's Western backers have already tapped the revenues generated by the funds to finance Kyiv, short of touching the principal.[1]

## The EU reparations loan

By October 2025 the [[european-union|European Union]] was discussing a so-called "reparations loan" of up to €140 billion ($163 billion) to Kyiv, using the frozen Russian assets as collateral backing bloc-issued bonds.[1] Because Ukraine would be obliged to repay the loan only once Russia compensated it for war damages, the arrangement would effectively amount to a seizure of the funds.[1]

## U.S. opposition

Bloomberg reported on October 20, 2025 that the United States would not join the EU-led plan.[1] U.S. officials reportedly conveyed the position to European counterparts during an [[international-monetary-fund|International Monetary Fund]] meeting in Washington the previous week, citing risks to market stability associated with the potential seizure.[1] The refusal was described as a major setback for the EU, which had been trying to secure broader support within the [[g7|G7]] for action on the Russian funds.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the U.S. position as President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] again shielding [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]], but argued the substance went beyond [[sovereign-alliance|Sovereign Alliance]] optics: Russia holds investments worldwide, particularly in energy — with [[rosatom|Rosatom]] singled out — and seizing them could trigger cascading effects producing economic devastation far beyond Russia itself.[1] He doubted the EU and [[nato|NATO]] were capable of that level of nuance.[1]

## December 2025 EU summit and US opposition

EU leaders met in Brussels on Thursday, December 18, 2025 to debate using an estimated €210 billion ($246 billion) in Russian central bank assets to back a so-called "reparations loan" for Kiev, a move Moscow condemned as "theft."[2] [[belgium|Belgium]], where most of the assets are held, demanded that the legal risks be shared among bloc members.[2] Politico and The Times reported, citing officials in Washington, that the [[european-union|EU]]'s insistence on seizing the assets stands in the way of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s attempts to resolve the Ukraine conflict.[2]

"The EU's problem isn't really Belgium, it's Trump," Politico wrote, describing US backchanneling with capitals that brought Italy, Bulgaria, Malta and the Czech Republic into the bloc of nations opposing the seizure.[2] The Times cited a source close to the US discussions saying that even if the money were seized, "the Europeans are going to have to give it back," with the frozen funds seen by Washington as a "crucial part of Trump's proposed settlement plan."[2] Under an early draft published by the media, Trump proposed that Russia's assets be unfrozen and invested in US-led reconstruction efforts in Ukraine as well as joint projects with Moscow.[2]

Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the episode as Trump coordinating with Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni to encourage EU states to defect and prevent the seized Russian assets from being plundered, predicting that Trump and [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] would soon be openly allied against common enemies in the Western hegemony.[2]

## 2024 G7 $50 billion decision

The first major use of the immobilized funds came in June 2024, when [[g7|G7]] leaders meeting at the [[2024-g7-summit-in-italy|summit in Italy]] agreed to send Ukraine $50 billion in aid by the end of the year, financed by profits generated by roughly $300 billion in sovereign Russian assets frozen in Western countries — an arrangement announced by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] on Thursday, June 13, 2024.[3] Western leaders had debated for months whether and how to leverage the assets for Ukraine's defense or recovery, with the United States proposing outright seizure and European leaders hesitant over legal and financial concerns.[3] The [[european-union|EU]] had instead agreed the previous month to aid Ukraine using only the interest thrown off by the assets, generating between $2.7 and $3.3 billion annually.[3] Analysts warned the move might open Western assets inside Russia to retaliatory confiscation under a decree signed by [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] earlier in 2024.[3] Badlands commentary characterized the decision as globalists looting their own national coffers and laundering the money through Ukraine.[3]

## Meloni's warning and the reparations loan (October 2025)

Italian Prime Minister [[giorgia-meloni|Giorgia Meloni]] called on the EU to increase pressure on Moscow while cautioning the bloc to respect international law in any plan to use the frozen assets, making the remarks in an address to the Italian Senate on Wednesday, October 22, 2025, ahead of the [[european-council|European Council]] summit in Brussels.[4] Western countries blocked an estimated $300 billion of Moscow's funds after the escalation of the Ukraine conflict in February 2022, some €200 billion ($213 billion) of which is held by the Brussels-based clearinghouse [[euroclear|Euroclear]]; revenues generated by the assets have already been tapped to bankroll Kiev, and Russia has described those steps as "theft" while vowing retaliation.[4] The [[european-commission|European Commission]] proposed using proceeds from around €210 billion in frozen assets held in the EU to guarantee loans to [[ukraine|Ukraine]] in 2026 and 2027 — the [[ukraine-reparations-loan|Ukraine reparations loan]] — as US military aid declined and EU budgets tightened.[4]

Analysis circulating at the time held that the loan did not require unanimity, and therefore could not be vetoed by [[viktor-orban|Viktor Orbán]] or [[robert-fico|Robert Fico]], but did require approval from [[belgium|Belgium]] and the [[european-central-bank|European Central Bank]].[4] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] framed Meloni's intervention as following the Trump administration's own opposition to seizure, putting "the EU communists on notice," and wondered aloud whether the EU charter might eventually be challenged by Russia over the bloc's conduct in Ukraine.[4]
