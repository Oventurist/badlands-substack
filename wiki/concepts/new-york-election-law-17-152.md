# New York Election Law 17-152

New York Law 17-152 is a state election statute that became central to the Manhattan District Attorney's 2024 criminal prosecution of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] as the predicate offense elevating business-records charges.[1]

## Text and use at trial

The statute provides that any two or more persons who conspire to promote or prevent the election of any person to a public office by unlawful means, where the conspiracy is acted upon by one or more of the parties, shall be guilty of a misdemeanor.[1]

New York prosecutor [[joshua-steinglass|Joshua Steinglass]] told the jury that one of the crimes Trump allegedly committed, in listing payments to [[stormy-daniels|Stormy Daniels]] as a "legal expense," was a violation of 17-152.[1] The theory, as characterized by legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]], was that Trump committed a crime by conspiring to unlawfully promote his own candidacy — by paying to quash a potentially embarrassing story and then reimbursing his lawyer through entries booked as legal expenses.[1]

## Criticism

Turley argued that after months of confusion over what crime the indictment actually alleged, the prosecution had offered a new theory so ambiguous and undefined that it recalled Justice Potter Stewart's famous obscenity formulation in *Jacobellis v. Ohio*, 378 U.S. 184 (1964) — "I know it when I see it."[1] He described the case brought by [[alvin-bragg|Alvin Bragg]] as bordering on the legally obscene: an openly political prosecution resting on a theory that even some liberal commentators had dismissed.[1]

Badlands commentary framed the reliance on 17-152 as evidence that the prosecution had been forced into a war of attrition, in which the lawfare apparatus risked either establishing legal precedent it would rather avoid or being seen abandoning the case in full public view.[1]
