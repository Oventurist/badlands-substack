# Dope, Inc.: Britain's Opium War Against the World

*Dope, Inc.: Britain's Opium War Against the World* is a book first published in 1978 and commissioned by the controversial American political figure [[lyndon-larouche|Lyndon LaRouche]], written by the editors of the [[executive-intelligence-review|Executive Intelligence Review]], the weekly newsmagazine LaRouche founded in 1974.[1] The book's central thesis is that international drug trafficking is not a criminal fringe activity but a structural feature of the world banking system, organized historically by British imperial and financial interests.[1]

## Thesis

The book argues that the global narcotics trade originated as a deliberate instrument of British statecraft during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and that the proceeds of that trade were subsequently institutionalized through banking, offshore finance, and real estate.[1] In the framing adopted by Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]], the work "filled in a lot of the gaps" regarding why particular historical events and narratives occurred and how apparently unconnected actors were in fact coordinated — a Deep State cabal "whose tentacles reach far and wide."[1] Carlson summarized the underlying claim he had once mocked: that if illegal drugs were eliminated, the world's financial system would collapse.[1]

## Key claims cited

On the establishment of the [[hsbc|Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation]] after the second Opium War, the book states that HSBC "became the central clearinghouse for all Far Eastern financial transactions relating to the black market in opium and its heroin derivative," and that by 1860 Britain controlled seven-eighths of the vastly expanded trade into China.[1]

On the American [[prohibition|Prohibition]] era, the book states that "Prohibition brought narcotics traffic, narcotics traffickers, and large-scale organized crime into the US. Illegal alcohol and illegal narcotics made up two different product lines of the same multinational firm," with the British supplying both through Scottish and Canadian distilleries and Shanghai and Hong Kong opium refineries, and financing the traffic through banks in Canada and the Caribbean.[1]

On the fall of [[richard-nixon|Richard Nixon]], the book argues that while Nixon "generically understood the top-down nature of the problem," neither he nor his assistants grasped that by attacking the drug infrastructure they were "taking on the entire British oligarchy and the entire underpinnings of the [[eurodollar|Eurodollar]] market and the People's Republic of China," and that had he understood the drug problem as a "London-Peking problem" he might have been better prepared for the "inside-outside" attack on his presidency.[1]

## Reception and controversy

LaRouche, who died in 2019, was described variously as a Marxist in his youth and later as a right-wing extremist; Carlson interprets this trajectory as a consistent disdain for government and establishment institutions expressed first from the left and then from the right.[1] The [[anti-defamation-league|Anti-Defamation League]] labeled LaRouche an antisemite despite his denunciations of antisemitism, and LaRouche in turn regarded the ADL as a Deep State propaganda tool.[1]
