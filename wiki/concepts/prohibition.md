# Prohibition

Prohibition was the United States ban on alcohol enacted in the early twentieth century. In the account given in [[dope-inc-book|*Dope, Inc.*]] and summarized by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]], Prohibition was not a spontaneous moral reform but the vehicle through which British opium traffickers declared war on the United States in the early 1920s — a war that first required Prohibition itself to be manufactured.[1]

## Manufacturing the ban

The [[womens-christian-temperance-union|Women's Christian Temperance Union]] and its [[anti-saloon-league|Anti-Saloon League]] offshoots are described as a small, well-financed and highly organized circle enjoying the financial backing of the Astors, the Vanderbilts, the Warburgs, and the Rockefellers — backers with direct connections to the American financial system, the [[city-of-london|City of London]], real estate, and the elite generally.[1] The movement was run by [[jane-addams|Jane Addams]], who studied the [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]]'s London settlement house experiment at [[toynbee-hall|Toynbee Hall]].[1]

At the same time that the Rockefellers were funding groups demonstrating for Prohibition, they were also taking control of the medical industry, converting the medical system from herbal-based medicines to pharmacology.[1]

## Consequences

*Dope, Inc.* states: "Prohibition brought narcotics traffic, narcotics traffickers, and large-scale organized crime into the US. Illegal alcohol and illegal narcotics made up two different product lines of the same multinational firm. The British, through their distilleries in Scotland and Canada, and the British, from their opium refineries in Shanghai and [[hong-kong|Hong Kong]], were the suppliers. The British, through their banks in Canada and the Caribbean, were the financiers."[1]

Agents of [[meyer-lansky|Meyer Lansky]] and [[arnold-rothstein|Arnold Rothstein]] made their first trips to the Far East in the 1920s to buy heroin from the British.[1] Britain's leading distilling companies sold bulk quantities of liquor to Rothstein and to [[joseph-p-kennedy|Joseph Kennedy]], whose British liquor wholesaling rights were secured through the [[duke-of-devonshire|Duke of Devonshire]].[1]

The combined revenues of the illegal alcohol and drug trade during Prohibition constituted a multibillion-dollar industry, and families such as the Kennedys and the [[bronfman-family|Bronfmans]] were positioned to transition smoothly into the legalized liquor trade in the early 1930s.[1] With liquor legal again, the crime syndicates built during Prohibition turned to casino gambling, and Lansky relocated to the Caribbean to prepare the way for British offshore unregulated banking.[1]
