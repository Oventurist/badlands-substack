# Cybercab

The Cybercab is [[tesla|Tesla]]'s purpose-built robotaxi, unveiled at the company's long-awaited "We, Robot" event held in Burbank, California, on October 10, 2024.[1]

## Unveiling and market reaction

Alongside the Cybercab, the event showcased the futuristic-looking Robovan concept and the latest version of Tesla's humanoid robot, Optimus.[1] Tesla shares stumbled in premarket trading after the presentation.[1]

Wall Street analysts weighed in within hours, praising the lineup of innovative products said to be capable of revolutionizing transportation and other areas of the economy, but noting a lack of technical detail.[1] A team of [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] analysts led by Mark Delaney and Will Bryant attended the event and said Tesla "demonstrated very strong progress with the Optimus humanoid robot, and we think the Cybercab looked attractive," while cautioning that "the lack of data shared on Full Self Driving (FSD) performance, relatively limited details on the robotaxi business plan, and absence of a lower-cost consumer vehicle unveil may be disappointing for some market participants."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the reaction as part of a recurring pattern in which media dismiss [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s timelines after initial slippage. He noted that Musk's 2018 claim that the Model Y would launch in 2019 and become the world's bestselling car in 2020 was declared impossible when missed, yet the Model Y launched in 2020 and by 2023 was the world's bestselling vehicle.[1] Similarly, Musk's 2019 promise of an autonomous fleet on American roads by the end of 2020 was dismissed, but robotaxis were demonstrated live on October 10, 2024, with production slated for 2025.[1]

He added that Musk and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had both recently claimed that American households would soon be powered by individual power packs forming a decentralized energy grid — described as the most scalable aspect of Tesla's business plan — a claim the media likewise rejected.[1] His conclusion: lowering one's time preference reveals that "the future isn't just coming, it's already here. It's just not evenly distributed yet."[1]
