# United States v. Skrmetti

United States v. Skrmetti is the challenge to Tennessee's law banning gender-affirming medical care for minors, argued before the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] on December 4, 2024.[1]

## Oral arguments

After more than two hours of argument, the Court's conservative majority appeared likely to uphold the Tennessee ban, wading into a culture-war dispute that had grown more politically fraught since the 2024 election.[1] Chief Justice [[john-roberts]] and Justice [[brett-kavanaugh]], described as two of the Court's most important votes, repeatedly suggested that questions about gender-affirming medical treatments for minors should be resolved by legislatures rather than courts.[1] Roberts's skepticism about judicial involvement was read as a particularly bad sign for the challengers, given that he had sided with federal transgender employees in the 2020 decision holding such employees covered by the workplace discrimination protections of the Civil Rights Act.[1]

Several conservative justices focused on so-called "detransitioners" — people who regret gender-affirming treatments received earlier in life — while expressing skepticism that transgender Americans should receive heightened legal protection.[1] Justice [[samuel-alito]] concentrated in particular on whether transgender status is "immutable," a characteristic the Court has historically treated as central to identifying groups deserving greater constitutional protection.[1] Kavanaugh pressed Solicitor General [[elizabeth-prelogar]] on the implications for girls' and women's sports, asking, "If you prevail here, what would that mean for women's and girls' sports in particular?"[1]

One of the argument's most noted features was silence: Justice [[neil-gorsuch]], considered a key vote because he authored the majority opinion in Bostock v. Clayton County four years earlier protecting transgender employees from workplace discrimination, said nothing at all.[1] Gorsuch's Bostock opinion had been cheered by LGBTQ+ advocates and roundly criticized by conservatives, including colleagues on the bench.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands Media commentary highlighted Gorsuch's silence and Alito's immutability line of questioning as the two standout moments, arguing that if self-identification is fluid and changeable then it cannot be immutable, and therefore should not generate protected status.[1] In this reading, the practical result would be that transgender individuals retain the same rights as everyone else while losing what the commentary called "special rights" enabling infringement on others' protected rights, such as in girls' sports and women's award records.[1] The piece speculated that petitioners might come to regret bringing the question to the High Court, framing a potential ruling as a victory for equality and a blow to equity, and comparing its potential systemic effect to Loper Bright's transformation of Chevron deference and to [[dobbs-v-jackson-womens-health-organization|Dobbs]]'s transformation of Roe.[1]
