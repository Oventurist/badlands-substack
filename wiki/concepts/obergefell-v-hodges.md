# Obergefell v. Hodges

**Obergefell v. Hodges** is the United States Supreme Court decision establishing a nationwide right to same-sex marriage. Its continued viability became a live question in November 2025 when the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] declined to take up a case that could have produced a challenge to the precedent.[1]

## The 2025 cert denial

On Monday, November 10, 2025, the Court denied out of hand an appeal filed by former Kentucky county clerk [[kim-davis|Kim Davis]], who sought to mitigate hundreds of thousands of dollars in fines and damages arising from her refusal to issue same-sex marriage licenses.[1] By rejecting the appeal, the Court closed one of several procedural avenues through which it could have revisited Obergefell.[1]

Democratic lawmakers had consistently claimed the justices would not pass up an opportunity to overturn Obergefell, and far-left activists had identified the Davis appeal as the likely vehicle for doing so, by analogy to the overruling of Roe v. Wade.[1] The principal evidence cited was a short passage in Justice Clarence Thomas's concurring opinion in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization suggesting that same-sex marriage should also be revisited; no other justice expressed a similar view or indicated that overturning Obergefell was an aim.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor Ashe in America responded with a libertarian argument that government should have no role in marriage at all, describing marriage as "a cord of three strands" in which God, not the state, is the third.[1] In this view, civil marriage exists chiefly because couples sought favorable tax treatment, and the same-sex marriage campaign was premised on extending those tax "privileges" to gay couples — a demand for "chains" that, on this reading, should have been abolished rather than expanded.[1]
