# Israel October 7 Foreknowledge Allegations

The Israel October 7 foreknowledge allegations refer to the contention that Israeli political, military, and financial actors possessed advance knowledge of the [[october-7-attacks|October 7, 2023 Hamas assault]] — codenamed Operation Al-Aqsa Flood — and either failed to act on it or profited from it. Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] advanced the case in a December 22, 2023 article, combining reporting on ignored intelligence warnings with an academic study documenting anomalous short selling of Israeli securities in the days before the attack.[1]

## Ignored warnings

In the days preceding the massacre, which claimed more than 1,400 lives, [[egypt|Egypt]] reportedly warned that "something big" was coming, yet no precautions were taken by [[israel|Israel]] to bolster its defenses.[1] Israel is said to maintain one of the most fortified borders on the planet, but amid these warnings a massive breach was allowed to occur at the cost of hundreds of civilian lives.[1] Subsequent reporting — including accounts published by The Guardian and Haaretz — indicated that Tel Aviv had received numerous warnings and possessed intelligence, including material on Hamas training for precisely such an operation, meaning its extensive security and military apparatus could have and should have anticipated the attack.[1] DeLarme argued that this raises the evident question of why the attack was allowed to proceed regardless.[1]

The attack's success reportedly surprised even [[hamas|Hamas]] operatives themselves; according to reporting cited in the article, the attackers essentially ran out of plan after a certain point because they had not anticipated being able to advance so far toward Tel Aviv.[1]

## Anomalous trading

The core evidentiary claim rests on a study by [[robert-jackson-jr|Robert J. Jackson Jr.]] and [[joshua-mitts|Joshua Mitts]], which found a "significant spike" in short selling of listed Israeli companies immediately before October 7 and concluded that the evidence is consistent with informed traders anticipating and profiting from the Hamas attack.[1] Short interest in the [[msci-israel-etf|MSCI Israel Exchange Traded Fund]] "suddenly, and significantly, spiked" on October 2, and short selling of Israeli securities on the [[tel-aviv-stock-exchange|Tel Aviv Stock Exchange]] increased dramatically just before the attack.[1] The [[israel-securities-authority|Israel Securities Authority]] later confirmed that the matter was under investigation by all relevant parties.[1] See [[short-selling|short selling]] for the mechanics of the trades involved.

A parallel pattern was identified in April 2023, when Hamas was reportedly preparing a similar operation timed to begin on the eve of Passover, April 5. Two days earlier, shorting of the Israeli ETF peaked at levels very similar to those observed on October 2, and far higher — by an order of magnitude — than surrounding days.[1] That April strike was canceled after Israeli intelligence obtained advance notice and Tel Aviv publicly raised the national alert level, a sequence the authors said strengthens the interpretation that the October and April trading was related to the planned Hamas attacks rather than random noise.[1]

## Interpretation and context

DeLarme situated the allegations within a broader pattern he identified in Western war-making, in which propaganda precedes intervention, taxpayer-funded civilian casualties follow, media coverage eventually shifts to criticism, and financial interests finally profit from reconstruction contracts — the dynamic described at [[war-profiteering|war profiteering]].[1] He noted that calls from U.S. neoconservatives such as [[nikki-haley|Nikki Haley]] and [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]] to "flatten Gaza" and to "finish them" stoked long-standing animosity and gave Israel a pretext to pursue long-planned regional expansion.[1]

The article explicitly declined to endorse claims that Tel Aviv deliberately allowed Operation Al-Aqsa Flood in order to manufacture a pretext for the campaign in Gaza, allowing that Israeli complacency and a genuine intelligence failure of historic proportions might explain the attack's success.[1] It nonetheless argued that in light of episodes such as the [[september-11-attacks|September 11 attacks]] and the [[gulf-of-tonkin-incident|Gulf of Tonkin incident]], questioning whether ulterior motives were at play is not unreasonable.[1]
