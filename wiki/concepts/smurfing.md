# Smurfing

"Smurfing" is the term used in Badlands coverage for the practice of breaking large or illicit political contributions into many small donations attributed to other people, thereby laundering money through the small-donor fundraising apparatus.[1]

## Origins claimed

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who has discussed smurfing for years on the program Why We Vote alongside co-host Brian, argues that smurfing "was birthed by Obama."[1] She points to The New York Times' 2008 debunking of [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]]'s small-donor claims, the reversal in messaging by 2012 — when a Campaign Finance Institute analysis reported that nearly half of donors to Obama's reelection campaign in 2011 gave $200 or less, more than double the 2007 proportion — and the $375,000 [[federal-election-commission|Federal Election Commission]] fine levied against the 2008 campaign in 2013.[1] She notes that similar small-donor patterns were also credited to Mitt Romney in 2012, observing that "bipartisan fraud is easier to sell as shifting societal behavior."[1]

## Statutory exposure

A list of federal statutes potentially violated by [[actblue|ActBlue]] and [[winred|WinRed]], credited to Peter Bernegger, includes money laundering (18 U.S.C. § 1956), monetary transactions in property derived from unlawful activity (§ 1957), false statements to federal agencies (§ 1001), contributions in the name of another (52 U.S.C. § 30122), wire fraud (§ 1343), mail fraud (§ 1341), conspiracy to defraud the United States (§ 371), identity theft and document fraud (§ 1028), the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (§ 1030), the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (15 U.S.C. §§ 6801–6809), and the Bank Secrecy Act (31 U.S.C. 5311 et seq.).[1]

## Fifth Amendment invocations

Ashe in America reported that ActBlue employees — including those responsible for the platform's fraud-prevention operation, whom the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] had sought to interview — invoked the Fifth Amendment to avoid self-incrimination 146 times.[1] She argued that if the campaign finance apparatus was set up to launder money "in the name of democracy," counsel were wise to advise silence.[1]

## Ossoff allegations and the 2025 Democratic donor drought

In February 2025 Badlands writers applied the smurfing frame to reporting that Democratic donors had stopped giving after the party's 2024 defeat, arguing that a large chunk of the donations were never real and that the funding shut off because [[doge|DOGE]] was cleaning house.[2] Investigator [[peter-bernegger|Peter Bernegger]] posted on X that Senator Jon Ossoff had been "caught Smurfing," with records showing 20 people making 7,380 contributions totalling $197,223 through [[actblue|ActBlue]] alone.[2] Smurfed donations appear as small individual contributions in officially required paperwork, but the donors' identities are used without their knowledge or consent, making each contribution a potential campaign finance violation, forgery, identity theft, and wire fraud.[2] Commentators also revisited a 2008 New York Times item debunking claims about Barack Obama's small donors, noting that no comparable debunk exists for the 2012 cycle, when an analysis by the Campaign Finance Institute found nearly half of Obama's 2011 reelection donors gave $200 or less — more than double the 2007 proportion.[2]

## 2025 fundraising probes

The June 2025 House and Justice Department investigations into ActBlue centered on "straw" or "dummy" contributions — the practice commonly described as smurfing.[3] President Donald Trump authorized Attorney General Pam Bondi to investigate the unlawful use of online fundraising platforms for such contributions.[3] Badlands commentary argued that the practice is bipartisan, citing investigator Peter Bernegger's claim that Senator Ted Cruz is the top national recipient of smurfed donations and calling for equivalent scrutiny of WinRed.[3] The core concern is that illegal or foreign campaign money is washed through the identities of real individuals without their knowledge or consent, so that on paper the donations appear to come from small independent donors.[3]

## Reid Hoffman context (November 2025)

After President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] called on November 18, 2025 for an investigation of Democratic mega-donor [[reid-hoffman|Reid Hoffman]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] revisited the smurfing thesis to place the story in context, noting that she and [[canncon|CannCon]] had discussed smurfing for more than a year on [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]] and had laid out the basics in an April 2025 Brief after the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]] reported that [[actblue|ActBlue]] had intentionally relaxed fraud-prevention measures during the 2024 election.[4] She reiterated that smurfing was piloted by the Obama Victory Fund during [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]]'s first presidential campaign, that The New York Times debunked the small-donor claims in a scandal producing the largest campaign finance fine in history, and that by 2012 a new Campaign Finance Institute study was carried uncritically while the 2008 scandal was ignored.[4] ActBlue, launched in 2004 to harness grassroots small donors, instead "harnessed it through a meeting of the minds to engage in a highly coordinated portfolio of crime," in her framing.[4] She stressed that the statutory list would likely apply to each of millions of individual transactions, that state analogues exist where payments originated, and that [[winred|WinRed]] is implicated as well — leaving the central open question: "Whose money are they laundering?"[4]
