# Smurfing

Smurfing, in the Badlands corpus, denotes the practice of breaking large or illicit political contributions into many small donations attributed to other people's names, thereby disguising the true source of campaign funds. The term is borrowed from anti-money-laundering usage, where it describes structuring transactions to evade reporting thresholds.

## Origins claimed in the corpus

In the April 21, 2026 Badlands Brief, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] writes that in her opinion "Smurfing was birthed by [[barack-obama|Obama]]." She points to a 2008 New York Times item debunking Obama's small-donor claims, a debunk carried by major outlets under the framing of "Obama's Small Donor Myth," and to the reversal in 2011–2012, when a Campaign Finance Institute analysis reported that "Nearly half of the donors to Obama's reelection campaign in 2011 gave $200 or less, more than double the proportion seen in 2007." She notes that a comparable pattern was said to have helped Mitt Romney in 2012, observing that "Bipartisan fraud is easier to sell as shifting societal behavior," and that the 2011–2012 communications campaign promoted a new study correcting the old one while ignoring the 2008 scandal entirely. That scandal was resolved in 2013 when the [[federal-election-commission|Federal Election Commission]] fined the 2008 Obama campaign $375,000 for reporting violations.

## Statutory exposure

The practice's legal core is 52 U.S.C. § 30122, the Federal Election Campaign Act prohibition on contributions in the name of another. The Brief, citing a compilation by [[peter-bernegger|Peter Bernegger]], lists this statute alongside money laundering (18 U.S.C. § 1956), monetary transactions in criminally derived property (§ 1957), false statements to federal agencies (§ 1001), wire fraud (§ 1343), mail fraud (§ 1341), conspiracy to defraud the United States (§ 371), identity and document fraud (§ 1028), the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (§ 1030), the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (15 U.S.C. §§ 6801–6809), and the Bank Secrecy Act (31 U.S.C. 5311 et seq.) as potentially violated by [[actblue|ActBlue]] and [[winred|WinRed]].

Ashe and her co-host Brian have discussed smurfing for years on [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]]. In the 2026 Brief she connects the practice to the report that ActBlue employees invoked the Fifth Amendment 146 times before the [[house-judiciary-committee|House Judiciary Committee]], concluding that if "the campaign finance apparatus was set up to, and did, launder money in the name of democracy, their lawyers were wise to advise their clients to keep their mouths shut."
