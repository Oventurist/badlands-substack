# Fischer v. United States

Fischer v. United States is a legal challenge brought by January 6 defendant [[joseph-fischer|Joseph Fischer]] contesting the federal government's use of the charge of "obstructing an official proceeding" against people who breached the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021.[1] Fischer's challenge accuses prosecutors of relying on an overly broad interpretation of the statute — a provision of the [[sarbanes-oxley-act|Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002]] concerned with financial record keeping — to launch hundreds of cases arising from the Capitol riot, including the federal case against former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[1]

## Supreme Court review and early releases

The [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] agreed to review the legitimacy of the obstruction charge, with argument scheduled for the spring of 2024 and a decision expected by the end of June.[1] In the interim, at least two January 6 defendants successfully petitioned for early release from custody pending the outcome, on the theory that their convictions might not survive the Court's ruling.[1]

## Commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], noted that the obstruction count had been applied to roughly 152 defendants, with more than 100 sentenced, and carries a maximum sentence of 20 years.[1] She characterized the sudden willingness of district judges — including [[amit-mehta|Amit Mehta]] — to release defendants as "feigned temperance" prompted only by the Supreme Court's grant of review, describing the shift as "narrative whiplash" and framing it as a white pill rather than a black pill because the judiciary was visibly retreating.[1]

## Effect on January 6 sentencings

By January 18, 2024, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]]'s decision to take the case had already put several rioters' sentencings on hold and threatened to affect hundreds more prosecutions — including that of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[2] If the challenge succeeds, the Court could wipe away two of the four counts special counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] brought against the former president in his federal election interference case, and upend felony convictions for dozens of January 6 defendants.[2]

Since the Court agreed to hear Fischer v. US, more than a dozen January 6 defendants asked judges to halt their upcoming sentencings and trials; while some judges balked, others granted delays in a handful of cases.[2] Trump's defense team was reported to be examining the case's implications for his own prosecution.[2]

## Supreme Court grant of certiorari, December 2023

On Wednesday, December 13, 2023, the Supreme Court agreed to hear the appeal of January 6 defendant Joseph Fischer, taking up the question of whether he could be charged under the obstruction provision of the 2002 Corporate Fraud Accountability Act — a statute enacted in the wake of the Enron collapse.[3] The provision makes it a crime for anyone who "corruptly" either "alters, destroys, mutilates, or conceals a record, document, or other object ... with the intent to impair the object's integrity or availability for use in an official proceeding" or "otherwise obstructs, influences, or impedes any official proceeding."[3]

The interpretive fight centers on the word "otherwise." Nearly every judge overseeing riot-related cases in the D.C. federal district court had agreed with prosecutors that defendants who sought to stop Congress from certifying Joe Biden's 2020 victory were "otherwise" obstructing an official proceeding, while Judge Carl J. Nichols ruled that the clause reached only other forms of document tampering.[3] The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit rejected Nichols's narrower reading in a split decision, sending the question to the Supreme Court.[3]

The statute had been invoked against roughly 327 Capitol riot defendants and against Donald Trump himself.[3] Badlands commentary argued that if the Court found the law misapplied, the result would be "a tidal wave of convictions being overturned and indictments being thrown out," including two of the four counts in Jack Smith's indictments, and suggested the case might vindicate Trump's judicial appointments and explain sustained left-wing attacks on conservative justices such as Clarence Thomas.[3] Commentators also drew a pointed comparison to Representative Jamaal Bowman's pulling of a Capitol fire alarm to delay a vote, asking why that conduct would not fall under the same provision.[3]
