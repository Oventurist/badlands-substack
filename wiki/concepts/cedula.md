# Cédula

The **cédula de identidad** is Costa Rica's national citizen identity card, and in Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]]'s account it is one of the most consequential election-integrity safeguards ever devised — a model he believes the United States is about to adopt in some form.[1]

## What it is

Citing QCostaRica.com, the article describes the modern cédula as a credit-card-sized plastic card carrying a photograph of the bearer, a personal identification number, and the owner's personal information including full name and birth date. It is issued to every Costa Rican over the age of twelve.[1] Critically, **foreigners with legal residency are not issued a cédula**; legal residents receive a "Dimex" residency card instead, and all others must use a passport, which along with the cédula and dimex is one of the few acceptable forms of identification in the country.[1]

## Effect on election fraud

The same source reports that there has not been a single identifiable case of voter fraud in post-1948 Costa Rican elections — 1948 being the year an election was deemed fraudulent and annulled by Congress, precipitating the [[costa-rican-civil-war|Costa Rican Civil War]].[1] The article attributes this record to one word: "cédula."[1] Because the cédula is the only identification used for voting and is issued exclusively to citizens, it structurally prevents non-citizens from voting in any election.[1]

## Origin

Lange credits the safeguard to [[jose-figueres-ferrer|José "Pepe" Figueres Ferrer]], who created it as part of the constitutional settlement that followed the 1948 conflict, alongside the abolition of the armed forces, non-consecutive term limits, and the nationalization of the banks.[1] In his telling, the cédula prevented election fraud, prevented a dictator, and safeguarded the people's power to elect their own representatives.[1]

## Contrast with the American system

Lange contrasts the cédula with the United States, where a driver's license serves as identification for voter registration but does not prove citizenship.[1] He argues the American system has been "purposely corrupted" by motor voter laws and by states issuing driver's licenses to illegal immigrants and to foreigners present legally.[1] The cédula eliminates that ambiguity because it is both the sole voting credential and a proof of citizenship.[1]

## Proposed American analogue

The article ties the cédula to a Q drop describing "US-owned" [[voter-id-laws|voter ID laws]], reading the parenthetical "(s)" as a signal that a single federal law is intended.[1] Lange predicts a federal law or constitutional amendment forcing all states to use the same citizenship-proving voter ID; he argues that although elections are "run" by the states with divergent standards, states are required to follow federal law in federal elections, and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] designated elections as critical infrastructure for a reason.[1] He believes exposure of election fraud will open the door to that amendment, safeguarding future elections and returning power to the people.[1]
