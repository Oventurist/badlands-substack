# Soviet-Afghan War

The Soviet-Afghan War was the conflict that began when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan in December 1979 and, according to the conventional account, immediately assumed military and political control of Kabul and large portions of the country.[1] The invasion was met with resistance from an amalgam of Afghan rebel groups that had formed during the chaos of the earlier communist-friendly regime of Nur Muhammad Taraki.[1]

Occurring at the height of the Cold War, the invasion was seen by hawks in Washington as an opportunity to bleed Moscow through a proxy force, and the resulting American covert program became [[operation-cyclone|Operation Cyclone]].[1] The insurgent coalition opposing the Soviets was known collectively as the [[mujahideen]], a plural of *mujahid* or "doers of jihād" that was never the official name of any single group but functioned as a Western media catch-all for the various Afghan guerrilla factions.[1]

## Revisionist chronology

Badlands Media's account disputes the framing of the invasion as unprovoked. Citing [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]]'s 1998 interview with *Le Nouvel Observateur*, it holds that President [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]] signed the first directive for secret aid to opponents of the pro-Soviet regime in Kabul on July 3, 1979 — nearly six months before Soviet troops entered the country.[1] [[robert-gates|Robert Gates]], deputy CIA Director during the war, likewise suggests in his memoirs that U.S. intelligence was channeling aid to the Islamic brigades before the Soviets arrived.[1]

## Course and outcome

American funding of the insurgency was supplemented by Britain's MI6, [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]], and even China, whose relationship with the Soviet Union had deteriorated.[1] As Brzezinski had predicted, Afghanistan became a "Vietnamese quagmire" that greatly strained the Soviet economy.[1] Unable to quell the Western-backed insurgency, Soviet troops began withdrawing in the summer of 1987, with the last forces exiting in early 1989.[1]

Side effects attributed to the war and its covert accompaniment include the transformation of [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] into the world's largest opium supplier, the corruption of the Afghan school curriculum with militant teaching materials, and the emergence of the fundamentalist factions that later formed the [[taliban]].[1]

## The Valediction account

[[paul-fitzgerald|Paul Fitzgerald]] and [[elizabeth-gould|Elizabeth Gould]], the only two American journalists permitted into Afghanistan in 1981 and 1983, produced documentaries that went far to shatter the carefully constructed narrative of a "Russian Vietnam" built up for years by a western deep state.[2] Their book [[valediction-three-nights-of-desmond|Valediction: Three Nights of Desmond]] argues that the trap was set deliberately: the February 1979 assassination of Ambassador [[adolph-dubs|Adolph Dubs]] removed the diplomat negotiating a settlement that would have kept the Soviets out of Afghanistan, and supplied the propaganda needed to fuel anti-Russian hysteria.[2]

Soviet forces killed President [[hafizullah-amin|Hafizullah Amin]] on December 27, 1979, the day Russia's military entry officially began — a paradox the authors resolve by identifying Amin as a CIA asset rather than a genuine Soviet client.[2] The war that followed provided the living proof [[team-b|Team B]]'s fictitious thesis needed, appearing to demonstrate that Russia truly desired to dominate the world.[2] The Soviet Union was indeed bled, leading to its ultimate dissolution under Gorbachev, and the world was given Islamic terrorism funded, armed and trained by CIA, MI6 and ISI operatives.[2]

## The defensive-invasion thesis

"Who Is The Real Enemy?" rejects the conventional account of the December 1979 Soviet intervention as an act of communist expansion.[3] Citing Jonathan Steele's *Ghosts of Afghanistan*, which drew on declassified Soviet documents, it holds that the Russians invaded not as part of a plan for the world victory of communism but to protect the security of their southern frontier against Islamic unrest and American intrigue — phrases the article reads as terrorism and the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] respectively.[3] Senior Soviet generals had warned against entanglement in an Afghan civil war, citing the precedent of Vietnam, but the leadership proceeded because it could not think of a better alternative, hoping to change the Afghan leader, train his army and police, and withdraw within months.[3]

A communist government already ruled [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] from 1978, which the article treats as fatal to the expansion narrative.[3] The trigger, in this reading, was [[hafizullah-amin|Hafizullah Amin]]'s murder of [[nur-mohammad-taraki|Nur Mohammad Taraki]] in October 1979 and his secret contacts with the American chargé d'affaires, reported to [[leonid-brezhnev|Leonid Brezhnev]] by KGB chief [[yuri-andropov|Yuri Andropov]] together with warnings about American missiles being deployed in Afghanistan.[3] Soviet special forces entered Kabul to kill Amin as the invasion began.[3]

The war is described as a pivotal point in world history that marked the end of the [[cold-war|Cold War]] narrative and the beginning of the never-ending [[war-on-terror|war on terror]].[3] It also inaugurated the CIA's Afghan heroin operation: the agency funded [[gulbuddin-hekmatyar|Gulbuddin Hekmatyar]] through the Pakistani [[inter-services-intelligence|ISI]] and [[bank-of-credit-and-commerce-international|BCCI]] more heavily than any other commander, and Afghan opium output rose from 200 metric tons in 1980 to 1,980 metric tons in 1991, when both superpowers agreed to end their aid.[3] The article argues the decade-long war drained Soviet resources and bankrupted the state, and that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 2019 remarks to this effect — widely mocked in the press — were substantially correct.[3] When the Soviets withdrew, a bloody civil war erupted over control of the heroin trade.[3]
