# Bedouin

The Bedouin — *badawī* in Arabic, meaning "desert dweller" — are the nomadic tribes of the Arabian peninsula, the Levant, Africa, and modern Syria and Iraq. The Badlands Media essay arabian nights devotes a substantial section to them, treating them as the cultural and military substrate of Arabian history.

## Origins and demography

The source places their origin in the Syrian and Arabian deserts, with dispersal across the Middle East and Africa during the Golden Age of Islam. They are contrasted with the *ḥāḍir*, the sedentary population, though the article notes it is universally accepted that all *ḥāḍir* were originally Bedouin, as it suggests all human societies once were. It gives a global Bedouin population of roughly 25 million, with one of the largest concentrations — about 2 million — in [[saudi-arabia]], whose total population it puts at over 32 million. It observes growing modern political pressure to marginalise those who live "off the grid."

## Tribal structure

For both settled and nomadic Arabians, the source describes diplomacy, politics and personal identity as operating through a tribal system built on bloodlines. The nuclear family is the base unit; families form a clan, clans a tribe, and tribes a grouping the article calls a "Skull," with further distinctions within each level.

## Oral history

The essay attributes a mystical or esoteric character to the Bedouin. Where sedentary civilisations rise and fall, the Bedouin are described as a constant since the dawn of man, maintaining an oral history that is both collaborative and decentralised: tribes that meet exchange stories, and when a tribe fades the others carry its history forward. The source argues that, unlike a centralised written record, such a history is nearly impossible for any authority or government to alter. As an illustration it cites Bedouin accounts of ancient Babylon — including the origins of blood magic and the gods worshipped there — which it says were incorporated into the Quran without corroboration in the Bible or any older text and were only confirmed by twentieth-century archaeology.

## Character and military role

The author reports finding substantial commonality between the Bedouin and what he calls the American spirit: fierce independence, self-reliance, a militant yet God-fearing disposition, and a practical recognition of the need for diplomacy and trade, with strength drawn from esoteric knowledge of the desert and how to traverse it safely.

High-born Saudi boys from the settled population have traditionally been sent on coming of age to live with an allied Bedouin tribe to learn desert survival and Bedouin toughness — a practice the source compares to American boys joining the Boy Scouts. The most elite units of the Saudi military recruit directly from Bedouin tribes and are organised by tribal affiliation, both to instil camaraderie and to prevent tribal feuds from degrading military effectiveness.

On dress, the source argues that the garments Westerners associate with the perceived misogyny of Islam were worn by desert dwellers for thousands of years before Islam, as protection against sun and wind-blown sand.

The Bedouin appear throughout the essay's narrative: [[qusayy-ibn-kilab]] united nomadic clans to retake the [[kaaba]], forming the [[quraysh]]; [[hashim-ibn-abd-manaf|Hashim]] organised them into bi-annual merchant caravans; and [[mohammed-bin-saud]] won their loyalty through his own competence as a desert traveller.
