# Parallel Duties and Perpendicular Freedoms

"Parallel duties and perpendicular freedoms" is a conceptual framework introduced by [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] in the February 24, 2023 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay "The Steering Wheel of Civilization," the first installment in a planned series on the [[nature-of-civilizations|Nature of Civilizations]].[1] The framework describes what the essay calls the "physics of civilization" — the relationship between the obligations citizens accept and the liberties those obligations generate.[1]

## Definition

A duty, in this framing, is something that must be done to achieve a particular result; the example given is a baseball team whose members agree to attend practices, maintain fitness and arrive at games on time, thereby producing the game they each enjoy playing.[1] When multiple parties orient their labor toward a shared anchor point of values, they are described as working "in parallel," embracing a parallel duty to cooperate for survival and thriving.[1]

Perpendicular freedoms are the freedoms accrued by a civilization as a result of those parallel duties — freedoms that may or may not benefit society, but which would not be available at all unless citizens participated by performing a parallel duty.[1] The essay states the underlying principle bluntly: freedom to enjoy the rewards of civilization cannot exist without the duty to do one's fair share of labor.[1]

## Illustrations

The essay's household illustration holds that the more each member of a family performs their collective duties, the more freedom each enjoys as an individual: a kitchen kept clean after each use leaves the next person free to use it, while neglected duties fill the sink with dirty dishes and deprive everyone of that freedom.[1]

The economic illustration pairs a farmer and a blacksmith. The farmer needs a plow, rope and scythe but lacks time to craft them; the blacksmith needs food.[1] Recognizing mutual need, they form an agreement in which each is contracted to perform a duty and in return receive a benefit — the goals differ but the underlying survival value is shared.[1] The resulting benefits are unique to each and translate into increased freedom, which each may spend as he sees fit so long as he does not violate the other's rights.[1]

A third illustration describes a bus driver whose forty hours a week of duty secure housing, food, water and clothing, and who in return has the freedom to learn piano, cook, watch anime or go on dates — none of which would be possible without his survival needs being met first.[1] The essay similarly notes that in modern societies the dedication to duty of the farming industry frees most of the population to focus on other tasks.[1]

## Scale and exploitation

Scale is treated as decisive. In small groupings — the example given is a crew of about 100 aboard a sailing ship carrying trade goods from India to Europe — the duties and freedoms are easy to identify, and if the cook stops cooking the crew immediately notices.[1] The larger the grouping, the easier it becomes to hide a failure to do one's fair share, which the essay argues is precisely why the [[parasitic-elite|parasitic elite]] prefer large stratified populations.[1]

In modern large-scale civilizations the ratio between parallel duty and perpendicular freedom is described as alarmingly out of balance, with elites receiving far more of the freedoms than anyone else and using that surplus time to devise ever more sophisticated methods of defrauding the people of their labor while convincing them it is justified.[1] The [[shadow-men|Shadow Men]] are characterized as figures who used their disproportionate perpendicular freedoms to become expert manipulators of other minds.[1] The essay also notes that as a civilization advances, a monetary system is invented to measure each member's contribution, and that this system can later be hijacked by the parasitic elite for unfair advantage.[1]
