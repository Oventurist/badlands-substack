# Gain-of-Function Research

Gain-of-function research refers to laboratory experiments that enhance the transmissibility or virulence of pathogens. Since the COVID-19 pandemic it has been the focus of sustained congressional scrutiny in the United States, particularly over the role of the [[national-institutes-of-health|National Institutes of Health]] in funding and approving such work.[1]

## Republican reorganization proposal (June 2024)

In June 2024, congressional Republicans called for a reorganization of the NIH that would strip the agency of its authority over gain-of-function research and freeze such experiments until new reforms were established.[1] The draft plan originated with the [[house-energy-and-commerce-committee|House Energy and Commerce Committee]] and would have created a new oversight process for risky pathogen research proposals, transferring the power to review, approve, reject and oversee those experiments from NIH to a "public, independent oversight entity."[1]

Axios reported that the proposal fed a narrative dating from the pandemic portraying federal health agencies as having lost public trust, and that it could serve as a blueprint for a future Republican administration and Congress.[1] The plan also increased immediate pressure on NIH ahead of a difficult fiscal 2025 budget cycle, following the halt of a long stretch of funding increases earlier that year.[1]

## H5N1 as an alleged gain-of-function creation (2025)

In March 2025 Legal Insurrection revisited the question of whether the [[h5n1-avian-influenza|H5N1]] strain of bird flu was a gain-of-function creation, an argument first raised there in 2022 during the early days of the bird flu outbreak.[2] Two reasons were given: the strain's high kill ratio, since a fatality rate approaching 100 percent would likely only be achieved if the virus's genetics were rearranged to target a host species, and reports that Chinese researchers had been experimenting with H5N1.[2] The author concluded that while the gain-of-function judgment may have been correct, the attribution to a Chinese laboratory was wrong — the virus being, in this account, possibly a joint American and Dutch creation.[2]
