# 2024 Vice Presidential Debate

The 2024 vice presidential debate was held on Tuesday, October 1, 2024, between Senator [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] (R-Ohio) and Minnesota Governor [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]] (D).[1] Vance received broadly favorable reviews, with pundits describing him as "sharp" and delivering a "commanding performance."[1]

## Scheduling and negotiation

In mid-August 2024 both vice presidential candidates agreed to CBS's proposal for an October 1 face-off in New York City, after the network invited them on Wednesday and offered four possible dates: September 17, September 24, October 1 and October 8.[3] [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]], who remained the presumptive Democratic vice presidential nominee until the Democratic National Convention later that month, accepted in a post on X reading: "See you on October 1, JD."[3]

[[jd-vance|JD Vance]], who had initially told Fox News he would most likely agree but that his team needed time to review the rules and conditions, accepted the CBS date and upped the ante by also accepting a debate proposed by CNN for September 18.[3] "The American people deserve as many debates as possible, which is why President Trump has challenged Kamala to three of them already," Vance wrote on X. "Not only do I accept the CBS debate on October 1st, I accept the CNN debate on September 18th as well. I look forward to seeing you at both!"[3] At the time of reporting it was unclear whether Walz had agreed to the September 18 CNN debate.[3]

## Reaction

New York Times columnist Ross Douthat wrote: "For Vance, it was a commanding performance. For Walz, it was a nervous ramble. For the audience, it was the most civil and substantive debate of the Trump era."[1] Alyssa Farah Griffin, co-host of "The View" and a CNN commentator, said she was struck by the fact that "JD Vance is a significantly more eloquent [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]," adding that although she disagreed with him on much, "he speaks to MAGA in a way that he comes off as an incredibly effective communicator."[1]

Democrats also acknowledged the performance. Michigan Governor [[gretchen-whitmer|Gretchen Whitmer]] told Jimmy Kimmel that "JD Vance was a better debater than Donald Trump," and that "Donald Trump should have taken some lessons from him."[1]

## Trump's response

Trump praised Vance in a Fox News Digital interview the following Wednesday morning, saying "JD was fantastic last night — it just reconfirmed my choice," and that "there was a brilliance to what he did."[1] He said Walz "embarrassed himself and the Democrat Party last night but was made to look even worse by JD's brilliant performance," while still placing the governor "a large number of steps above" Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]].[1] Trump had already posted on Truth Social that his running mate "crushed it."[1]

## Badlands special coverage

Badlands Media ran a special live stream of the debate on Rumble beginning at 8:30 p.m. ET, in which [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]], [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] and [[zak-paine|Zak Paine]] discussed the event in detail.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] observed that Walz used the word "fundamental" four times in the debate's first 70 seconds, but allowed that it was not a bad word for summing up the theme on display.[2] In his reading, Walz was on the attack, going after [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] by using Vance as a proxy, but attacks framed in the realm of the potential did not hold up well in the public mind because voters had already witnessed an actual Trump presidency.[2] He described the moderation as giving the event the feeling of a "canned hunt," and characterized Vance as a common-sense figure cutting to the core of problems to trace them to logical solutions.[2] The commentary added that this format was not suited to people accustomed to discussing real issues such as election fraud, and concluded that the American people tend to blame whoever is "in charge" while things go wrong.[2]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] noted surprise at the degree of capitulation to the Vance-won consensus within what the outlet calls the Media Industrial Complex, and at how strongly Trump reiterated support for his running mate ahead of the November 5 inflection point.[1] The commentary urged a "Bicameral" approach to such narrative patterns: readers need not trust Vance simply because he is being thrust into the spotlight, particularly given his support from the "Con Inc. doom brigade," yet his presence in the race appeared to send traditional media into a tailspin.[1] Such narrative chaos, the piece argued, tends to benefit the "Infinite Player" on the game board — usually Trump.[1]

## Free speech exchange between Vance and Walz (DeLarme account)

Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] highlighted the debate as an instance of the Democratic Party's "ever-imminent 'threat to democracy'" framing being deployed to justify political censorship, in this case by [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]].[4] [[jd-vance|J.D. Vance]] rebutted the claim, after which Walz immediately reached for the "yelling fire in a crowded theatre" trope.[4] DeLarme responded that the trope has nothing to do with political expression, that the act itself is not necessarily illegal, and that the decision it comes from — [[schenck-v-united-states|Schenck v. United States]] — is considered bad law, having originated in the prosecution of leafleters opposing the World War One draft under Woodrow Wilson.[4]
