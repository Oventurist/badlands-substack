# Espionage Act

The Espionage Act is the United States statute governing the unauthorized retention and transmission of national defense information. It has served as the principal legal instrument in modern prosecutions involving the mishandling of classified and national-defense records.[1]

## Use in the Trump classified records case

On July 27, 2023, the [[justice-department|Justice Department]] filed a superseding indictment in the [[trump-classified-documents-case|classified records case]] against former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] that added a further Espionage Act charge, bringing the total number of counts against him to 40.[1] The added count was based on military documents Trump had boasted of possessing during a recorded meeting, in which he acknowledged that he could not share them because he had failed to declassify them.[1] The same filing accused Trump of acting with Mar-a-Lago property manager [[carlos-de-oliveira|Carlos de Oliveira]] and aide [[walt-nauta|Walt Nauta]] in an attempt to delete surveillance footage at the property.[1]

## Commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] noted the symbolism of "40 counts for the 45th President" and argued that the accumulation of charges was being read within the [[truth-community|Truth Community]] as narrative material rather than as legal jeopardy — part of what he described as the largest disclosure campaign in American history.[1]

## Use against Julian Assange

As of August 2023, 17 of the 18 U.S. criminal charges pending against WikiLeaks founder [[julian-assange|Julian Assange]] alleged violations of the Espionage Act, arising from his publication of classified American government materials in 2010.[2] Secretary of State [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]] defended the prosecution as involving "very serious criminal conduct," while U.S. Ambassador [[caroline-kennedy|Caroline Kennedy]] suggested a plea deal could resolve the case.[2]

## Use against Donald Trump (2023)

Following [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s June 2023 arraignment in the [[trump-classified-documents-case|classified documents case]], Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] described the Espionage Act as a law more than a century old, created at the outset of the First World War to prohibit acquiring or disseminating information relating to national defense with intent or reason to believe the information might injure the United States, but primarily used to squash dissent in wartime scenarios.[3] He noted its revival as an Obama-era tactic against figures such as [[julian-assange|Julian Assange]] and [[chelsea-manning|Chelsea Manning]].[3]

DeLarme argued that while the statute was being deployed against Trump, what he characterized as far more egregious violations by [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] went ignored and underreported.[3] In his account the decisive distinction is that a former president enjoys the protection of the [[presidential-records-act|Presidential Records Act]], which supersedes the Espionage Act and renders irrelevant whether the material removed was "a single postcard or a million documents."[3]
