# United States Constitution

The United States Constitution is the founding charter of the American federal government, ratified in 1789.[1] In the September 2, 2024 Badlands News Brief, the document became the subject of an extended commentary responding to a New York Times books essay titled "The Constitution is Sacred. Is It Also Dangerous?", which argued that the Constitution is increasingly dysfunctional and antidemocratic.[1]

## The New York Times critique

The essay excerpted in the brief held that the Constitution "is in trouble," pointing to [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s post-2020 call for the "termination of all rules, regulations and articles, even those found in the Constitution" as evidence of authoritarian inclination.[1] It simultaneously advanced a second and different argument: that Trump *owes* his political rise to the Constitution, because he won the presidency in 2016 while losing the popular vote but carrying the Electoral College under Article II.[1] The essay further noted that Trump appointed three Supreme Court justices under Article III, two of whom were confirmed by senators representing just 44 percent of the population under Article I, and that those justices helped overturn Roe v. Wade against majority public opinion.[1] Legal scholar Erwin Chemerinsky was quoted from his book "No Democracy Lasts Forever" arguing that American democratic failures "stem from the Constitution itself."[1]

## Badlands commentary

The Badlands writer GhostofBasedPatrickHenry dismissed the essay as "more jabbering from more baboons" and framed it as media groundwork for discarding the Constitution altogether.[1] The response drew on a "Federalism/Anti-Federalism" panel held at the GART gathering in Deadwood.[1]

Central to the argument was a distinction between the Constitution proper and the [[bill-of-rights|Bill of Rights]]: the commentary observed that when most people invoke "the Constitution" today they are in fact invoking the Bill of Rights, which was not part of the 1789 document but was written and ratified in 1791 by [[james-madison|James Madison]] of the Federalist Party as a concession to the Anti-Federalists.[1] The Anti-Federalists had complained that the original Constitution focused almost exclusively on the powers of government rather than the rights of the people.[1]

The commentary argued that the Constitution had already been subverted many years ago and is "somewhat of a failed concept," crediting [[patrick-henry|Patrick Henry]] with correctly warning that [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]]'s [[federalist-papers|Federalist Paper]] No. 67 on the Executive Department would produce a new form of tyranny through an unlimited bureaucracy each holding a fragment of executive power.[1] It closed by calling for a resurrection of the Federalist/Anti-Federalist debate.[1]
