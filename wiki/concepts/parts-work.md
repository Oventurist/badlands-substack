# Parts Work

**Parts work** — also referred to as *ego states* or *subpersonalities* — is the psychological framework invoked throughout "The Remembering: Fractured Selves and the Path Beyond Ego," an April 19, 2025 feature published by [[observing-consciousness|Observing Consciousness]] through [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]].[1] The essay presents the framework as the clinical vocabulary for an experience it insists needs no label: that a person is "not broken" but "layered," and that fragmentation was not a failure but a survival strategy.[1]

## The many selves

The essay's central assertion is that "there is no single self. There never was."[1] A person is instead described as "a mosaic of selves — a complex, adaptive system of identities formed in moments of need," each arising to protect what the author calls the flame of one's being when the world tried to extinguish it.[1] Illustrative parts named in the article include the child who stayed quiet to keep the peace, the teenager who rebelled to reclaim control, the achiever who overperformed to earn love, and the joker who laughed to hide an ache.[1]

These roles are described as having formed before language, logic, or mature judgment, in what the article calls "the raw, impressionable terrain of early experience — of fear, confusion, longing, and love."[1] The pleaser is traced to a child who learned love was conditional; the perfectionist to a child who believed mistakes would cost connection; the loner to one who was ignored.[1] The essay characterises these adaptations not as flaws but as "creative genius born of necessity" and "intuitive brilliance."[1]

Not all parts are said to originate in injury. The article also counts the imaginative dreamer, the storyteller who turned pain into poetry, and the friend who sensed another's sadness before it was spoken, arguing that these joy-born selves "hold your original rhythm."[1]

## Relation to ego transcendence

"The Remembering" is framed as a sequel to the earlier feature "Transcending Ego," and it argues that transcending the ego does not end internal complexity: "the ego was the shell. What it protected remains."[1] The turn described is therefore inward rather than upward — a "deeper descent beneath transcendence" in which wholeness comes not from rising above the parts but from weaving what was splintered into "a living, breathing mosaic of being."[1] The essay explicitly rejects the idea that spiritual purity requires exiling any part of the self.[1] See [[ego-transcendence|ego transcendence]].

## Frozen parts and liberation

Parts are said sometimes to pay a cost, becoming "frozen, locked in roles they never meant to carry forever" — the inner child who never got to play, the adolescent who still feels defensive, the high achiever who secretly longs to rest.[1] Liberation is described as giving back freedom, expression, and peace: taking the hand of the one who panics in crowds, and answering the inner critic with "Thank you for trying to keep me from harm, but I choose kindness now."[1]

Such parts are said to speak through emotion, memory, and behaviour, surfacing in relationships, stress, and moments of decision as resistance, irrational panic, or disproportionate reaction — beneath which, the essay claims, lies "a messenger."[1] The article concludes that these parts "are not blocks to enlightenment. They are bridges to wholeness."[1]

## Clinical authorities cited

The article grounds its case in several trauma clinicians. [[bonnie-badenoch|Bonnie Badenoch]] is quoted on integration as a state in which every part communicates with every other and no part need be exiled for a person to feel safe, loved, or whole.[1] [[bessel-van-der-kolk|Bessel van der Kolk]] is quoted from *The Body Keeps the Score* on trauma as a fundamental reorganisation of how mind and body manage perception.[1] [[deb-dana|Deb Dana]] is quoted on welcome and safety as the precondition of connection, and [[marion-woodman|Marion Woodman]] and [[gabor-mate|Gabor Maté]] are cited on pain, truth, and safety as connection.[1]
