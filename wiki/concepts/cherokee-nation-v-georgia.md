# Cherokee Nation v. Georgia

*The Cherokee Nation v. The State of Georgia*, 30 U.S. 1; 8 L.Ed. 25 (1831), is a United States Supreme Court decision cited in Badlands Media commentary for a single, narrowly linguistic reason: the opinion's treatment of the words "state" and "nation" as interchangeable terms of art.[1]

## The cited language

The Badlands Media analysis quotes the Court as holding that "the terms 'state' and 'nation' are used in the law of nations, as well as in common parlance, as importing the same thing."[1] The author, writing under the pen name [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]], treats that sentence as an authoritative judicial definition rather than as dicta, and uses it as the hinge of a larger argument about the constitutional character of the American union.[1]

## Argument built on the case

If "state" and "nation" import the same thing in the law of nations, the article reasons, then the familiar compound "nation-state" is a redundancy — analogous to calling a pet cat a "feline-cat."[1] More consequentially, it follows on the author's reading that each of the several American states is itself a nation, and that the federal entity assembled from them is therefore not a nation but a union of nations.[1] The article pairs the case with [[chisholm-v-georgia|Chisholm v. Georgia]], [[people-ex-rel-attorney-general-v-naglee|People ex rel. Attorney General v. Naglee]], [[robinson-v-norato|Robinson v. Norato]], and definitions drawn from [[blacks-law-dictionary|Black's Law Dictionary]] to build a cumulative case that American legal terminology has been deliberately obscured in popular education.[1]

## Framing

The case appears within a broader methodological claim about [[natural-and-formal-languages|natural and formal languages]]: because legal English is a heavily formalized dialect in which words are expected to carry singular meanings, the article argues that apparent synonymy in a Supreme Court opinion must be taken seriously rather than dismissed as loose usage.[1] The piece was published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on November 1, 2023, and explicitly frames the inquiry as unfinished research rather than settled conclusion.[1]
