# Trump v. Slaughter

*Trump v. Slaughter* is a recent [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] decision cited by the [[white-house|White House]] as precedent for the president's authority to remove officials from independent, bipartisan federal commissions.[1]

The case was invoked in July 2026 when a White House official defended President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s removal of the final three members of the U.S. [[election-assistance-commission|Election Assistance Commission]] on July 9, stating that the president had authority to remove officials who were not aligned with the administration's election-security priorities.[1] Because the commission was created by [[help-america-vote-act|the Help America Vote Act of 2002]] with a statutory requirement that its four presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed members be divided evenly between the two major parties, the removals raised the question of how far the removal power recognized in *Slaughter* extends to structurally bipartisan bodies.[1]

## Oral arguments, December 2025

The Supreme Court heard oral arguments in the case in early December 2025, and reporting at the time indicated the justices appeared likely to give President Trump greater control over the federal government by upholding his claim that he can fire members of independent agencies.[2] The dispute arose from Trump's dismissal of [[rebecca-kelly-slaughter|Rebecca Kelly Slaughter]] from the [[federal-trade-commission|Federal Trade Commission]].[2] Justice Brett Kavanaugh questioned the accountability of independent agencies, while Chief Justice John Roberts challenged the continuing relevance of a 1935 precedent that had historically insulated such agencies, suggesting that the modern FTC exercises significant power properly subject to presidential authority.[2] A ruling was anticipated by the end of June.[2]

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that an "independent agency" within the Executive Branch is a contradiction in terms and would be extraconstitutional, asserting that the President must hold plenary authority over the Executive Branch and the ability to remove any officer at will.[2]
