# US–Iran Memorandum of Understanding

The US–Iran memorandum of understanding is the 14-point framework agreement signed in June 2026 between the Trump administration and the Iranian government, concluding the armed confrontation between the two states.[1]

## Terms

The memorandum was signed ahead of schedule and codified an immediate and permanent ceasefire, phased sanctions relief, a $300 billion rehabilitation framework, the urgent reopening of the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], and a 60-day window for final nuclear negotiations — a window Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized as itself performative.[1] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said that Iran could retain some ballistic missile capabilities under the arrangement.[1]

## Implementation despite disrupted signing

Formal signing ceremonies scheduled in Switzerland were postponed after [[israel|Israel]] launched overnight strikes against [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]] targets in southern Lebanon; the [[israel-defense-forces|IDF]] said it had struck Hezbollah positions while Lebanese reports cited multiple fatalities, and Switzerland confirmed that the planned talks at Bürgenstock were canceled.[1] Vice President [[jd-vance|J.D. Vance]] cancelled or delayed his travel to the talks, a delay Badlands attributed to Israel's resumption of hostilities.[1]

Despite the canceled ceremony, the United States and Iran proceeded with implementation.[1] [[centcom|CENTCOM]] announced that U.S. forces had lifted the maritime blockade on Iranian ports and coastal areas in accordance with the President's direction, while retaining naval presence in the region to verify compliance.[1] Iran reported moving more than five million barrels of oil since the electronic signing on the preceding Sunday.[1]

## Reception and commentary

Legacy outlets including [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] argued that Trump emerged weakened from the conflict and that the agreement represented a retreat from the administration's original objectives, contending that Iran had weathered the conflict and that the outcome undercut Trump's claims of leverage.[1] A related newsletter criticized Trump's comments at the G7 summit and argued the administration had shifted away from its earlier Middle East goals.[1]

Burning Bright framed this coverage as the [[media-industrial-complex|Media Industrial Complex]] in "full, flailing retreat along Overton's Goalpost," discombobulated by the very off-ramp it had spent months insisting Trump could never locate without catastrophic loss of face.[1] In this reading, media voices and their [[uniparty|Uniparty]] representatives had declared during the "controlled burn of the Iranian theater" that no diplomatic path existed short of perpetual entanglement or regime change on their terms; with the memorandum signed, they inverted the narrative to present the solution as the problem, claiming Trump had made concessions to a terrorist regime, squandered a generation of proxy-war leverage, and left Iranian nuclear infrastructure, defensive autonomy, and financial networks intact.[1] Burning Bright described this as a deliberate trap that exposed the war hawks and [[neoconservatives|NeoCons]] whose devotion was to perpetuating the forever-war construct.[1]

Vance publicly defended the deal and criticized opposition from Israeli officials, dressing down Israeli leadership from the White House podium; Badlands described this as a "heel turn" and predicted an accelerating decline in Israel's international popularity.[1] Senator [[ted-cruz|Ted Cruz]]'s objections were derided in the same commentary, which raised the prospect of Republicans torpedoing an earnest peace between Iran and Trump.[1]

## Framework terms and the Beirut strike

Reuters reported that U.S. and Iranian officials had agreed on a framework to end the war, halt the U.S. blockade of [[iran|Iran]] and reopen the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], with the memorandum of understanding scheduled to be signed on a Friday in Switzerland.[2] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] posted on the preceding Sunday that the deal with Iran was "now complete," and Pakistani Prime Minister [[shehbaz-sharif|Shehbaz Sharif]] said the pact called for the immediate and permanent termination of military operations on all fronts, including Lebanon.[2] The secretariat of Iran's [[supreme-national-security-council-iran|Supreme National Security Council]] said military operations would end permanently starting Monday night, and Iran's deputy foreign minister said a broader agreement including sanctions relief would be negotiated during a 60-day ceasefire period.[2]

The signing was delayed by several hours after [[israel|Israel]] struck [[hezbollah|Hezbollah]] targets in Beirut's southern suburbs on the day of Trump's 80th birthday; Trump told Axios that the agreement remained on track despite the strike.[2] According to [[barak-ravid|Barak Ravid]], Trump said in a phone call: "Why did Bibi have to do a fucking attack? I was so pissed off. I let him know. He has no fucking judgement."[2] Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] told Fox News that Iran had planned to retaliate but backed down at the request of the United States.[2]

## Opposition in Israel and Washington

Israeli National Security Minister [[itamar-ben-gvir|Itamar Ben Gvir]] said Israel was not a party to the deal, argued that military operations should continue until Hezbollah is dismantled, and opposed withdrawing from territory held by Israeli forces in Lebanon.[2] Opposition Leader [[yair-lapid|Yair Lapid]] called the deal "terrible for Israel," saying it erased Israeli military achievements, and former Prime Minister [[naftali-bennett|Naftali Bennett]] warned that Israel faced "an existential moment."[2] Israeli politicians and military leaders described the outcome as a "strategic failure," and Finance Minister [[bezalel-smotrich|Bezalel Smotrich]] joined the criticism; on Ynet, Israeli officials told reporters they had no input or influence in the peace process.[2]

Badlands contributor GhostofBasedPatrickHenry argued that the [[israel-lobby|Israel Lobby]] intended to use its influence over [[congress|Congress]] to block the deal from being finalized, predicting this would be the moment the GOP turned on Trump and that "MAGA infiltrators" — including commentators such as [[ben-shapiro|Ben Shapiro]] and [[mark-levin|Mark Levin]], and figures like [[laura-loomer|Laura Loomer]] and Senator [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]] — would reveal themselves.[2] He credited Trump with averting World War III by negotiating a peace with Iran that explicitly excludes Israel, and reported that violence had erupted in the West Bank from Jewish settlers in response to the deal.[2]

## Terms of the interim MOU (June 14, 2026)

Reporting on June 17, 2026 described the instrument as an interim memorandum of understanding announced by [[donald-trump|Trump]] on June 14, 2026, extending an April ceasefire by 60 days to allow negotiation of a permanent truce.[3] The MOU was virtually signed by Trump, Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] and Iranian Parliament Speaker [[mohammad-bagher-ghalibaf|Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf]], and provides for lifting the U.S. naval blockade on the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]] and Iranian ports, free transit through Hormuz for an initial 60-day period, and the launch of nuclear negotiations.[3] Trump emphasized that Iran had agreed never to acquire a nuclear weapon and described Iranian leaders as rational partners, with a formal signing scheduled for that Friday in Geneva.[3]

## Unresolved details and congressional friction

The full text of the MOU was not released, and significant questions remained over how quickly sanctions relief or asset unfreezing would occur, the handling of Iran's highly enriched uranium stockpile, the scope of future nuclear talks, and a reported $300 billion reconstruction fund potentially financed by Gulf states.[3] Iran linked any permanent truce to an end to Israel's presence in southern [[lebanon|Lebanon]], while Israeli Prime Minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] stated that [[israel|Israel]] is not bound by the agreement and would maintain its forces there.[3]

Trump indicated he would send the MOU to [[congress|Congress]] for review after the formal signing, but lawmakers of both parties complained that no classified briefings or text had been provided despite the agreement's national security implications; Senate Democratic Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] and some Republican senators called for immediate details and briefings, noting the unusual lack of transparency.[3] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] read the delay as deliberate — a coordinated strategy to give "traitors and infiltrators" in the GOP enough rope to hang themselves, with the eventual congressional nullification of the deal exposing which legislators answer to a foreign power.[3] He noted that Vance had been dispatched on a media tour during the interim and told [[megyn-kelly|Megyn Kelly]] that even he did not fully understand the delay, while [[mark-levin|Mark Levin]], [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]] and others signaled intent to scrutinize and potentially nullify the document under congressional review.[3] He added that Trump was by then posturing that the United States would not pursue extraction of Iran's "nuclear dust."[3]

## Early remote signing and the Doha channel

By June 18, 2026 the United States and Iran had reportedly finalized and digitally signed the memorandum ahead of the previously scheduled signing ceremony in Switzerland, with [[donald-trump|President Trump]] and Iranian leaders signing remotely and bringing the interim accord into effect earlier than anticipated while formal negotiations on a permanent agreement continued over a 60-day window.[4] The 14-point framework includes provisions for ending hostilities, reopening maritime traffic through the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]], sanctions relief tied to compliance, and negotiations over Iran's nuclear program; Reuters and the Associated Press separately published details of the draft, describing it as an interim agreement designed to create conditions for a broader settlement.[4]

[[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]] examined [[qatar|Qatar]]'s role in the diplomacy, noting that Doha's longstanding ties with both Washington and Tehran provided communication channels before and during the conflict, that Qatari officials had worked to broker contacts with the Trump administration before hostilities began, and that the country's unique relationships made it particularly well positioned to mediate.[4] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] said the confirmation of Qatar as the backchannel vindicated years of his own speculation, tracing the arrangement back to the 2017 "soft war" between [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] and Qatar.[4]

The chaotic rollout of the memorandum was itself read by GhostofBasedPatrickHenry as intentional: he argued Trump had baited opponents in the GOP into attacking the agreement before its contents were known, deepening the split within the [[maga-coalition|MAGA coalition]].[4]
