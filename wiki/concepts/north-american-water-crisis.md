# North American Water Crisis

The North American water crisis is described by Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as one of a set of existential crises facing the United States — partly natural and partly man-made — arising from decades of neglected water management, power generation and agro-industrial development.[1]

## Causes

Ehret attributes the strain on water systems to the conscious undermining, over several generations, of the traditions of water management and infrastructure building by what he calls degenerate Malthusian technocrats.[1] The absence of basic maintenance, and the failure to construct new hydroelectric dams over the past forty years, has produced structural rot in vital infrastructure and the accelerating destruction of fragile water systems needed by farmers, industry and residents, especially on the west coast.[1]

## Symptoms

Lake Mead in Nevada and Arizona and Lake Powell in Utah — strategically important reservoirs — have fallen to their lowest levels in history and now face "dead pool" status, meaning water will soon no longer flow downstream.[1] Aquifers including the [[ogallala-aquifer|Ogallala]] have been drained over the decades, threatening both drinking water and food production.[1] Reservoirs built during the "age of water projects" from the 1930s to the 1960s — Shasta Lake, Lake Oroville and Folsom Lake among them — are on the verge of depletion, while the dams built 50 to 90 years ago that provided hydroelectric power, flood control and water management are decaying and require billions of dollars in repairs.[1]

U.S. farming income has continued to collapse over the past eight years, with the Food and Agricultural Policy Research Institute forecasting an additional 6 percent decline in 2025.[1]

## Policy response

Rather than increasing fresh water access to the continent, Ehret argues, trans-Atlantic leadership has accelerated the destruction of farmers and citizens through forced triaging of water use and by blaming small and medium industrial farmers for climate problems, pressuring the shutdown of food production in line with the [[agenda-2030|Agenda 2030]] scheme.[1] The available remedies are, in his account, both trapped in [[zero-sum-thinking|zero-sum thinking]]; the alternative he proposes is the revival of the [[north-american-water-and-power-alliance|North American Water and Power Alliance]] on the model of China's [[south-north-water-transfer-project|Move South Water North Project]].[1]

## Geography of the resource

North America's water cycle concentrates rainfall and unused runoff in the sparsely populated, lightly industrialised north while leaving very little water in the populous, agro-industrial south.[1] More than 2,200 million acre-feet per year of rain falls on Alaska and the Yukon, and in British Columbia 1,300 million acre-feet per year of that rain runs directly into the Arctic and North Pacific salt water, never entering any biospheric process — against a continental average of 110 million acre-feet per year, and roughly 40 times the runoff experienced in northern Mexico or the southwestern United States.[1]
