# Telegraph

The electric telegraph was the nineteenth-century communications technology developed by [[samuel-morse|Samuel Morse]] and his collaborators, and in Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] it is treated as the culmination of a long Platonic scientific tradition in the study of electricity.[1]

## Scientific background

The word electricity derives from the Greek for amber, and while the effect was observed by Thales of Miletus, the first scientific study is attributed by the article to Plato in the fourth century BC in *The Timaeus*, which also explored magnetite and offered the first non-superstitious hypothesis about the invisible force.[1] The study was revived by the English Platonist William Gilbert (1544–1603), who demonstrated that the earth itself operates as a magnet; his work inspired Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), the first to recognise the solar gravitational field as magnetic in character, and Stephen Gray (1666–1736), who made an enemy of Isaac Newton by demonstrating that matter and space are shaped by invisible subtle currents of electricity.[1]

This was the tradition [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] joined during his early work in England in the 1720s, culminating in his demonstration that the discharge from thunderclouds is of the same nature as that of a Leyden jar, and in his theory of positive and negative components of an electrical "fluid."[1] Franklin's discoveries of 1750–53 polarised thinking across Europe and America and stimulated an explosion of work by Alessandro Volta, Carl Friedrich Gauss, Wilhelm Weber, André-Marie Ampère and others.[1] Volta compiled the earliest known batteries and found electrical properties even in living tissue, in the movement of animal and human limbs.[1]

## Development and consequences

Recognising the extreme speed of the electrical fluid, scientists took up the transmission of information at that speed, and the experiments of Gauss, Weber, [[joseph-henry|Joseph Henry]] and Morse led quickly to a working telegraph, applied first in the United States and then across Europe, Canada and beyond.[1] Morse — quoted in the article as saying in 1833 that "if the presence of electricity can be made visible in any part of the circuit, I see no reason why intelligence may not be transmitted instantaneously by electricity" — became known as the "Lightning Man," and the telegraph joined Robert Fulton's steamship as a uniquely American creation bending the notions of time and space.[1] Where news of the bloodshed at Lexington and Concord took three weeks to travel from Massachusetts to South Carolina in 1775, and a week to pass between Albany and New York City by sailboat, news now travelled in seconds and people and goods in hours.[1] By the Civil War, lines had been laid across the United States, Europe and the Atlantic itself.[1]
