# Humanitarian City Plan

The "humanitarian city" plan was a proposal announced in July 2025 by Israeli defense minister [[israel-katz|Israel Katz]] to construct a single controlled settlement on the ruins of Rafah, in the southern [[gaza|Gaza Strip]], into which the enclave's entire Palestinian population would be relocated.[1] Critics of the initiative immediately branded the proposed compound an internment camp and warned that it invited widespread human rights abuse.[1]

## Announced structure

According to Katz's briefing to reporters, the site was expected to accommodate roughly 600,000 Palestinians in its first phase — primarily displaced persons then living in the coastal Mawasi area to the northwest of Rafah — with the eventual intent of moving all of the estimated 2.2 million residents of Gaza into the zone.[1] The perimeter was to be secured by the Israeli military "from a distance," while day-to-day administration would be handled by unspecified international organizations.[1] Palestinians would be screened before entry to ensure that no [[hamas|Hamas]] operatives slipped in, and those admitted would not be permitted to return to other parts of Gaza.[1]

Katz acknowledged that the scheme was ultimately designed to displace the entire Gaza population and to encourage residents to "voluntarily emigrate" from the enclave altogether.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for Badlands Media, GhostofBasedPatrickHenry framed the proposal as a logical absurdity: Gaza is a strip of land measuring roughly five miles by twenty-five miles, and the defense ministry was in effect arguing that this area was too large for the Israel Defense Forces to secure, so 2.2 million people would have to be compressed into a still smaller camp nominally sized for 600,000.[1]

The commentary went further, arguing that the plan invited an unavoidable comparison between the treatment of Gazans and the historical persecution of European Jewry, and that public willingness to revisit twentieth-century historical narratives was growing as trust in official accounts eroded.[1] The piece contended that either possible resolution of that debate would be damaging — a discredited or embellished Holocaust narrative would generate resentment toward those who promulgated it, while an upheld narrative would raise the question of how the State of Israel could within a single lifetime perpetrate what the author characterized as a similar war crime against another population.[1] The author noted that American bombs and tax dollars were being used in the campaign, and objected specifically to the invocation of Christianity in its defense.[1]
