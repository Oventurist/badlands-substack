# Long Depression

The Long Depression is a term the article uses to describe the economic period following the Coinage Act of 1873, when the United States was placed on the gold standard. The source claims that bankers labeled this period the "Long Depression" because the gold standard "only hurt the banks, loaners, and the corruption within the economic system."

According to the article, the Long Depression was not actually a depression for ordinary citizens. Citing [[Murray Rothbard]]'s _History of Money and Banking of the United States_, the source argues that the period was "only a misunderstood recession," since real wages and production were increasing throughout. Rothbard is said to have attributed falling prices to the deflationary gold standard in the United States in the aftermath of the Civil War.

The article also cites [[David Ames Wells]]'s observation of technological advancements during the period from 1870 to 1890, quoting him: "The economic changes that have occurred during the last quarter of a century—or during the present generation of living men—have unquestionably been more important and more varied than during any period of the world's history." Wells is said to have explained how deflation gave more power to the people, increasing productivity.

The source claims the Long Depression "was doing more damage to the rich and powerful" and that this motivated the elite to seek something to "permanently suppress the citizens," ultimately leading to the push for an income tax and the cabal's 1913 consolidation. The article presents the Long Depression as a period of citizen flourishing under the gold standard that bankers deliberately mischaracterized to justify their later push for fiat currency.
