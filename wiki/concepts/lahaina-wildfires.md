# Lahaina Wildfires

The Lahaina wildfires of August 2023 devastated the historic town of Lahaina on the island of Maui, Hawaii. Lahaina was hit harder than any other area affected by the fires, and the destruction of homes and property there triggered an immediate and widely criticized wave of land-acquisition activity.[1]

## The fire

Three wildfires started on Maui in the week of August 7, 2023 and spread rapidly on Wednesday, August 9, fueled by strong winds from the distant Hurricane Dora.[2] Maui county authorities reported at least 36 fatalities amid the active Lahaina fire, and preliminary estimates showed that nearly 300 structures had been damaged or destroyed with more than 1,000 acres of Maui's Upcountry charred.[2] Power was knocked out for thousands across the west of the island, mass evacuations were conducted in the resort town of Lahaina, and thousands of people lined Maui's Kahului Airport on Wednesday night trying to escape the inferno.[2] Maxar Technologies released before-and-after satellite imagery showing that the fire had effectively leveled the town.[2]

## Climate change framing

The fire was attributed to winds from the distant hurricane combined with unusual weather conditions, lower than usual humidity and an ongoing drought.[2] Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that this framing was quickly folded into climate-change messaging, noting that The New York Times ran an entire tab dedicated to climate change's role in the Maui fires under the headline "How Climate Change Turned Lush Hawaii Into a Tinderbox," which argued that "as the planet heats up, no place is protected from disasters."[2] She offered prayers for the victims and displaced residents of Lahaina while remarking on the pattern of never letting a crisis go to waste.[2]

## Post-disaster property frenzy

Within days of the fire, grieving property owners reported being bombarded with calls from investors and realtors offering to buy their land at below-market value.[1] One Maui resident's video denouncing the practice — "How dare you do that to our community right now" — drew international coverage, and she stated she personally knew multiple families who had received such offers.[1]

Reporting emphasized that pressure on Lahaina landowners to sell long predated the fire. Property values had risen sharply, with the average Lahaina home worth about $600,000 at the beginning of 2020 and roughly $1 million by 2023, making the fire-cleared parcels unusually attractive to well-capitalized buyers.[1] Hawaii Governor [[josh-green|Josh Green]] publicly warned against the pressure campaign directed at fire victims.[1]

## Interpretation in Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] used the Lahaina case as the anchor for an extended analysis of [[disaster-opportunism|disaster opportunism]], distinguishing ordinary post-disaster profit-seeking from what he described as the potentially criminal question of whether such tragedies are "curated for the vultures."[1] He drew a comparison with the failure of the New Orleans levees during Hurricane Katrina, where the Army Corps of Engineers and other regulators were later found to have failed to maintain the levees, and argued that Lahaina fit a recognizable development-and-gentrification pattern: attract population, constrain housing supply, zone for lower-income labor, then clear the way for higher-income redevelopment.[1]

He further argued that the capital and time horizons required to rebuild a destroyed district create high barriers to entry that exclude local residents and favor large institutional or oligarchic investors.[1]

## Casualty toll and scale of destruction

By an overnight update from Maui county officials, the death toll from the fires that ravaged the resort town of Lahaina had jumped to 80, with firefighters still working to contain blazes in Lahaina, Pulehu/Kihei, and Upcountry Maui.[3] At least 1,700 buildings were burnt to a crisp in what was described as the deadliest natural disaster to strike Hawaii since a 1960 tsunami that killed 61 people.[3] At that stage the Maui fire was the second-deadliest in the United States in the previous hundred years, trailing only the 2018 Camp Fire in Northern California, which killed 85.[3] Hawaii Governor [[josh-green|Josh Green]] cautioned that a complete assessment would take weeks of sifting through "rubble and ashes."[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that the Associated Press reported late that Sunday that the toll had climbed to 93, making it officially the deadliest fire in U.S. history.[3] She noted that no cause had been established, though some Hawaiians claimed the fire began with downed power lines, and that once ignited it was driven by winds from a distant hurricane combined with "unusual conditions" in the region.[3] Additional fires were reported on the island, straining already overwhelmed emergency services; more than 46,000 residents had been evacuated from Maui and officials asked visitors to stay away.[3] She observed that conspiracy theories proliferated over the weekend — from HAARP and directed-energy weapons to comparisons with Little St. James — and concluded that while the truth about the cause might never be known, the victims were real.[3]
