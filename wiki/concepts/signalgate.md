# Signalgate

**Signalgate** is the name given to the controversy over U.S. Defense Secretary [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]]'s use of the [[signal|Signal]] encrypted messaging application to transmit operational updates about American military strikes in Yemen.[1] The affair became one of the defining scandals of the early second Trump administration and prompted a lengthy inquiry by the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] inspector general.[1]

## Inspector general report

The Pentagon inspector general released its long-awaited report on Hegseth's role in the affair on Thursday, December 4, 2025.[1] Investigators concluded that Hegseth's use of Signal to send updates about strikes in Yemen broke rules governing the handling of sensitive information and risked putting American troops in danger, even though the secretary possesses the authority to declassify such information as he sees fit.[1] Reporting on the release summarized six principal takeaways: that Hegseth broke the rules; that he operates with a very tight inner circle; that he offered up incomplete records to investigators; that the report's conclusions are largely in the eye of the beholder; that Democrats were not satisfied by its findings; and that Hegseth had changed his tune over the course of the episode.[1]

Coverage of the release judged that the report offered nothing that would doom Hegseth — described as President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s most embattled Cabinet chief — but that it could complicate his political future.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands host [[canncon|CannCon]] questioned whether the choice of Signal had itself been institutionally encouraged, asking what it would mean if the [[cisa|Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency]] had advised officials to use Signal during the Biden administration, roughly a month before Trump took office.[1] He pointed to [[katherine-maher|Katherine Maher]] — chair of the board of the [[signal-foundation|Signal Foundation]] and chief executive of [[npr|NPR]], with prior work for the National Democratic Institute, UNICEF, the World Bank, Access Now, the [[atlantic-council|Atlantic Council]], and the U.S. State Department Foreign Policy Affairs Board — as a reason to wonder whether the platform's promotion served other interests.[1] CannCon also noted that no screenshot had ever been published showing how the officials in the chat would have seen journalist Jeffrey Goldberg's name among the participants.[1]

## Goldberg's account of the leak (March 2025)

[[jeffrey-goldberg|Jeffrey Goldberg]], editor in chief of [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]], wrote that the world learned shortly before 2 p.m. Eastern on March 15, 2025 that the United States was bombing [[houthis|Houthi]] targets across Yemen — but that he had known two hours before the first bombs exploded, because Secretary of Defense [[pete-hegseth|Pete Hegseth]] had texted him the war plan at 11:44 a.m., including precise information about weapons packages, targets, and timing.[2] Goldberg recounted that on Tuesday, March 11 he received a connection request on [[signal|Signal]] from a user identified as [[mike-waltz|Michael Waltz]], and accepted it hoping it was the actual national security adviser wanting to discuss Ukraine or Iran.[2] He framed the Biden administration as having been ineffective through 2024 at countering Houthi attacks, with the incoming Trump administration promising a tougher response.[2] Goldberg wrote that he had "very strong doubts that this text group was real," because he could not believe the national-security leadership would communicate on Signal about imminent war plans or that the national security adviser would be reckless enough to include him in discussions with senior officials up to and including the vice president.[2]

## Badlands assessment

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] noted Goldberg's history — remembering him as the figure who spun up the "Suckers and Losers" story about Trump and the WWII cemetery in France — and treated his honesty as a major assumption, joking that he would love to learn Goldberg had been catfished by anons.[2] Assuming the texts were genuine, he argued the "leak" was a strategic win for the administration: it debunked the notion that the Houthi strike served Israel, made clear that Trump was "bailing out" Europe, and demonstrated earnest debate among the cabinet.[2] In this reading [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] emerged as the figure with his finger on the pulse of the movement, acknowledging the strike might be a mistake and that supporters might not understand why it was necessary, while Hegseth explicitly stated they wanted to act before Israel and that messaging would be difficult regardless.[2] The commentary concluded that the episode at least affirmed the administration did not expect the public to understand the decision.[2] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] added a second take, asking who wanted this to become a public narrative and cautioning against assuming the texts were real or that Goldberg was added accidentally.[2]

## European naval capability exchange

Among the messages published from the Signal chat, Vice President [[jd-vance|J.D. Vance]] questioned why the United States should get involved bombing the Houthis over a canal through which only 3% of American trade passes.[3] National Security Adviser [[mike-waltz|Mike Waltz]] answered that "whether we pull the plug or not, today European navies do not have the capability to defend against the types of sophisticated, antiship, cruise missiles, and drones the Houthis are now using."[3] Badlands contributor Ryan DeLarme cited the exchange as documentary evidence of European military weakness at the very moment EU leaders were talking about confronting Russia.[3]
