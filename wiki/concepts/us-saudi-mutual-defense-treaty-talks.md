# US-Saudi Mutual Defense Treaty Talks

The US–Saudi mutual defense treaty talks were negotiations, reported in September 2023, in which the United States discussed the terms of a mutual defense treaty with [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] modeled on American military pacts with Japan and South Korea.[1]

## Terms under discussion

Under such an agreement, the United States and Saudi Arabia would generally pledge to provide military support if the other were attacked in the region or on Saudi territory.[1] The East Asian treaties used as models are considered among the strongest U.S. commitments outside its European pacts, and the plan to emulate them had not previously been reported.[1]

## Diplomatic context

The talks sat at the center of President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s high-stakes diplomacy aimed at persuading the kingdom to normalize relations with Israel.[1] Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]], Saudi Arabia's de facto ruler, regarded a mutual defense agreement as the most important element of his talks with the Biden administration about Israel.[1] Saudi officials argued that a strong defense agreement would help deter potential assaults by Iran or its armed partners, even as the two regional rivals re-established diplomatic ties.[1]

Prince Mohammed also asked the administration to help Saudi Arabia develop a civilian nuclear program, which some U.S. officials feared could serve as cover for a nuclear weapons program to counter Iran.[1] Any treaty resembling the East Asian pacts was expected to draw strong objections in [[congress|Congress]], where some senior lawmakers, including top Democrats, view the Saudi government and Prince Mohammed as unreliable partners with little regard for U.S. interests or human rights.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the negotiations as a piece of [[devolution|Devolution]] evidence, arguing that although the Biden administration was ostensibly in control of the game board, its moves — new defense pacts in the Indo-Pacific and now with Saudi Arabia — were "oddly reminiscent" of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s foreign policy.[1] He noted that the Saudis had publicly snubbed Biden on multiple occasions while committing to the expansion of the [[brics|BRICS]] alliance, and concluded that Biden "may be the 'president,' but he is not the leader of the western world, and he's not being treated as such."[1]

## June 2024 landmark treaty offer

By June 2024 the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration was reported to be close to finalizing a treaty with [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] that would commit the United States to help defend the Gulf nation, as part of a long-shot effort to encourage diplomatic ties between Riyadh and [[israel|Israel]].[2] The success of the effort hinged on Israel's commitment to a separate Palestinian state and, more immediately, an end to the war in Gaza — an unlikely proposition amid months of fruitless cease-fire talks and an Israeli weekend raid to retrieve hostages from the heart of the territory.[2] In return for the opportunity to achieve normal relations with Saudi Arabia and greater acceptance in the Arab and Muslim worlds, Israel would have to support a credible pathway toward a [[two-state-solution|two-state solution]], something the sitting Israeli government and most of the Israeli public opposed.[2]

The push marked a remarkable turnaround for Biden, who as a candidate had vowed to treat Saudi Arabia as a pariah and make it pay a price for the assassination of journalist Jamal Khashoggi, a U.S. resident.[2] Aaron David Miller, a former U.S. peace negotiator with the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, observed that it "would be the first time the U.S. concluded a mutual defense pact that would carry the force of law since the 1960 revision of the U.S.-Japan treaty, and the first time it concluded such an agreement with an authoritarian country."[2]

## Badlands critique of the 2024 offer

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] called the development "very ominous—and foolish," drawing a direct parallel to the 1973–74 oil embargo, when King Faisal halted oil sales to the United States and its allies over their support for Israel in the [[yom-kippur-war|Yom Kippur War]].[2] That crisis, he wrote, was resolved by Secretary of State [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]], who promised Faisal American protection from Israeli aggression on the condition that Saudi Arabia trade its oil exclusively in U.S. dollars — creating the [[petrodollar|petrodollar]].[2]

The commentary argued that Saudi Arabia no longer needs American protection, citing its alignment with Russia and China and the five years Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] and Foreign Minister [[faisal-bin-farhan|Faisal bin Farhan]] have spent building bilateral relationships worldwide.[2] It concluded that no state would go to war with the kingdom without facing a clan of nation-states protecting their financial and strategic interests, and that Biden would more likely need to rely on MBS to navigate 2024 geopolitics — a situation blamed on a [[state-department|State Department]] said to have no idea how to conduct diplomacy "without a bag of cash and a dagger in their hand."[2]
