# Commercial Driver's Licenses for Immigrants

The federal crackdown on commercial driver's licenses (CDLs) issued to immigrants is an enforcement initiative led by the U.S. Department of Transportation that, by December 2025, had found problems in eight states in the wake of several deadly crashes.[1]

## Federal enforcement

Transportation Secretary [[sean-duffy|Sean Duffy]] publicly threatened to withhold millions in federal money from California, Pennsylvania, Minnesota and, most recently, New York after investigations found problems such as licenses that remained valid long after an immigrant's legal status had expired. The department also quietly sent letters detailing similar concerns to Texas, South Dakota, Colorado and Washington during the government shutdown, after briefly mentioning those states in September.[1]

Duffy proposed new restrictions in September 2025 that would severely limit which noncitizens could obtain a license to drive a semi or a bus, but a court placed the new rules on hold.[1]

## Triggering crashes

Concern about immigrant truck drivers intensified after a tractor-trailer driver who was not authorized to be in the United States made an illegal U-turn and caused an August crash in Florida that killed three people. A fiery California crash in October that also killed three people and involved a truck driver in the country illegally added to the worries.[1]

## Colorado system glitch

Investigators discovered a glitch in Colorado's computer system that reverts to issuing a license valid for four years when a worker has to perform multiple searches in a federal immigration database; unless the worker is vigilant, some of those extended licenses slip through. Eighteen Mexican citizens who were not eligible were also issued commercial licenses.[1]

## Commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] connected the DMV coding failures to election administration, noting that she had questioned a Deputy Secretary of State under oath at trial about a "glitch" in DMV systems, and arguing that the complexity of public-private partnerships supplies plausible deniability for government wrongdoing. Her conclusion was that admitting "criminally invading migrants" into these systems is "literally killing people and diluting the vote," a theme she tied to the [[doj-voter-registration-list-lawsuits|DOJ voter registration list lawsuits]].[1]
