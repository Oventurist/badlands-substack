# The Master and His Emissary

***The Master and His Emissary*** is a book by the neuroscientist and philosopher [[iain-mcgilchrist|Iain McGilchrist]] that argues, on the basis of a large body of empirical evidence, that the Western population lives entrenched in the left hemisphere of the brain and consequently struggles both to use the beneficial qualities of the right hemisphere and to perceive that it is trapped in this way.[1] The book supplied the framework for the March 2024 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay "How Your Western Brain Exposes You to 5G Warfare" by [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]].[1]

## The left hemisphere's purpose

McGilchrist is quoted as writing that "the left hemisphere view is designed to aid you in grabbing stuff. Its purpose is utility and its evolutionary adaptation lies in the service of grasping and amassing things, and as such it is seductive."[1] Esler reads the grasping described here on two levels: the pursuit of control over the material realm, and the amassing of thoughts as things — the left hemisphere's appetite for "knowings," forms of certainty that can be seized like an object and filed alongside the rest of one particular worldview.[1]

A further quotation contrasts the hemispheres directly: "the left hemisphere is concerned with what it knows, where the right hemisphere is concerned with what it experiences."[1] The right hemisphere, McGilchrist writes, "is able to maintain ambiguous mental representations in the face of a tendency to premature over-interpretation by the left hemisphere," and its tolerance of uncertainty is implied throughout its subtle capacity for metaphor, irony and humour, "all of which depend on not prematurely resolving ambiguities."[1]

## Understanding from parts versus wholes

The book is also quoted on the two modes of understanding: "According to the left hemisphere, understanding is built up from the parts; one starts from one certainty, places another next to it, and advances as if building a wall, from the bottom up," whereas "according to the right hemisphere, understanding is derived from the whole, since it is only in the light of the whole that one can truly understand the nature of the parts."[1]

Elsewhere McGilchrist characterizes the left hemisphere's outlook as offering "simple answers," prizing consistency above all and claiming the same mechanistic models for everything that exists — a mode of thinking he associates with naive reductionist science or [[scientism|scientism]], with enthusiasm for technological solutions to complex problems, and with the designers and implementers of bureaucratic systems.[1]

## Attachment to place and the Industrial Revolution

On the "homeless mind," the book states that attachment to place runs deep, since in neurological terms the evolutionary roots of the integrated emotional system involved in forming social attachments may lie in more ancient animal attachments to place — "some animals bond as much with their nest sites as with their mothers."[1]

McGilchrist attributes much of the modern condition to industrialization, writing that "it is the Industrial Revolution which enabled the left hemisphere to make its most audacious assault yet on the world of the right hemisphere — or perhaps one should say that the left hemisphere's most daring assault was the Industrial Revolution."[1] Since that revolution, he argues, the natural world, cultural tradition, the body, religion and art have been so conceptualized, devitalized and deconstructed that their power to help us see intuitively beyond the hermetic world set out by the left hemisphere has been largely drained from them.[1]

A related passage describes the replacement of a physical environment of natural rhythms and cycles by "an unyielding, inert, confrontational environment of non-living surfaces, straight lines, concrete masses and largely generic shapes, which are widely experienced as alienating," with the result that the left hemisphere's world has become externalized: when the right hemisphere checks against the real world of experience, that world "out there" has already been colonized by the left hemisphere's vision, producing "a self-reflexive hall of mirrors."[1]
