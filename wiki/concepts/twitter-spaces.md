# Twitter Spaces

Twitter Spaces is the live audio conversation feature of the social platform [[twitter|Twitter]], launched in December 2020 as a competitor to the audio-based social network [[clubhouse|Clubhouse]].[1] In a February 20, 2023 essay for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], the writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described Spaces as a new frontier for American community after [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s purchase of the platform restored the accounts of many previously banned users.[1]

## Function and format

Spaces allows users either to participate in live conversation as speakers or simply to listen, with hosts and co-hosts controlling who is rotated up from the audience — the pool of listeners informally known as "the nest."[1] Removing a speaker back down to listener status is colloquially called being "moon-doored."[1] Emoji reactions are the primary way listeners engage with an ongoing discussion.[1]

## Disabling controversy

Under Musk's leadership in December 2022, Spaces was briefly disabled on the platform after the new chief executive learned that banned users still retained access to the feature.[1] The controversy coincided with the return of many previously suspended accounts, which effectively created a new and returning user base for the product.[1]

## Adoption on the political right

Ashe in America argued that users who had spent roughly two years exiled to alternative platforms — including [[telegram|Telegram]] live chats and the app Stereo — adopted Spaces rapidly for communication, collaboration, and social connection once readmitted to what she called "the public square."[1] Recurring categories of Spaces described in the essay include news-of-the-day discussion, local organizing, deep-dive research, religious worship, comedy and game shows, cryptocurrency education, and open-mic style entertainment.[1]

Religious Spaces include prayer groups, Bible studies, and testimony sharing; a recurring worship Space entitled "Jesus is King" was hosted several times a day by a Canadian user known as JAVA.[1] The activist organization [[america-mission|America Mission]], run by [[dustin-stockton|Dustin Stockton]] and [[jennifer-lynn-lawrence|Jennifer Lynn Lawrence]], used Spaces both as structured organizing meetings with agendas and action items and as late-night conspiracy deep dives, including a "Crypto/NFT for Newbies" session on decentralized finance.[1]

Game-show Spaces described as generally irreverent and explicit include "Boo's Clues," a Thursday-night movie and television guessing game hosted by users Paddy and Megalito, and "Who's This, What's That?", run by the user Vince in the Bay, in which players give deliberately wrong answers to an image or sound file placed in the nest.[1]

## Presentation Spaces and criticism

The most common format is the presentation Space, in which experts or featured guests speak for about an hour before hosts open dialogue to a panel or audience.[1] Ashe in America hosted such a Space featuring [[mike-lindell|Mike Lindell]] and Mesa County clerk [[tina-peters|Tina Peters]], covering the Republican National Committee chair election, the Maricopa audit, the [[mesa-county|Mesa County]] election reports, and Peters' upcoming trials.[1]

She was critical of very large Spaces, describing them as "effectively the mainstream media of Twitter Spaces," and singled out hosts such as [[mario-nawfal|Mario Nawfal]] and Grant Cardone for featuring regime-friendly experts who defend the central narrative.[1] She wrote that prepared presentation content feels more like a show than authentic connection.[1]

## The "Tweaker Space"

On the last Friday of January 2023 — a day dominated by California mass shootings, the indictment of the Memphis officers who killed Tyre Nichols, the announcement that [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] would send Abrams tanks to Ukraine, a US counterterrorism strike in Somalia, and a large joint US-Israel military drill — a user named Nikki opened a Space entitled "WTF PSYOPS EVERYWHERE?!" with a single rule barring serious discussion of news or current events.[1] The room persisted for six days, renaming itself "PsyOps and Rabbit Holes" and then "MonkeyOps and Rabbit Holes," accumulating roughly two dozen co-hosts and swinging between three and five participants and audiences of 400 to 500.[1] Topics ranged across child trafficking, virology, claims that Biden is a clone, and the shape of the earth.[1] The Space finally crashed on the sixth day.[1]

## Feature requests

The essay closed with an open letter to Musk proposing user stories for the Spaces development team: a single-action control to clear the nest against spam and "porn bombs," the ability to transfer host status to a co-host, persistence of the nest and chat thread after a Space closes, access to the full emoji board, and a persistent emoji board comparable to the sound board.[1]

## Significance

Ashe in America characterized the restoration of open conversation as the most profound consequence of Musk's acquisition, arguing that America Mission and the January 6 community demonstrate that Americans are capable of self-organization — and predicting that residual platform actors and "regime handlers" would attempt to interfere with those efforts.[1]
