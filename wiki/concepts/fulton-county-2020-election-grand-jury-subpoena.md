# Fulton County 2020 Election Grand Jury Subpoena

The Fulton County 2020 election grand jury subpoena is a federal demand issued by the [[department-of-justice|U.S. Department of Justice]] seeking the identities and contact details of essentially every person who worked on the 2020 presidential election in [[fulton-county|Fulton County]], Georgia.[1]

## Scope of the demand

The subpoena sought the names, positions, residential and email addresses, and telephone numbers of thousands of individuals, including county employees, bus drivers who operated mobile voting sites, volunteers, and temporary poll workers.[1] Commentary from Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] characterized the request as covering Election Day and election-night workers as well as those involved in risk-limiting audits, recounts, and signature verification — "everyone who was given an order … and everyone who gave an order."[1]

## Timeline

The subpoena was dated April 17, 2026 and served on April 20, 2026 on the county's director of elections.[1] Fulton County filed a motion to quash on May 4, 2026.[1]

## Context

The demand follows years of claims by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] that Fulton County, a Democratic stronghold, committed widespread voter fraud that cost him Georgia, a state he lost to Joe Biden by 11,779 votes.[1] County officials placed the subpoena within a broader pattern of [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] efforts to obtain 2020 election records from swing states, including a January 2026 FBI seizure of ballots from a Fulton County warehouse.[1]

## Motion to quash

County lawyers argued the subpoena was "grossly overbroad and untethered to any reasonable need" and "cannot yield any evidence that could result in a criminal prosecution" because the statute of limitations for the related federal crimes had expired.[1] Board of Commissioners Chairman [[robb-pitts|Robb Pitts]] called the move "outrageous federal overreach designed to intimidate and chill participation in elections," adding that "Fulton County will not be intimidated."[1]

Badlands commentary noted that motions to quash grand jury subpoenas are historically difficult to win, because a grand jury's role is investigative rather than adjudicative and courts therefore grant prosecutors broad latitude; the counter-argument offered was that "the accused criminals don't get to dictate the prosecution's witness list."[1]
