# End-to-End Encryption

End-to-end encryption is the practice of securing communications so that only the sender and recipient can read them, denying access to intermediaries including platform operators and governments. Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] identified the criminalization of encryption as one of the most alarming components of the multinational offensive against online speech.[1]

## Criminalization in the Durov indictment

Among the charges brought against [[telegram|Telegram]] founder [[pavel-durov|Pavel Durov]] by Paris prosecutor [[laure-beccuau|Laure Beccuau]] was "providing cryptology services, aiming to ensure confidentiality without certified declaration."[1] DeLarme wrote that under such a theory anyone who provides an encryption service — meaning any platform that makes it harder for the government's digital panopticon to record private messages — could be held criminally liable.[1] A further count, "providing a cryptology tool, not solely ensuring authentication or integrity monitoring without prior declaration," meant in his reading that any encryption not allowing the government a backdoor with full access is a criminal offense capable of sending the heads of technology companies to prison.[1]

## Historical backdoor campaigns

DeLarme argued that the implication of the indictment is essentially the end of internet freedom as we know it, a long-standing objective of the ruling class.[1] He traced the effort back at least to the Clinton administration in the wake of the [[oklahoma-city-bombing|Oklahoma City bombing]], when officials argued that dangerous domestic elements could not be allowed to hide behind encryption and that a backdoor into the internet was therefore necessary.[1] He summarized the recurring pattern with the phrase "Ordo ab Chao" — order out of chaos.[1]

## Relation to platform bans

The same demand for state access to content underlay [[turkey|Turkey]]'s October 2024 block of [[discord|Discord]], justified by the platform's refusal to share information including IP addresses and content with Turkish security units.[1] DeLarme presented these actions as parts of a single trend in which governments, tired of merely pressuring platforms to censor, increasingly rely on force.[1]
