# Edible Insects

Edible insects — chiefly farmed crickets and mealworms processed into powders — became a live political issue in Europe in January 2023 when a cricket-derived additive was authorized for use across a wide range of ordinary food products.[1]

## EU cricket additive authorization

As of late January 2023, a food additive made out of powdered crickets began appearing in foods from pizza to pasta to cereals across the European Union, with defatted house crickets entering the continental food supply without the awareness of the vast majority of consumers.[1] The change followed a ruling by the [[european-commission|European Commission]] passed earlier that month, which cited the scientific opinion of the [[european-food-safety-authority|European Food Safety Authority]] in finding the additive safe for a range of products "including but not limited to cereal bars, biscuits, pizza, pasta-based products, and whey powder."[1] Under the rules, the crickets must be made to "discard their bowel content" before being frozen.[1]

## Health concerns

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that what "sounds like a hip, progressive new trend that will surely deal a decisive blow against climate change might actually prove quite hazardous to our health."[1] He noted that several bacterial species have been associated with both farm-reared and wild-caught edible insects, including species from the genera Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Micrococcus, Lactobacillus, Erwinia, Clostridium and Acinetobacter, as well as members of the family Enterobacteriaceae, and that insects can potentially serve as replicative vectors for viruses that infect vertebrates.[1] He closed by asking whether a third-party organization could at least verify that the insects' bowel contents had been discarded.[1]

## Industrial scale-up and institutional backing

By October 2023 insect protein had moved from regulatory approval into industrial production in the United States, when [[tyson-foods|Tyson Foods]] announced a partnership with the Netherlands-based manufacturer [[protix|Protix]] involving a cash investment and an agreement to build a large enclosed facility covering the breeding, incubating and hatching of insect larvae.[2] Processed larvae were slated for use as ingredients in aquaculture and pet food and potentially in livestock and plant feed.[2] Protix, selected in 2015 as one of the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]]'s Technology Pioneers, produces roughly 14,000 metric tons of insect food per year under the stated rationale of fighting climate change.[2]

Badlands coverage situated these developments within a wider promotional push by globalist governments and the World Economic Forum, which has anticipated the day when \"we can all buy a bag of edible insects at our local grocery store.\"[2] The same coverage cited EU approval of cricket powder in major processed foods and reporting that 1,000 Australian schools had added \"eco-friendly\" cricket chips to their canteens, with children conditioned on camera to enjoy them.[2]

## Dispute over framing

Contributor Ashe in America noted that [[npr|NPR]] published a 2023 podcast episode describing criticism of insect eating as a racist and antisemitic conspiracy theory about \"global elites,\" despite NPR itself having promoted insect consumption in 2016, 2017 and 2018.[2] She argued that the existence of a collaborative network of governments, corporations, pseudo-governmental entities, academics, NGOs and charitable organizations seeking a shift away from animal protein is documented rather than speculative, and that Tyson's roughly 14% share of the global protein market makes its entry into bug protein a material rather than symbolic development.[2]
