# Ancient German Language Theory

The **Ancient German language theory** (abbreviated "AG" in the source series) is the claim, advanced in the [[the-werewolf-game|Werewolf Game]] series, that the entire world once spoke a single tongue best reconstructed as Ancient German, and that surviving place names and vocabulary worldwide still encode its meanings.[1] The proposed test of the theory is predictive: knowledge of AG is said to allow one to make correct predictions about the world.[1]

## Dodson's predictive claim

The theory is attributed principally to [[frederick-dodson|Frederick Dodson]], described as a multimillionaire success coach and language researcher who has published many books using AG to make predictions about people, culture and geography.[1] The article quotes the first chapter of Dodson's *The Secret History of Polynesia*, in which Dodson recounts a bike tour from Kaikohe to Horeke in New Zealand and says he knew in advance the roughly 40-kilometre ride would be mostly downhill because the German word *hohe* means "heights" — and claims he could ride around the country predicting rivers, lakes, plains, glaciers, shrubs, forests and views simply from town names.[1] Dodson's *Levels of Heaven and Hell* and *Extraterrestrial Linguistics* are cited as containing hundreds of pages of comparisons between AG and world languages.[1]

## Sample cognate arguments

The article offers two worked examples. The first concerns making and motherhood: the Arabic *maher* ("deft, skill," "a job well done") is compared to the Māori *mahi* ("effort"), whose first syllable *ma* means "made by/acted upon," and to the near-universal ma/pa dichotomy for mother and father — Mandarin *muqin* and *fuqin* — with the synthesis that a mother is a maker of babies.[1] The Buddhist concept *Maya*, that creation is illusion, and *Mahayana* Buddhism's self-description as a great path are enlisted to link creating, skill and greatness through *maha*.[1]

The second example concerns words for God. Latin *Dei* is related to *Theos*; Chinese *Tian*, Pashto *Xwdai*, Persian *Xuda*, Indian *Dev*, Kazakh *Quday* and Kyrgyz *Kuday* are presented as cognates.[1] The article extends the family to *ten* as a word for God or the Ultimate, Hungarian *Isten*, the Lakota *Wakan Tanka* ("Great Mystery"), Uyghur *Tengri*, and Turkish *Tanri* — whose final syllable is linked to "ray" and to the Egyptian deity Ra — and to the Tibetan Buddhist *Tenzin*.[1] The author invokes the [[qanon|Q]] formulation, "how many coincidences before it's mathematically impossible?"[1]

## Implications claimed

If all languages share one origin, the article argues, mainstream history is demolished, since it categorically denies trans-oceanic contact between ancient peoples.[1] Extensive overlap between Māori and Aztec vocabulary is offered as evidence that the [[koryos|Koryos]] and other peoples were engaged in trans-Pacific travel possibly thousands of years earlier than accepted chronology allows.[1] The suppression of monogenesis research by the [[linguistic-society-of-paris|Linguistic Society of Paris]] in 1866 is presented as the institutional cover-up of this fact.[1]

Mainstream historical linguistics regards these comparisons as chance resemblances rather than demonstrated cognates, and does not recognize an Ancient German world language.[1]
