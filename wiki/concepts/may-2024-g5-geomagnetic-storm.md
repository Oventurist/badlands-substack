# May 2024 G5 Geomagnetic Storm

The **May 2024 G5 geomagnetic storm** was an extreme space weather event that struck Earth over the weekend of May 10–12, 2024, prompting widespread warnings about possible failures of the United States power grid and communications networks.[1]

## Warnings and impact

The [[space-weather-prediction-center|Space Weather Prediction Center]] of the [[national-oceanic-and-atmospheric-administration|National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration]] warned on the Friday night of the event about an "extreme" G5 geomagnetic storm impacting Earth, which lasted into the early morning hours.[1] G5 represents the highest level of geomagnetic storm on a scale running from G1 to G5.[1]

Although there were disruptions in communications, no significant failures in the US power grid were reported.[1] The event caused disturbances in some satellite orientation and operations, deterioration in radio communications that rely on ionospheric transmission, and some outages in GPS networks.[1] Captain [[john-konrad|John Konrad]], chief executive of Captain, wrote on X that "The solar storm has knocked out almost all long-distance shortwave radio."[1] [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] reported that a "Major geomagnetic solar storm" was underway, the "Biggest in a long time," adding that [[starlink|Starlink]] satellites were "under a lot of pressure, but holding up so far."[1]

The last G5 storm of comparable strength struck Earth in October 2003, sparking power outages across Sweden and damaging transformers in South Africa.[1] Because the digitalization of the economy has increased dramatically since then, commentators noted an increasing risk of grid failures and communications disruptions from intense solar activity.[1]

## Commentary in Badlands Media

Writing in the Badlands News Brief, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] mocked the coverage, characterizing the resulting headlines as "OMG, I can't believe we actually survived the fake crisis we just made up!"[1] She described the media's promotion of a "cannibal solar storm" as fear porn that "reached 11 over the weekend," noting that the "cannibals never materialized" while many Americans got their first pictures of the auroras — visible, she wrote, mainly through modern mobile phone cameras.[1] She characterized the episode as a "mass formation" whose breaking news amounted to "nothing happened."[1]
