# Sodium Nitrate and Sodium Nitrite

Sodium nitrate and its close counterpart sodium nitrite are preservatives, food colorings, and "flavor enhancers" used in bacon, ham, fish, hotdogs, lunch meats, corned beef, smoked fish, cured meat, and other processed foods.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that the ingredient, despite its harmless-sounding name, is highly carcinogenic once it enters the human digestive system.[1]

## Mechanism

Once ingested, the compound forms a variety of nitrosamine compounds that enter the bloodstream and, in the article's description, wreak havoc on a number of internal organs — the liver and pancreas in particular.[1]

## Regulatory history

Sodium nitrite is widely regarded as a toxic ingredient, and the [[usda|USDA]] attempted to ban the additive in the 1970s.[1] That effort was vetoed by influential food manufacturers who complained they had "no alternative" for preserving packaged meat products.[1]

## Why it persists

The article answers its own question about the industry's continued reliance on the chemical: sodium nitrite turns packaged meats bright red.[1] Functioning as a "color fixer," it makes old, dead meat appear fresh and vibrant for an unnaturally long time.[1]
