# Dot-com Bubble

The dotcom bubble was the speculative market mania that burst in 2000, when euphoria among Americans to invest their savings in any new company with "dotcom" in its name was promoted by media that Joe Lange describes as controlled by the big Wall Street banks profiting from initial public offerings of companies that would not exist a few years later.[1] Many of these companies had no profits, and their valuations rested entirely on speculation about their futures; a few, such as Oracle and Amazon, grew into large firms, but most did not.[1]

Lange highlights [[global-crossing|Global Crossing]] as the exemplary failure of the era, and notes that [[terry-mcauliffe|Terry McAuliffe]], a close friend of President [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]], sold his stock before the crash.[1] Ordinary shareholders who believed the promises about the companies' potential lost everything.[1]

He treats the episode as one iteration of a repeated cabal "template" — a narrative of novelty and inevitability that draws inexperienced retail investors in at the top of a bubble — running from the [[stock-market-crash-of-1929|1929 crash]] through the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 housing crisis]] to the artificial intelligence rally he argues is driven by only seven NASDAQ stocks.[1]
