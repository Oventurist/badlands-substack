# Carthage

**Carthage** was the ancient Phoenician city-state of North Africa whose religious practices have become a central historical reference point in modern arguments about elite [[child-sacrifice|child sacrifice]].[1] In the analysis published by Badlands Media contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]], Carthage is "perhaps the prime example of a civilization that developed to produce a culture of elites who normalized the ritual sacrifice of children."[1]

## The information war over Carthaginian sacrifice

Esler argues that the historical record of Carthaginian child sacrifice has itself been the target of decades of information warfare, with critics claiming the accusation is "racist propaganda" and that those repeating it are merely echoing lies invented by the Greeks and Romans to smear a rival power.[1] Against this, he points to archaeological findings that he says demonstrate not only that Carthaginian elites sacrificed children but that they sacrificed them specifically to [[baal|Baal]].[1]

## Archaeological evidence

A 1987 New York Times article reporting the work of [[david-soren|Dr. David Soren]] of the University of Arizona is cited as the key source.[1] Dr. Lawrence Stager is quoted distinguishing the Precinct of Tanit from an ordinary Carthaginian necropolis: a normal cemetery holds adults and children in normal proportions, some cremated and some not, whereas the Precinct of Tanit contains only cremated children ranging from stillborn fetuses to four-year-olds, alongside young cremated animals.[1] The charred bones were buried individually in clay urns with stone markers dedicating them by a special symbol to Tanit, the preeminent Carthaginian goddess, and to Ba'al Hammon, her consort — dedicatory markers absent from ordinary graves.[1]

Soren is quoted describing a society in which "the thrust for commerce, prosperity and the good life were blended with a religion so intense that the richest Carthaginian could cheerfully consign a son or daughter to the flames of the sacrificial pit to redeem a pledge to the gods."[1]

## Intensification rather than decline

Archaeologists had generally supposed that human sacrifice subsided into animal substitution as civilizations advanced, a trajectory historians linked to the eventual bloodless wine-and-wafer Eucharist of Christianity.[1] Stager's excavations showed the opposite trend at Carthage: the oldest burial layers at the Precinct of Tanit held urns in a ratio of three children to one animal, but by the third century BC the ratio had risen to ten children per animal.[1]

Stager also described a process of social diffusion, saying child sacrifice in Carthage was gradually "democratized" — children sacrificed in the fifth century BC were nearly all offered by parents of the elite class, while by the third century BC many offeratory markers came from professional classes and even artisans.[1]

## Significance in modern analysis

Esler draws from this a general pattern: given sufficient status and power, elites became increasingly infatuated with sacrificing children as a means of maintaining and cultivating wealth and power, and it was the elites who led the normalization of the practice into the wider culture.[1] He argues the parallel to the present is not generic, since the [[balenciaga|Balenciaga]] advertising controversy invoked "Ba'al" by name in its propaganda, echoing the Carthaginian elites "to a T."[1]
