# Foreign Corrupt Practices Act

The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) is the principal United States antibribery law governing corporate conduct abroad. For decades it anchored a bipartisan American effort to rein in international corruption, and its enforcement was treated as a central pillar of the U.S. compliance regime.[1]

## Trump enforcement pause (2025)

On February 10, 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] signed an executive order pausing enforcement of the FCPA.[1] Policy observers described the order as a significant break from the previously bipartisan consensus on combating international bribery, and many predicted it signalled the end of the statute in practice.[1] The move prompted concern in some quarters that the decades-long U.S. anticorruption effort would be rolled back.[1]

Compliance experts interviewed by The Wall Street Journal argued that the alarm was overstated. Bribery remains a crime in the United States, and the executive order does not invalidate the FCPA itself.[1] As a federal law, the FCPA carries a statute of limitations of at least five years, meaning U.S. regulators could still charge a company for violations under a subsequent administration.[1] Continued enforcement of comparable antibribery laws in other jurisdictions, together with the reputational risk borne by firms seen to be skirting the law, should still motivate corporations to remain compliant, experts said.[1] Some advised companies to use the shift in enforcement priorities as a window to remediate existing breaches.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands writers read the reaction as revealing. Burning Bright argued that the "western globalist hegemon" was alarmed by Trump's agenda precisely because it threatened to dismantle the rules-based international order that had governed the world for eighty years.[1] Ashe in America noted the irony of outlets that had covered for decades of overt corruption, bribery, money laundering and antitrust violations suddenly discovering an interest in corruption, contrasting the Wall Street Journal's measured coverage with a more alarmed treatment at Talking Points Memo, which warned of "structural changes" wrought less than two months into Trump's second term.[1] Her rejoinder was that bribery and money laundering would still be prosecuted — the policy simply needed strengthening first so that convictions would stick.[1]
