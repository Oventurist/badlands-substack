# World Problematique

"World Problematique" was a doctrine developed in the early 1960s and championed by [[club-of-rome|Club of Rome]] founders [[aurelio-peccei|Aurelio Peccei]] and [[alexander-king|Sir Alexander King]], described by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as a pseudoscience that can simply be characterized as "the science of global problems."[1]

## Method

Unlike other branches of science, solving the problems facing humanity was not the concern of followers of Problematique; its adherents asserted instead that the future could be known by first analyzing the infinite array of "problems" which humanity creates in modifying the environment.[1] Ehret illustrates the method with three examples: thinking people build a dam to mitigate flood damage, and biodiversity is harmed — a problem; thinking people discover the structure of the atom and obtain nuclear power, and atomic bombs and nuclear waste follow — a problem; a cure for malaria is discovered for a poor nation, mortality drops, population rises and stress is placed on the environment — a problem.[1] The list, he notes, can go on literally forever.[1]

## Implied conclusion

An adherent to Problematique would fixate on every problem caused by humans naively attempting to solve problems, observing that every human intervention leads to dis-equilibrium and thus unpredictability.[1] The Problematique-oriented mind would then conclude that if "the problem that causes all problems" were eliminated, a clean, pre-determined world of perfect stasis, and thus predictability, would ensue.[1] Ehret treats this as the hidden syllogism behind the depopulation agenda.[1]

## Institutional spread

Reporting on the growth of the Club of Rome's World Problematique agenda in 1972, [[oecd|OECD]] Vice Chair and Club of Rome member Hugo Thiemann told *Europhysics News* that research had previously been aimed at "understanding" in the belief that it would help mankind, that experience had not borne out that belief, that a serious conflict was developing between planetary dimensions and population, and that "science policy should be guided by preservation of the biosphere."[1] The organization quickly set up branches across the Anglo-Saxon world, drawing members from political, business and scientific circles who agreed that society's best form of governance was a scientific dictatorship.[1] The doctrine's statistical expression was the 1972 study [[limits-to-growth|*Limits to Growth*]].[1]
