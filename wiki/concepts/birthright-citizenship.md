# Birthright Citizenship

Birthright citizenship is the doctrine under which persons born on United States soil acquire American citizenship at birth, derived from the Citizenship Clause of the [[fourteenth-amendment|Fourteenth Amendment]] to the United States Constitution, which extends citizenship to persons born in the United States and "subject to the jurisdiction thereof."[1]

## The 2026 Executive Order Challenge

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] issued an executive order seeking to limit birthright citizenship by reading the Fourteenth Amendment's "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" language as excluding children born to parents who are not lawfully and permanently within the United States.[1] Lower federal courts blocked the order and related enforcement efforts, holding that the constitutional text and long-standing precedent protect birthright citizenship regardless of the immigration status of a child's parents.[1]

The dispute reached the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]], which heard oral arguments in [[trump-v-barbara|Trump v. Barbara]] on April 1, 2026.[1]

## Precedent

The controlling precedent is [[united-states-v-wong-kim-ark|United States v. Wong Kim Ark]], in which the Court held that children born in the United States to non-citizen parents are citizens under the Fourteenth Amendment.[1] Legal analysts cited in reporting on the case indicated that overturning or narrowing that precedent would require the Court to reinterpret more than a century of settled constitutional law.[1] While the Court's conservative majority has shown a willingness to revisit precedent in other areas, immigration and constitutional-text cases have produced mixed outcomes.[1]

## The "Foreign Power" Argument

During oral argument, Justice [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]] pressed the theory that dual obligations owed to a foreign state — such as a duty of military service inherited from a father who is an Iranian national — might mean a child born in the United States remains "subject to a foreign power," and therefore outside the Citizenship Clause's guarantee.[1] He extended the hypothetical to children born in the United States to Russian and Mexican parents.[1]

## The June 2026 ruling

On June 30, 2026 the Supreme Court upheld birthright citizenship under the Fourteenth Amendment and struck down Trump's 2025 executive order, reaffirming that the amendment guarantees citizenship to nearly all children born on U.S. soil regardless of their parents' immigration status.[2] The Court relied heavily on English common law and the historical purpose of the amendment in overturning Dred Scott, and left the Wong Kim Ark precedent intact save for narrow exceptions such as the children of diplomats.[2] Justice [[clarence-thomas|Clarence Thomas]] dissented, warning that the ruling devalues American citizenship and "may not stand the test of time."[2]

Following the decision, Sen. [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] revived his constitutional amendment to preserve citizenship, and reporting by [[just-the-news|Just the News]] argued that restriction could still be achieved either through a favorable ruling in Trump v. Barbara or through congressional legislation.[2] See [[2026-supreme-court-birthright-citizenship-ruling|the ruling]] for full coverage.[2]

## May 2025 oral arguments

On May 15, 2025, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] heard oral arguments in three consolidated cases challenging President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order curtailing birthright citizenship.[3] The proceedings centered less on the substance of the order than on whether a single district judge may issue a [[universal-injunction|universal injunction]] halting a presidential policy nationwide, with commentators suggesting a possible 5-4 outcome hinging on Chief Justice [[john-roberts|John Roberts]].[3] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that the three cases were consolidated for argument and argued that nationwide injunctions are not only a departure from tradition but unconstitutional.[3]

## The January 2025 executive order

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s sweeping birthright citizenship executive order, signed in the opening days of his second term, was intended to deny citizenship to babies born to undocumented immigrants in the United States and immediately sparked legal challenges, as blue-state attorneys general moved quickly to attack an effort they decried as unconstitutional.[4] Reporting at the time noted that Trump was still fighting to muscle his most aggressive orders, birthright citizenship among them, past the courts and Democrats, and that he would face steep obstacles in funding and implementing his policies — though unlike four years earlier, when he entered the [[white-house|White House]] with hastily drawn orders, he and his allies had used his time out of office to draft a more robust agenda.[4]

## Ninth Circuit ruling against the Trump executive order (July 2025)

On Wednesday, July 23, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit dealt the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]] a new legal loss over President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order on birthright citizenship, ruling the order unconstitutional and upholding a lower-court decision blocking its nationwide enforcement.[5] The order had sought to prevent children born on U.S. soil from automatically receiving citizenship if neither parent was an American citizen or lawful permanent resident at the time of birth.[5] The decision reinforced the long-standing interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment making birthright citizenship available to nearly all individuals born in the United States regardless of their parents' legal status.[5]

Badlands commentary welcomed the prospect of the case returning to the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] as a route to legal clarity, arguing that if the Court adopts Trump's reading of the citizenship clause — which commentators claimed is supported by the congressional debates of the era — the matter is settled, and if it upholds the "magic soil" precedent, the remedy lies with legislation, which some legislators were already pursuing.[5]

## Trump's May 2023 campaign pledge

On May 30, 2023, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] released a campaign video renewing an earlier promise to end birthright citizenship for the children of illegal immigrants — whom he called "anchor babies," describing the practice as "birth tourism" — and said he would do so by executive order on day one of a new term.[6] In the ad Trump said he would "sign an executive order making clear to federal agencies that under the correct interpretation of the law going forward the future children of illegal aliens will not receive automatic U.S. citizenship."[6]

The conservative outlet Red State criticised the pledge, arguing that American citizenship is a constitutional question rooted in the Fourteenth Amendment and therefore outside the president's unilateral authority, and that the children in question would themselves become tax-paying citizens with rights; its columnist quipped that Trump was running on "Making Americans Not Americans, Again."[6]

Badlands contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] disputed that framing, writing that Trump was identifying a longstanding problem of people entering the country to give birth and then drawing on public benefits, and that comparisons to Dred Scott and slavery were inapt.[6] He asked what became of citizens' unalienable rights and why Americans must "take a back seat constantly in the name of woke excuses," agreed that immigration reform should create a clear legal path for people who want to work and pay taxes, and argued that Ronald Reagan, George H. W. Bush and Bill Clinton had all been quiet proponents of illegal immigration because it benefited entrenched government interests.[6]
