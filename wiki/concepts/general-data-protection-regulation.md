# General Data Protection Regulation

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the [[european-union|European Union]]'s data privacy regime, passed in 2016 and brought into effect in 2018.[1] It immediately created compliance headaches for United States businesses accustomed to operating with few restrictions on the handling of personal data.[1]

## Enforcement

The GDPR authorizes substantial monetary penalties for data protection violations. In 2021 Amazon was fined 746 million euros under the regulation.[1] That record was surpassed in May 2023 when the EU fined [[meta-platforms|Meta]] 1.2 billion euros (about $1.3 billion) and ordered the company to stop transferring European users' personal information across the Atlantic by October — the largest penalty since the regime took effect five years earlier, and the latest development in a decade-long case originating in fears about US cybersnooping.[1] The decision applies to user data such as names, email and IP addresses, messages, viewing history and geolocation data.[1]

## US contrast and the "data freedom" argument

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] used the Meta fine to argue that the United States trails the world in protecting the online rights of its own citizens, calling for an Internet Bill of Rights and real data freedom legislation.[1] In this reading, GDPR is imperfect — corporations "always just evolve in the name of compliance" — but American data handling has remained a "Wild West" since the dawn of the digital age.[1] The commentary further contended that describing the disputed data as information used "for targeted online ads" understates the reality that US technology companies leverage Americans' personally identifiable information without restriction, and that data freedom should mean informed consent, real privacy rights, and meaningful oversight.[1]
