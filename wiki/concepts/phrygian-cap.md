# Phrygian Cap

The Phrygian cap is a soft conical headpiece that has served for more than two millennia as a symbol of liberty on revolutionary banners, coinage and state insignia, and simultaneously as an iconographic attribute of ancient [[mystery-cults|mystery cults]]. Its double history made it the central case study of [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]'s November 15, 2025 Badlands Media essay "Don't Be Symbol-Minded," which used the cap to argue that symbols are contested rather than fixed in meaning.[1]

## Origins in the Ionian Revolt

Ehret dated the cap's political meaning to the [[ionian-revolt|Ionian Revolt]] of the Ionian city states — collectively referred to as Phrygia — against the [[persian-empire|Persian Empire]] in 499–493 BC, where it functioned as a symbol of freedom and honor.[1] He situated the revolt alongside Solon's defeat of the oligarchy of Athens, the Battle of Marathon of 490 BC, and the age of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Socrates and Plato, which in his account gave birth to the best of what is sometimes called western civilization.[1]

According to Ehret, only after the Ionian revolt was sabotaged and Anatolia reabsorbed under Persian control — the Persian Empire being described as the "Marcher Lord of Babylon" which managed the global mystery cults — was the cap rebranded by the newly promoted deities Mithras and Cybele-Attis, which were infused into the governance of the beaten Anatolian and Ionian population.[1] He stressed that those cults did not depict their deities wearing Phrygian caps until after the revolts were put down, and that earlier Persian renditions of Mithra, prior to the cult's infusion into Phrygia a century after 493 BC, featured no Phrygian cap at all.[1] The cults reached Rome via Phrygia during the Second Punic War (218–201 BC), with Mithra's first official appearance in Rome dated to the era of the general Pompey (100–48 BC).[1]

## Use in the Americas and the French Revolution

[[peter-duke|Peter Duke]] catalogued the cap's appearance on the insignia of Iowa, New Jersey, North Carolina, Idaho, Arkansas, Hawaii, New York and West Virginia, on the [[united-states-senate|US Senate]] seal, on the 1867 Liberty Dollar and on the 1783 Libertatis Medal designed by [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], as well as in Uruguay, Cuba, Argentina, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Paraguay, the City of Rio de Janeiro and Santa Catarina in Brazil.[1] Ehret accepted the catalogue as demonstrable while rejecting the inference that Mithraists and mystery cultists had therefore been running all of North America for centuries, or that the fight for freedom of 1776 was a Mithraic trap.[1]

He conceded that the cap served as a motif of liberty during the French Revolution and that the [[2024-paris-summer-olympics|2024 French Olympics]] used a Phrygian cap as its mascot amid a number of Mithraic themes, a case he had earlier presented as "The Occult Messaging of the Olympics" on [[del-bigtree|Del Bigtree]]'s The Highwire.[1] In that setting, he argued, the cap clearly paid homage to the cults of Mithra, Cybele-Attis and Orpheus which sought to destroy Christianity in its early years.[1]

## The battle over meaning

Ehret's general thesis was that there has always been a battle over the meaning of symbols stretching back to the most ancient times.[1] He offered parallel examples: a pentagon can signify the golden section and thus the divine plane of the Creator, or Baphomet worship and satanism; a triangle can signify the Holy Trinity and the triune nature of Creator, Created and Creation as Truth, Beauty and Goodness, or threefold alchemical Rosicrucian occultism and the Gnostic trinity; red and white stripes can indicate freedom from tyranny or the [[british-east-india-company|British East India Company]].[1] Freedom-minded leaders who know the terrain of that battle, he wrote, will tend to promote the cap precisely for its older republican meaning.[1]
