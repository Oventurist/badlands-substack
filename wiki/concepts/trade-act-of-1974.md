# Trade Act of 1974

The Trade Act of 1974 is the United States statute that restructured American trade authority in the post-Bretton Woods era, granting the President broad negotiating powers and creating the enforcement machinery still used to respond to foreign trade practices. It established the modern role of the [[office-of-the-united-states-trade-representative|United States Trade Representative]], created fast-track negotiating procedures, and set out remedies for injury to domestic industry.

Its most consequential surviving provision is [[section-301]], which allows the USTR to investigate foreign acts, policies, and practices that burden US commerce and to impose tariffs or other measures in response. Because the Act delegates this authority to the executive branch, tariff decisions can be made without a new vote in [[congress]] — a delegation that has made the 1974 law the legal backbone of tariff policy under [[donald-trump]].

The Badlands Brief of July 17, 2026 records its use against [[brazil]], where a yearlong Section 301 investigation led to a 25% tariff on certain imports announced by [[jamieson-greer]] as part of the administration's [[america-first]] program.
