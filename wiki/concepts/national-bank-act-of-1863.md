# National Bank Act of 1863

The **National Bank Act of 1863**, also referred to as the Bank Act of 1863, was the centerpiece of the banking legislation enacted under President [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] to restore federal authority over the American currency during the [[american-civil-war|Civil War]].[1]

Together with the Banking and Currency Acts of 1862 and 1863, the law placed thousands of local state banks under a federal charter with federal supervision for the first time in decades.[1] By imposing a 10 percent tax on state bank notes, the legislation caused private independent state banks to shrink from 1,466 in 1861 to only 297 by 1865, while over 1,630 national banks took their place.[1]

The Bank Act established reserve requirements for the first time and capped interest rates in order to destroy usury within the nation itself.[1] To eliminate international interference and manipulation by Wall Street financiers, the act also required that 75 percent of all bank directors reside in the state in which the bank was located, and that all directors be American citizens.[1]

Nationally chartered banks were obliged to deposit at least one third of their capital into the federal treasury in exchange for government notes issued by the Mint and Treasury. Because a federal charter was needed to avoid the tax on state bank activity, banks found themselves lending to the government, giving Lincoln the means to avoid usurious loans from London and Wall Street.[1] The act worked in tandem with the [[legal-tender-act-of-1862|Legal Tender Act of 1862]] and the issuance of [[greenbacks|greenbacks]] and [[five-twenty-bonds|5:20 bonds]].[1]
