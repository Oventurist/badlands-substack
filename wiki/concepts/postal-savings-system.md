# Postal Savings System

The United States Postal Savings System was a public savings option operated through the United States Postal Service from 1911 until 1967. In [[a-seismic-shift|A Seismic Shift]], [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] treats it as the strongest historical precedent for a nationwide public banking option and as a system deliberately dismantled in the interests of private bankers.

## Creation and operation

Citing the Roosevelt Institute, the article notes that the system was created in 1911, before the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] was founded, and that for more than fifty years it gave Americans without access to traditional banks an alternative to predatory nonbank businesses. It relied on local banks to manage accounts and allowed all Americans to make deposits into no-cost savings accounts at post offices. Deposits earned interest, encouraging saving. The original deposit limit of $500 was raised to $2,500 by 1918; Lange identifies this cap, imposed by [[congress|Congress]], as the system's only significant drawback. An opinion piece in The Hill cited in the article dates the system's founding to an act of Congress in 1910 and describes its 55-year existence, during which it became one of the largest depository institutions in the country and provided simple, reliable basic banking services, especially in rural communities.

## Performance during the Great Depression

During the Great Depression, when the public "justifiably" lost faith in private banks, the Postal Savings System was a uniquely safe and reliable alternative and grew more popular. By 1947 more than four million people held $3.4 billion in savings across more than 8,000 postal units. The Hill's account describes it as a "safe harbor" during the Depression.

## Decline and abolition

According to the Roosevelt Institute passage quoted in the article, usage declined during the post-war economic boom as interest rates at private banks rose and after the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation began offering depositors at private banks a guarantee; the program was discontinued in 1967.

Lange's interpretation goes further. He argues that private banks had always sought to undermine the system because they could not compete for depositors in hard times, and that by getting Congress to create federal deposit insurance they made private deposits appear as secure as postal deposits, deliberately eliminating the public option's competitive advantage. Private banks could then offer higher interest through riskier investments while taxpayers absorbed the risk. He describes this history as "strategically hidden from the American people by the same private bankers who now control our media and government."

## Contemporary relevance

The article observes that where 8,000 postal units once held the savings of four million Americans, there are now more than 30,000 post offices serving every community in the country, and argues that a revived postal savings option — potentially merged with FedAccounts — would be a safeguard for the American people comparable to the [[bank-of-north-dakota|Bank of North Dakota]] at state level and to the public banking safeguards created in Costa Rica by José "Pepe" Figueres. Advocates cited include Mark Dimondstein of the American Postal Workers Union and the Postal Service Office of Inspector General; the principal opponent cited is the American Bankers Association.
