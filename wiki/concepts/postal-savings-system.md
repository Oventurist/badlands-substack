# Postal Savings System

The United States Postal Savings System was a public savings option operated through the U.S. Post Office that existed before the founding of the Federal Reserve and lasted until 1967.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Badlands Media presents it as historical proof that a nationwide [[public-banking-option|public banking option]] can work in the United States, and as a template for a future restoration of postal banking.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Creation and operation

The system was created in 1911 — before the Fed was founded — and relied on local banks to manage accounts while allowing all Americans to make deposits into no-cost savings accounts at post offices.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Deposits earned interest, and the original deposit limit of $500 was raised to $2,500 by 1918; the article notes that the only real drawback was that Congress limited how much Americans could deposit.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) A separate account in the source dates the system's statutory origin to an act of Congress in 1910 and describes a 55-year existence during which the Postal Savings System became one of the largest depository institutions in the country, serving as a safe harbor during the Great Depression and providing simple, reliable basic banking services especially in rural communities.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Performance during the Great Depression

During the Great Depression, when the public justifiably lost faith in private banks, the Postal Savings System was a uniquely safe and reliable alternative.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) By 1947 more than 4 million people held $3.4 billion in savings across more than 8,000 postal units.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Badlands Media argues that the system became more popular precisely because the private banking system had collapsed, and that this history has been strategically hidden from the American people by the same private bankers who are said to control the media and government.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Decline and termination

During the post-World War II economic boom, interest rates at private banks rose, and after the [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation]] offered depositors at private banks a guarantee, usage of the Postal Savings System declined and the program was discontinued in 1967.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) The article characterizes federal deposit insurance as a "cabal trick": by removing the risk that had made private banks unattractive, the government stripped the Postal Savings System of its competitive advantage, allowing private banks to offer higher interest through riskier investments while depositors were protected with taxpayer money.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Private bankers, the article says, had always sought to undermine postal savings because they could not compete for depositors in hard times.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

## Case for revival

Where 8,000 post offices once served more than 4 million savers, there are now over 30,000 separate post offices, and unlike private banks they serve every community in the country.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Restoring postal savings is presented as a way to eliminate predatory non-bank options such as payday lenders and check-cashing companies: FDIC data cited in the article found 6.5 percent (8.4 million) of U.S. households were "unbanked" and 18.7 percent (24.2 million) were "underbanked."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) Because the footprint of the [[united-states-postal-service|U.S. Postal Service]] tracks population rather than commercial activity, it offers a way to reach this vulnerable population quickly.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)

Proposals to merge postal banking with [[fedaccounts|FedAccounts]] would, in the words of one cited scholar, make the U.S. money-and-payments system fully public infrastructure akin to roads, sidewalks, public libraries and the judicial system.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift) The [[american-bankers-association|American Bankers Association]] has warned that FedAccounts and postal banking together would represent a "seismic shift in our national banking infrastructure."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-seismic-shift)
