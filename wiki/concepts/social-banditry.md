# Social Banditry

Social banditry is a concept introduced into the historical and sociological lexicon in 1959 by the Marxist scholar Eric Hobsbawm. It describes figures — sometimes fictional, sometimes real — who operated outside the law and were widely revered for their efforts to mete out justice in an unjust world.[1]

## Theory

Hobsbawm's theory, which historians continue to debate, rested on a specific Marxian analysis of power and economic relationships in agrarian societies, with bandits (or the idea of bandits) providing a form of resistance in the face of rampant inequality.[1] Such characters were said to transcend geography and era, ranging from the legendary English outlaw Robin Hood of Sherwood Forest and his band of Merry Men in 14th-century England, to violent real-life outlaws such as Jesse James and Billy the Kid in the post-Civil War United States, to Pancho Villa in early 20th-century Mexico.[1]

## Invocation after the killing of Brian Thompson

In December 2024, Politico invoked the concept to explain the public reaction to the killing of UnitedHealthcare chief executive [[brian-thompson|Brian Thompson]] in New York. In the wake of the early-morning shooting, social media lit up not with shock and horror but with something more akin to joy, with users posting sentiments such as "This needs to be the new norm — EAT THE RICH."[1] The glee with which many online responded shocked politicians and pundits alike, and Politico suggested history offered an explanatory framework in Hobsbawm's model.[1]

## Badlands critique

Badlands commentary treated the article as a rare instance of a legacy outlet admitting its Marxism openly rather than concealing it behind academic framing.[1] The principal objection raised was to the invocation of Robin Hood: the commentary argued the popular claim that Robin Hood "stole from the rich to give to the poor" is a distortion, and that in the story — dating to the late 15th or early 16th century — Robin stole from the treasury, taking from the government and returning to the people what was rightfully theirs.[1]

On that reading, the true Robin Hood idea is manifested in efforts such as DOGE or the #Digital1776 position advanced by the writer GMONEY, while the counterfeit version is the celebration of a self-proclaimed Marxist named Luigi murdering a private citizen in the street.[1] The commentary described this as a moral inversion representing the logical conclusion of social Marxism, using weaponized empathy to decriminalize crime while simultaneously reframing government as a benevolent public good.[1]
