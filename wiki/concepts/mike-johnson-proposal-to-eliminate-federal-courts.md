# Mike Johnson Proposal to Eliminate Federal Courts

On March 25, 2025, Speaker of the House [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] (R-La.) publicly floated the possibility that [[us-congress|Congress]] could eliminate some federal district courts, responding to pressure from his party's right flank over judges who had ruled against President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[1] The remark came amid a broader Republican campaign against the federal judiciary, which had blocked a series of actions taken by the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]].[1]

## Statements

"We do have the authority over the federal courts, as you know. We can eliminate an entire district court. We have power of funding over the courts and all these other things," Johnson told reporters, adding: "But desperate times call for desperate measures, and Congress is going to act."[1] Johnson, a former constitutional attorney, later clarified that he was making a point about Congress' "broad authority" over the "creation, maintenance and the governance" of the courts rather than announcing a specific legislative plan.[1]

## Constitutional basis

Article III of the Constitution establishes the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] but leaves it to Congress to "ordain and establish" inferior federal courts, a structure that gives the legislature authority to create, restructure, or abolish lower courts.[1] Congress has done so before: in 1913 it abolished the [[commerce-court|Commerce Court]], redistributing its judges to the federal appeals bench.[1]

## Context

The proposal accompanied funding threats and calls by Trump and conservative allies for the impeachment of specific federal judges, most notably U.S. District Judge [[james-boasberg|James Boasberg]], who attempted to halt the administration's use of the [[alien-enemies-act|Alien Enemies Act]] to deport Venezuelan migrants.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] framed the episode through the Anti-Federalist critique of the ratified Constitution, quoting Brutus 11's warning that the judiciary would "mould the government, into almost any shape they please" and that its opinions "will have the force of law; because there is no power provided in the constitution, that can correct their errors."[1] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted separately that because district courts were created by Congress they may likewise be abolished or reformed by Congress, and tied the debate to what she described as federal judges behaving as "activists in robes."[1]
