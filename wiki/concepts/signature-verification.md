# Signature Verification

Signature verification is the process by which election officials confirm the identity of a mail-in voter by comparing the signature on the ballot affidavit envelope against signatures already held in the county's records.[1] Under Arizona law, the term means specifically that a human being looks at a signature on a mail-in ballot envelope and compares that signature to other signatures in the county record.[1]

## Central issue in Lake v. Hobbs

Signature verification was the narrow legal question at the heart of the second phase of [[kari-lake|Kari Lake]]'s election challenge, [[lake-v-hobbs|Lake v. Hobbs et al.]] — namely whether [[maricopa-county|Maricopa County]] conducted signature verification in accordance with the Arizona statute.[1]

On day two of the trial, held Thursday, May 18, 2023, Team Lake used the county's own data to argue that lawful verification did not occur.[1] According to testimony, 779,330 signatures were compared in less than six seconds each; 512,597 of those in less than four seconds; and 321,495 in less than three seconds.[1]

The data also showed a correlation between faster review speed and higher approval rates. Plaintiffs highlighted the example of the reviewer designated User 79, who processed 54,298 signatures at a 98.9 percent approval rate overall; of these, 45,217 were compared in under six seconds at a 99.91 percent approval rate, 37,524 in under four seconds at 99.97 percent, and 27,196 in under three seconds with a 100 percent approval rate.[1]

Plaintiffs' expert [[erich-speckin|Erich Speckin]] testified that the 45,670 signatures approved by User 26 and User 9 should be removed from the 321,495 figure because those comparisons were completed too quickly to have been performed by a human.[1] The plaintiffs' conclusion was that if machines were conducting signature verification, then verification under Arizona law and statute did not take place.[1]

## Statutory standard litigated in 2023

Under [[arizona-revised-statutes-16-550|A.R.S. § 16-550]], Level 1 signature verification means that a human being reviews the signature on the ballot packet envelope and compares it against at least one exemplar signature held in the county database.[2] Lake's attorneys emphasized that the statute's words "shall compare" require an actual comparison, and that approving signatures without comparing them to an exemplar is not signature verification; the defendants' own witness testified to the same understanding.[2]

At the Count III trial in May 2023, Team Lake sought to show that for at least 70,839 and possibly 274,319 ballot packets, Level 1 verification as defined in the law did not occur.[2] Expert [[erich-speckin|Erich Speckin]] testified from the county's own data that 731,835 comparisons were completed in five seconds or less, 615,425 in four seconds or less, 465,259 in three seconds or less, 274,319 in two seconds or less, and 70,839 in one second or less.[2] Testimony that more than 45,000 signatures were approved via batch upload supported the argument that, to the extent machines performed the comparisons, statutory verification did not take place.[2]

Judge [[peter-thompson|Peter Thompson]] rejected the claim, holding that adopting a deficiency-of-time theory would require the court to rewrite both the Elections Procedures Manual and Arizona law to insert a minimum time for signature verification.[2] Critics argued the ruling effectively upholds the position that the mere presence of a signature on a ballot packet suffices for approval, and that rubber-stamping tens of thousands of ballots in under one second qualifies as lawful verification.[2] Because signature verification is the single control on [[mail-in-voting|mail-in balloting]], commentators including [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that if signatures are not compared per the statute there are no controls ensuring only lawful ballots are cast, and that the promise on which Americans were sold mail-in voting was thereby shown to be false.[2]
