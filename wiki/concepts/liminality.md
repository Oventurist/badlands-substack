# Liminality

**Liminality**, as set out in the April 5, 2025 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] feature "The Liminal: Navigating the Emerging Space Between Selves" by [[observing-consciousness|Observing Consciousness]], is the threshold state a person occupies after the illusions of an old identity have cracked but before a new self has formed.[1] The essay, published as a follow-up to the author's earlier piece "Walking the Path," describes the reader as "no longer asleep, but not fully awake in the way you imagined," having crossed "the bridge of realization," stood "in the fire of emotional unraveling," and begun the work of integration — only to find that what follows is quiet.[1]

The article characterises that quiet as "not peaceful, necessarily, but eerily still," and defines the liminal as "a space between old and new, between letting go and arriving," neither destruction nor creation but "the pregnant pause between breaths."[1] Its central reassurance to readers who feel uncertain, disoriented or numb is that "you are not lost. You are becoming."[1]

## Systems-theoretic and neurological framing

In systems theory, the essay states, liminality is a threshold state marking the boundary between two modes of being.[1] The anthropologist [[victor-turner|Victor Turner]] is credited with describing it as "a sacred disorientation," a moment outside time and structure in which transformation becomes not only possible but inevitable.[1]

Neurologically, the state is presented as a disintegration of former identity structures.[1] [[andrew-newberg|Andrew Newberg]]'s work on the neurological basis of spiritual experience is cited for the claim that such states can deactivate the parts of the brain responsible for ego and spatial orientation, so that a person is "literally losing your sense of self, time, and place" — framed not as loss but as a gateway into a more expansive state of being.[1]

The energy-medicine researcher [[shamini-jain|Shamini Jain]] is invoked for the proposition that moments of inner stillness permit recalibration: the body, "no longer held together by outdated frequencies," begins to attune to a new [[coherence|coherence]].[1] The essay insists the process is not breakdown but re-coding.[1]

## Behavioural and emotional signatures

The article catalogues day-to-day effects of the recalibration. Thought patterns may shift from urgency and survival toward curiosity and spaciousness; sensitivity to previously neutral environments may increase; and a greater need for solitude may emerge as the system attunes to an inner frequency.[1] Emotionally, reactivity may fall away, or give rise to "a confusing numbness that is not depression, but rather a holding pattern before reactivation," with former anxiety triggers meeting quiet observation and the mundane suddenly feeling sacred.[1] The essay describes this as a reorientation "from doing to being, from reacting to sensing," governed by a new internal compass of intuitive awareness rather than conditioned response.[1]

Vivid, symbolic or prophetic dreams are named as common at this stage, together with swings between joy and hope and waves of inexplicable grief or apathy — described not as mood disorders but as "echoes of identity shedding."[1] Social withdrawal, aversion to small talk, cancelled plans and extended time alone in nature or in one's car or room are presented as attempts "to remember your original frequency" rather than escapes from life.[1]

## Case illustration

The essay's extended illustration is "Crystal," a woman who spent 17 years climbing the corporate ladder to become a VP of Sales on a six-figure salary before walking away one morning because she "simply could not do it anymore."[1] Living in a small cabin at the edge of a wooded town, she initially experienced freedom — coffee at sunrise, aimless walks — before a thick silence set in.[1] She is quoted saying, "I thought I'd feel liberated… But I just feel... invisible. Like I died, but I am still here."[1] The author's reading is that Crystal is not dead but gestating: "not stuck," but "fermenting. Resting. Re-aligning."[1]

## Living without a map

At this stage, the article says, titles, identities and routines have fallen away, old goals feel empty, passions go quiet and even spiritual practices may lose their spark — none of which constitutes failure.[1] [[lisa-miller|Lisa Miller]], author of *The Awakened Brain*, is cited for the term "spiritual lull," a neural shift in which brain regions responsible for higher meaning remain active while emotional centres transform, likened to a cocoon phase in which perception deepens and purpose reorganises beneath the surface.[1] [[dan-siegel|Dan Siegel]]'s formulation "Before integration, we must differentiate" is used to name this the differentiation stage, in which the old narrative is fully disassembled so a new one can be built "from truth, not trauma."[1]

The liminal is presented as a challenge to "our addiction to movement," to goals and to proof that something is happening: "your mind might scream, 'Do something!' But your soul whispers, 'Just be.'"[1]

## Collective and energetic dimension

Though liminal spaces feel lonely, the essay argues, they are energetically anything but.[1] [[lynne-mctaggart|Lynne McTaggart]], author of *The Field*, is cited for the claim that intention and emotion radiate into the quantum field and influence others through what the article calls [[morphic-resonance|morphic resonance]], so that the person in stillness "is broadcasting coherence."[1] [[konstantin-korotkov|Konstantin Korotkov]]'s [[biofield-imaging|biofield imaging]] research is invoked for the finding that coherent, heart-centred states produce energy fields that extend further and more symmetrically, making the liminal person "an anchor" of stable signal in a chaotic world.[1] The essay summarises: "You are not doing nothing. You are seeding the future," and calls the pause "energetic preparation for quantum contribution."[1]

## Mythic antecedents

The article situates the experience in an ancient lineage: the Belly of the Whale in [[joseph-campbell|Joseph Campbell]]'s [[heros-journey|Hero's Journey]], Inanna's descent into the underworld, and the Buddha beneath the Bodhi tree with Mara whispering temptations.[1] In Egyptian myth it is Isis reassembling the body of Osiris, read as a metaphor for gathering scattered fragments of the self; in Norse tradition it is Odin hanging upside down on the World Tree, sacrificing certainty for wisdom.[1] The lesson drawn is that "transformation demands disorientation" and that the pause before clarity is passage rather than failure.[1] Readers are told they walk "the same path mystics, healers, and seekers have walked for centuries," and are "not broken" but "being initiated."[1]

## Relationships

Because the reader is "no longer broadcasting from fear, need, or ego," the essay holds, familiar relationships can feel strange or painful: old friends may not know how to meet the person, conversations fall flat, and a sense of invisibility sets in.[1] People who once felt like home "may now feel like static," a gap attributed to resonance rather than judgment — "You are tuning to a new station, and they are still playing an old song."[1] Some connections fade and others break abruptly, which the article frames as alignment rather than failure, while acknowledging the pain of a heart that "remembers who they were to you, before *the shift*."[1]

The stage is said to require deep discernment: noticing who energises and who depletes, declining invitations, leaving group chats, setting boundaries, and treating the resulting guilt as itself part of the clearing.[1] The systems theorist [[nora-bateson|Nora Bateson]] is quoted: "When a system transforms, some connections dissolve to allow new patterns to emerge."[1] The reader is told they are "not meant to carry everyone across the threshold," that sometimes "the most loving act is distance," and that new resonance, when it comes, "will feel like oxygen" — soul family found "not by searching, but by becoming."[1]

## Emergence

The essay's closing sections promise no dramatic arrival: no trumpets, but a new voice rising within, surprise at one's own words, resonance where there was noise, "laughing without reason" and "crying without sadness."[1] Renewed vitality is attributed not to having figured everything out but to having "stopped forcing clarity and started flowing with truth," accompanied by returning synchronicities, more vibrant colours and a looser sense of time.[1] The poet [[david-whyte|David Whyte]] is quoted — "Let your solitude become a healing balm, not a wound that stings" — in support of the counsel that during foggy stretches the reader need not shine but should "be the soil," dark and rich, in which something is rooting.[1]

The final passage tells the reader they are "in between identities, timelines, lives" but not lost, "being rewritten by Light," and describes them as "the pause in God's inhale. The whisper between universes. The tuning fork for a better world" — an ending that is instead a starting again.[1] An author's note dedicates the piece to those who sense "an undercurrent beneath reality," and the article carries the standard Badlands disclaimer that contributors' views are their own, along with a pointer to more of the author's free work at Observing Consciousness.[1]

## Follow-up: the threshold as the site of ego work

The author's April 12, 2025 follow-up feature, "Transcending Ego: Meeting the Mirror at the Threshold," explicitly builds on "The Liminal," treating the threshold state as the setting in which the work of [[ego-transcendence|transcending the ego]] properly begins.[2] It restates the liminal as the moment when a person is "not quite who you were, and not yet who you are becoming," with the comforting scripts of identity — roles, habits and beliefs — stretched thin behind them and something wider and wordless calling ahead.[2] In that hush, the essay says, the ego speaks as a long-time guardian: "Be careful. Stay safe. Remember who you are."[2]

The follow-up argues that the ego can only be met compassionately in this in-between, because in the noise of the world one becomes entangled in its defenses, whereas in liminal stillness "identities soften and the soul speaks," allowing the ego to be witnessed "not as an enemy, but as a child shaped by fear."[2] The threshold is accordingly described as "the sacred pause before the becoming," a portal and "a soft ending before a radiant beginning," and crossing it is framed not as building another identity but as embodying "a truth longing to be embodied."[2]
