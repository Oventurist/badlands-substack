# Arizona Fake Electors Case

The Arizona fake electors case is the state criminal prosecution brought by Arizona Attorney General [[kris-mayes|Kris Mayes]] against allies of [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]] over the alternate slate of presidential electors advanced in Arizona after the 2020 election.[1]

## Procedural history

A judge dismissed the original indictment after finding that prosecutors had failed to present the grand jury with the precise statutory language tied to the alleged conduct — a defect in the charging process rather than a ruling on the underlying facts.[1] The state appealed. On June 4, 2026 the [[arizona-supreme-court|Arizona Supreme Court]] rejected that appeal, leaving the dismissal intact.[1]

Mayes responded by announcing that her office would restart the prosecution. "The Arizona Attorney General's Office will return this case to the grand jury," said Mayes spokesperson Richie Taylor, adding, "We decline to comment further at this time."[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the decision to re-indict as self-incriminating on Mayes's part, describing it as "hard to see this as anything other than a regime puppet outing themselves."[1] The argument advanced was that public sentiment has shifted, with a majority of the electoral franchise doubting the authenticity of the certified 2020 vote totals; if that skepticism is warranted, the commentary held, then the conduct of the so-called fake electors was justifiable.[1] Pressing the case at a moment when [[weaponization-of-government|weaponized government]] is more widely acknowledged than ever was characterized as a bold and self-defeating move.[1]

## May 2025 remand to the grand jury

On Monday, May 19, 2025, [[maricopa-county-superior-court|Maricopa County Superior Court]] Judge [[sam-j-myers|Sam J. Myers]] ordered state prosecutors to send the case back to a grand jury, a ruling that potentially jeopardized the high-profile indictments.[2] Myers sided with the Republican defendants and found that prosecutors had failed to provide the grand jury with the text of the 1887 [[electoral-count-act|Electoral Count Act]], the federal law central to the defense, which spells out how presidential electoral votes are to be cast and counted.[2] Defense attorney [[stephen-binhak|Stephen Binhak]], who spearheaded the effort to return the case to a grand jury, said the court "got it exactly right."[2] The decision was a major setback for Attorney General [[kris-mayes|Kris Mayes]], who pledged to appeal; absent a successful appeal the ruling would require her to assemble a new grand jury and present the case along with a copy of the statute, a presentation that initially took weeks.[2]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the omission meant prosecutors had misled the grand jury about a law that, in her reading, made the alternate electors' conduct entirely lawful, and questioned why the case was remanded rather than dismissed and why prosecutors were not sanctioned.[2] She noted that five states — Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada and Wisconsin — had initiated criminal prosecutions over the 2020 alternate elector slates, and suggested defendants elsewhere should check whether their grand juries were similarly misled.[2]
