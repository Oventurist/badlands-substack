# Major Questions Doctrine

The major questions doctrine is the principle of administrative law holding that agencies and the executive may not claim sweeping economic or political authority absent a clear statement from Congress.[1] It supplied the reasoning behind the [[supreme-court-ieepa-tariff-ruling-2026|Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling]] that the [[international-emergency-economic-powers-act|International Emergency Economic Powers Act]] did not authorize broad tariffs without explicit congressional delegation.[1]

In a concurrence quoted at length by Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], Justice [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]] explained that Chevron deference had established "a presumption that was nearly the opposite of the major questions doctrine," putting a thumb on the scale in favor of delegated power whenever Congress failed to speak clearly.[1] With Chevron overruled, Gorsuch wrote, the conflict between the two doctrines is gone and the Court's application of the major questions doctrine is "not invention so much as return to form."[1]

Badlands commentary treated the doctrine as an instrument for returning the branches of government to their constitutional boxes, noting Gorsuch's broader campaign against the administrative state and the opinion's emphasis on the taxing power belonging to the representative branch.[1]
