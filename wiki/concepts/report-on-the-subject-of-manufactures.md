# Report on the Subject of Manufactures

The *Report on the Subject of Manufactures* was one of four major reports submitted to Congress by Treasury Secretary [[alexander-hamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] in 1791–92, and the document in which he set out the case for tying public debt to productive investment.[1]

## Content

In the report Hamilton argued for creating a qualitatively new form of debt by tying newly issued federal bonds to internal improvements such as roads, canals, academies, and industrial growth — a debt that would permit the nation to produce its way out of poverty and lead to "the augmentation of the active or productive capital of a country."[1] He distinguished bad debt from good debt by the guiding principle that the "creation of debt should always be accompanied with the means of extinguishment."[1]

Hamilton wrote that "to cherish and stimulate the activity of the human mind, by multiplying the objects of enterprise, is not among the least considerable of the expedients, by which the wealth of a nation may be promoted."[1] Throughout his works he maintained that value is not located in land, gold, money, or any arbitrary measure favored by followers of the British School such as [[adam-smith|Adam Smith]].[1] Hamilton biographer Nancy Spannaus, author of *Hamilton vs Wall Street*, writes that Hamilton rejected the worldview that wealth is measurable in land, precious metals, or power over other nations, holding instead that national wealth depends on the physical economic development of the nation, "including, most emphatically, the intellectual capabilities of its population for carrying out that development."[1]

Against the Jeffersonian faction promoting British free trade and attacking manufactures and strong federal government, Hamilton articulated the general principle "that every power vested in a Government is in its nature sovereign, and includes by force of the term, a right to employ all the means requisite, and fairly applicable to the attainment of the ends of such power," provided those means are not precluded by constitutional restriction, immoral, or contrary to the essential ends of political society — a power exercised "to give encouragement to the enterprise of our own merchants, and to advance our navigation and manufactures."[1]

## Place in the American System

The report is one component of the four-fold program that became known as the [[american-system-of-political-economy|American System of Political Economy]], alongside debt consolidation, national banking through the [[first-bank-of-the-united-states|First Bank of the United States]], and protective measures against foreign dumping of cheap goods, supplemented by federal bounties encouraging private enterprise aligned with the national interest.[1] Hamilton drew on the dirigiste tradition of France's Finance Minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert, whom he had praised in "The Continentalist" in 1782 for laying the foundation of French commerce.[1]
