# Louisiana Nitrogen Gas Execution of Jessie Hoffman Jr.

On Tuesday, March 18, 2025, the state of [[louisiana|Louisiana]] put a condemned man to death using nitrogen gas, carrying out its first execution since 2010 and becoming only the second state to administer the controversial method.[1] The prisoner, Jessie Hoffman Jr., had been convicted in the 1996 murder of a woman abducted from a New Orleans parking garage, and was pronounced dead at 6:50 p.m. at the Louisiana State Penitentiary in Angola.[1] Officials said the nitrogen gas had flowed for 19 minutes during what one official characterised as a "flawless" execution.[1]

## Legal challenges

Hours before the scheduled execution, a state district court judge rejected an attempt by Hoffman's lawyers to obtain a temporary pause.[1] Governor [[jeff-landry|Jeff Landry]], who the previous year had signed a bill making nitrogen hypoxia a legal alternative to lethal injection, declined to intervene.[1] On Tuesday evening the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] denied a last-ditch application in a 5-4 vote.[1]

Hoffman's attorneys had argued that the method violated his constitutional rights, including his ability to practice his Buddhist religion in his final moments — a claim resting on the contention that the breathing practice central to his faith could not be maintained under nitrogen hypoxia.[1]

## Gorsuch dissent and Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] singled out Justice [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]] as "the only Justice to actually believe in religious freedom," citing his published dissent from the denial of relief.[1] She acknowledged she did not know the details of Hoffman's case and allowed that he "could be the world's worst garbage human," but argued that "if rights are forfeited for the world's worst garbage human, then they can and will be forfeited for the rest of us, eventually."[1]

She added a personal assessment of Gorsuch, noting that he emerged from the Colorado legal community and, based on his book, "remained committed to equal justice under the law" — a stance she characterised as difficult to maintain in that environment, referencing the *Masterpiece Cakeshop, Ltd.* litigation cited in the dissent.[1] The piece closed by putting the underlying question to readers: whether death row inmates retain their right of religious freedom through execution, and whether the government can take that right at the moment of death.[1]
