# Judgment Fund

The Judgment Fund is a permanently appropriated account administered by the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|U.S. Treasury Department]] for the settlement of litigation claims against the United States.[1] Because Congress has approved the fund in advance and in perpetuity, a sitting president can use it to make settlements without seeking direct congressional approval for the specific payment — a structural feature that critics regard as an oversight gap.[1]

## Use in the 2016 Iran payments

The fund entered public controversy when it was used as the source of the $1.3 billion in interest paid to [[iran|Iran]] in January 2016, alongside the [[pallets-of-cash-to-iran|pallets of cash]] delivered to Tehran.[1] CBS News reported that the money came from the Judgment Fund and that the payment was structured as thirteen tranches of $99,999,999.99 plus a final payment of about $10 million.[1] In the Badlands Media essay "Panic in DC," [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues that this structuring existed to stay under a $100 million reporting threshold and thereby keep [[congress|Congress]] and other agencies unaware of the transfers.[1]

## The 1991 precedent

The same CBS reporting noted that the United States had previously paid out $278 million in Iran-related claims through the Judgment Fund in 1991, under President [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]].[1] Lange treats this as the decisive clue in the story, arguing that the recurrence of the same instrument, the same recipient, the same litigation pretext at the [[international-court-of-justice|International Court of Justice]], and the same backdrop of American hostages constitutes evidence of a repeated playbook rather than coincidence.[1] He notes that Bush had served as a member and then head of the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] before becoming Ronald Reagan's vice president.[1]

New York Times reporting from 1989 quoted in the article describes the Bush administration's eagerness to resolve financial disputes with Iran, including an arrangement under which Iran would receive $567 million from a frozen account, and notes that the administration did not wait for the claims tribunal at The Hague to rule against the United States but dispatched State Department legal adviser Abraham D. Sofaer to accelerate the process.[1]

## Criticism

The central objection raised in the article is that the fund allows the executive to move very large sums to a designated state sponsor of terrorism under the cover of litigation risk, even where — as Lange argues regarding the ICJ — the tribunal in question has no enforcement power and both parties have historically ignored its rulings.[1]
