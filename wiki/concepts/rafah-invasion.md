# Rafah Invasion

The Rafah invasion refers to [[israel|Israel]]'s planned February 2024 ground operation against Rafah, the southernmost city of the Gaza Strip, which triggered warnings from [[egypt|Egypt]] that the move could suspend the 1979 [[egypt-israel-peace-treaty|Egypt–Israel peace treaty]].[1]

Israeli Prime Minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] directed the Israeli Defense Forces to plan an evacuation of Rafah, which lies on Gaza's southern border with Egypt and reportedly held more than a million refugees already displaced from elsewhere in the 25-mile-long strip.[1]

The most sensitive element was the [[philadelphi-corridor|Philadelphi Route]], the nine-mile strip along the Gaza–Egypt border where diplomatic accords cap the number of troops either country may position, and which certainly do not authorize large numbers of Israeli troops and armored vehicles.[1] Netanyahu had said in late December that the route "has to be in our hands" if Gaza were to be effectively and permanently demilitarized, and in January an Egyptian official warned that "any Israeli move in this direction will lead to a serious threat to Egyptian-Israeli relations."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that the Israel–Gaza conflict going kinetic to a degree unseen in decades during the Biden administration was "more than serendipity," while declining to endorse any of the competing narratives.[1] He identified the continued political and social isolation of Israel as the dominant macro pattern, noting condemnation from the US State Department, the EU, Germany and the UK, alongside growing Saudi and Egyptian anger placing long-standing treaties under threat — closing with the rhetorical observation that a recent president had "all said treaties under control."[1]

## May 2024 escalation

By early May 2024 the operation moved from planning to execution. On May 6, 2024, [[israel-defense-forces|Israeli Defense Forces]] bombardment and air strikes hit eastern Rafah, with more than 50 targets struck as the IDF prepared for a ground operation.[2] Civilians were ordered out of the area and tens of thousands of evacuation flyers were dropped.[2] Israel said the ground offensive had become necessary after four soldiers were killed by rocket fire out of Rafah, and a senior official stated that Israel would achieve all of its war objectives.[2]

The strikes landed on a tent city housing an estimated 1.4 million displaced Palestinians, reportedly about half of them children, while the IDF had closed the Kerem Shalom crossing where the borders of Israel, Gaza and Egypt meet — leaving, in Badlands' account, nowhere for the population to go.[2] The Saudi Foreign Ministry issued a statement calling Israel's actions "genocide" and demanding that the international community intervene.[2]

International reaction was broadly negative. French President [[emmanuel-macron|Emmanuel Macron]] warned against the ground operation; the [[european-union|European Union]] called the offensive "unacceptable"; [[jordan|Jordan]] said Prime Minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Netanyahu]] was jeopardizing the ceasefire by bombing Rafah; and the [[palestinian-authority|Palestinian Authority]] alleged that the United States was not genuinely interested in achieving a truce.[2] President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] telephoned Netanyahu to urge against a Rafah ground operation absent an acceptable evacuation plan, and Axios reported that for the first time the United States had paused an ammunition shipment to Israel.[2]

## Ceasefire dispute

The escalation coincided with a collapse in the diplomatic track. [[hamas|Hamas]] announced it accepted a ceasefire and hostage deal, prompting premature street celebrations among Palestinians, but Israel responded that "this isn't what we agreed to."[2] Protesters in Jerusalem demanded a deal, chanting that "Bibi is abandoning the hostages," while Al-Quds Brigades fired rockets toward Israel.[2]

Badlands writer [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] traced the sequence: an Egyptian-brokered meeting in Cairo at which Hamas was to be presented an Israeli offer of a mutual hostage release and a six-week ceasefire, with a week to respond; Netanyahu's statement that the IDF would conduct operations in Rafah regardless of Hamas's answer, which critics called subversive and inflammatory; reporting that the Cairo meeting had failed; and then Hamas's acceptance arriving simultaneously with the bombing.[2] Against Israeli messaging that the accepted proposal had been altered from the original terms, he highlighted a quote from an anonymous Israeli official briefed on the talks who said the offer Hamas accepted "was effectively the same as one agreed at the end of April by Israel."[2]
