# Federal Reserve Balance Sheet

The **Federal Reserve balance sheet** is the accounting statement of the assets and liabilities of the United States central bank, and is one of the two principal levers — alongside the policy interest rate — through which the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] pursues its stated objectives of promoting employment, price stabilization, and moderate long-term interest rates.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] described the balance sheet and interest rates as "the two pedals on a one-speed bicycle," working in unison to accelerate or brake the economy, and argued that the balance sheet is the less publicly understood of the two.[1]

## Composition

Anything the Fed purchases or owns is an asset, and the Fed itself decides whether to hold, expand, or shrink those holdings.[1] The bulk of the asset side consists of government securities: Treasury bills with maturities of four to fifty-two weeks, Treasury notes maturing in two to ten years, and Treasury bonds maturing after more than ten years.[1] Second in size are agency [[mortgage-backed-securities|mortgage-backed securities]], packaged instruments containing baskets of home loans originated by banks and financial institutions and sold on to investors.[1] Fed assets also include loans to banks extended through the repo facility and the discount window — effectively the lending of Treasuries to banks to keep credit markets functioning smoothly and to support economic growth.[1]

On the liability side sit dollars in circulation within the United States, bank reserves, and reverse repurchase agreements, which are borrowings of Treasuries from commercial counterparties used to hold the federal funds rate within the Fed's target range.[1]

## Mechanics

By buying assets such as Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities the Fed infuses money into markets; by selling them it absorbs money from markets.[1] In 2008 the Fed bought large quantities of bonds to inject liquidity into the economy and push down bank rates.[1] In the conventional account, the Fed expands its balance sheet during recessions or extraordinary events such as the COVID-19 pandemic, and should in theory reduce or "normalize" the balance sheet once the economy recovers and the excess assets are no longer needed.[1]

Carlson argued that in practice this normalization has rarely occurred: over the fifteen years preceding his April 2023 essay, the Fed did very little to reduce its holdings, behaving, in his analogy, like a consumer who acquires new possessions without discarding old ones — a hoarder.[1] A second analogy he offered compares the balance sheet to a credit card: balances may be run up when cash flow is tight, but they must eventually be paid down, and paying down too aggressively can itself trigger a fresh cash-flow crisis requiring more borrowing.[1]

## Historical trajectory since 2008

Before the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 financial collapse]] the Fed held a little under $1 trillion on its balance sheet.[1] When the collapse occurred, the Fed increased the balance sheet by roughly 250 percent in a matter of months through purchases of government and mortgage-backed securities, on the stated reasoning that financial markets would collapse and banks become illiquid without such extreme measures.[1]

During the eight years of [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]]'s presidency the Fed added a further 219 percent to the balance sheet, on top of the 250 percent added in the three months leading into his inauguration, and at no point in those eight years did the Fed judge the economy healthy enough to begin reducing its holdings.[1] Carlson characterized this as a "sugar high" economy — an artificially stimulated propping up of activity — and argued the American economy was not in "intensive care" for the entire eight-year period, but that policymakers chose to keep it propped up so that the administration's policies would appear beneficial.[1]

In the first year of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s presidency the balance sheet stopped growing and shrank by about 1 percent, even though [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] — whom Carlson labelled a committed money printer — still chaired the Fed for that year.[1] After Trump appointed [[jerome-powell|Jerome Powell]] as chair, the balance sheet shrank by 15 percent over roughly a year and a half, a contraction Carlson said produced no perceptible economic pain because Trump's economic policies were strong enough to keep growth going while the Fed withdrew support.[1]

In late August and early September of 2019 the direction abruptly reversed and the Fed began adding to the balance sheet again, some six months before the COVID lockdowns and with no obvious precipitating event.[1] Between that turn and the March 2020 lockdowns the Fed quietly added 11 percent.[1] Carlson raised the speculative question of whether Trump and Powell had received intelligence that a pandemic narrative was about to be unleashed and were quietly preparing the economy for it, noting that in the same window [[kary-mullis|Kary Mullis]] died suddenly and COVID was rumored to have been released at the Military World Games in Wuhan.[1]

Between the start of the [[covid-19-lockdowns|lockdowns]] and [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s inauguration the Fed increased the balance sheet by a further 178 percent, roughly half of it in the pandemic's first three months and the remainder during the subsequent recovery.[1] From Biden's inauguration to Powell's first rate increase in early 2022 the Fed added another 21 percent.[1] Over the following year of rate hikes the balance sheet shrank 6 percent — a reduction Carlson said Powell accomplished quietly while public attention was fixed on inflation and interest rates.[1] In March 2023, when [[silicon-valley-bank|Silicon Valley Bank]] collapsed and other banks were feared to be in trouble, Powell rapidly added 4 percent back, again on liquidity grounds, though the balance sheet had already begun shrinking again within weeks.[1]

## Political interpretation

Carlson argued the balance sheet can be used for political ends: whoever controls Fed decisions can temporarily paper over bad conditions by cutting rates and expanding the balance sheet, which he likened to a double espresso after three hours of sleep — unsustainable as a way of life.[1] He contended that in the fifteen years to 2023 the only genuine progress toward stabilizing and lowering the balance sheet came during Trump's pre-COVID presidency and during Powell's year of rate hikes.[1]

The essay also engaged the widely circulated claim that Trump merged the [[us-department-of-the-treasury|US Treasury]] with the Federal Reserve, noting that if true this would logically make Biden the current head of the Fed — yet the Fed was, in Carlson's reading, doing everything possible to tighten against Biden's economy.[1] He offered the alternative reading, consistent with [[devolution|Devolution]], that Trump retains influence over the Fed and is preventing the [[deep-state|Deep State]] cabal from using Fed tools to bail out its own policies.[1] Separately, he noted that some form of Fed–Treasury merger appears to have occurred with the stimulus package created at the outset of the lockdowns, the [[cares-act|CARES Act]], with one view holding that the Treasury gained direct access to the money printer and another holding that nothing substantive changed because the Fed had always given the Treasury what it asked for.[1]

Carlson predicted that Powell would soon become the Democrats' designated villain, blamed for a recession caused by rate increases and balance sheet reduction, in the same way that the [[restrict-act|RESTRICT Act]] was, in his view, a Trojan Horse for broader government control.[1]
