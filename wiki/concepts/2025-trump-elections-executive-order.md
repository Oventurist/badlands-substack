# 2025 Trump Elections Executive Order

The **2025 Trump elections executive order** is an executive order on the administration of federal elections signed by President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] in late March 2025, one of a cluster of orders issued the same week alongside directives aimed at the Smithsonian Institution and the District of Columbia.[1]

## Content and framing

The order runs more than 2,500 words and is at times densely written.[1] *The Washington Post* argued it received less attention than warranted because it was issued amid the controversy over sensitive military operational details shared in a Signal chat group that accidentally included [[jeffrey-goldberg|Jeffrey Goldberg]], editor in chief of *The Atlantic*.[1]

The paper characterized the order as illustrative of how Trump was attempting to govern largely through dictates rather than legislation, described its legal foundations as questionable, and asserted that it was rooted in the president's long-standing — in the paper's framing, false — claims that the election system is rife with fraud.[1] It predicted the order could produce chaos and change before being fully litigated, and situated it in a pattern in which no president had sought more change in more institutions more rapidly through executive orders.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] called it "the executive order we've been waiting for," arguing that the fraudulent election system is "the elephant in the room that both sides of the aisle tell you not to look at or talk about," and that Trump was "putting a magnifying glass on it."[1]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] read the *Washington Post* story as proof "that the regime is wigging," contending that the truth about elections was emerging and could not be stopped.[1] She and CannCon broke down the order on the program [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]], joined by Colonel Shawn Smith (USAF, Ret.), who reviewed how no states were currently compliant and argued that, measured against common election systems, compliance was not actually possible.[1] Ashe added that the situation was worse than they had first described, and noted that [[david-becker|David Becker]] — an analyst from the opposing camp — had also concluded that compliance was impossible.[1]

## Multistate lawsuit

On Thursday, April 3, 2025, Democratic officials in 19 states filed a lawsuit against the order, calling it an unconstitutional invasion of the states' clear authority to run their own elections.[2] It was the fourth suit brought against the executive order in the week since its issuance, and it sought to block key provisions including the new requirement that people provide documentary proof of citizenship when registering to vote and the demand that all mail ballots be received by Election Day.[2]

"The President has no power to do any of this," the state attorneys general wrote in court documents. "The Elections EO is unconstitutional, antidemocratic, and un-American."[2] Trump's order had stated that the United States failed "to enforce basic and necessary election protection," while election officials maintained that recent elections had been among the most secure in US history.[2]

Badlands commentary responded by questioning why influencers downstream from [[turning-point-usa|Turning Point USA]] had spent the week persuading the "Uniparty Right" that elections are real and that the remedy is greater power and funding for TPUSA, when in the writer's view elections are fake.[2] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] closed the segment by noting that [[liz-harrington|Liz Harrington]] "is not messing around."[2]

## April 2025 partial injunction

On April 24, 2025, U.S. District Judge [[colleen-kollar-kotelly|Colleen Kollar-Kotelly]] in Washington blocked the administration from immediately enacting parts of the order, including the addition of a proof-of-citizenship requirement to the federal voter registration form, while allowing other provisions — notably a directive tightening mail ballot deadlines nationwide — to take effect for the time being.[3] The ruling followed swift lawsuits from the [[league-of-united-latin-american-citizens|League of United Latin American Citizens]], the [[league-of-women-voters-education-fund|League of Women Voters Education Fund]], the [[democratic-national-committee|Democratic National Committee]] and others, who called the order unconstitutional.[3] Kollar-Kotelly held that the Constitution gives the power to regulate federal elections to the states and Congress rather than the president, and noted that federal lawmakers were themselves working on proof-of-citizenship legislation.[3] Badlands commentary characterized the roughly 120-page opinion as confirming that executive authority over elections is limited but not powerless, pointing to conditional federal funding as a remaining lever.[3]

## April 2025 commentary and the Social Security database angle

In an April 2025 essay reprinted by Badlands, The Atlantic argued that because the court system has limited capability to act as a guardrail against what it called Trumpist authoritarianism, elections matter vitally, and that the ballot box has always been the final and most powerful check on [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[4] The magazine characterised the executive order on election "integrity" as "nothing less than an attempt to disenfranchise his opponents and forestall electoral defeat," describing parts of it as technical but its fundamental thrust as an attempt to completely rework the constitutional rules structuring the American election system.[4]

Badlands contributor [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] responded sardonically that he was "definitely on board with this EO" and was "just waiting for Judge [[james-boasberg|Boasberg]] to tell us whether or not Trump has permission to do this."[4] Contributor CannCon framed a related executive order — directing the cleaning of illegal aliens and deceased persons from the Social Security Administration's database — as "a MASSIVE Election Integrity move," which he discussed on Badlands Daily with [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] alongside material from an April 8 congressional hearing with several secretaries of state.[4] An editor's note corrected Ashe's on-air phrase "eligible but unauthorized" to the accurate term "eligible but unregistered."[4]

## Text of the order

The order's preamble argued that despite pioneering self-government, the United States "now fails to enforce basic and necessary election protections employed by modern, developed nations," contrasting American practice with India and Brazil tying voter identification to biometric databases while the United States "largely relies on self-attestation for citizenship."[5] It noted that Germany and Canada require paper ballots counted in public by local officials, reducing disputes relative to "the American patchwork of voting methods that can lead to basic chain-of-custody problems," and that Denmark and Sweden limit mail-in voting to those unable to vote in person and do not count late-arriving ballots regardless of postmark.[5] The order stated that "Free, fair, and honest elections unmarred by fraud, errors, or suspicion are fundamental to maintaining our constitutional Republic" and that under the Constitution state governments must safeguard elections in compliance with federal law guarding against dilution by illegal voting, discrimination, fraud, and other malfeasance.[5]

## Badlands reaction (March 2025)

[[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] called the order "a great first step" while cautioning that Trump "can do everything perfectly, employing the full power of his office and still not 'fix' elections," because elections are administered at the state and local levels.[5] He argued that fixing them requires Americans to accept that the elections themselves "ARE NOT REAL OR LEGITIMATE," and that solutions therefore cannot come from officials installed through them; expecting Trump to fix everything, he wrote, amounts to "hanging him out to dry."[5] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] said she would review the order with CannCon and Colonel Shawn Smith on [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]], echoed Paul's point that "Elections are fake," and argued that making federal funding contingent upon election integrity "presents a massive opportunity to fix state and local elections" but requires public participation, warning that otherwise "elections belong to private corporations with the best marketing."[5]
