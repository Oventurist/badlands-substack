# Mass Surveillance

Mass surveillance is the bulk collection and analysis of data about entire populations rather than named suspects. Badlands Media presents it as one of the primary motivations for the high-tech societal overhaul underway in Western states, arguing that Western globalists, apparently inspired by China's excessive surveillance state, are eager to build something similar for the rest of the world.[1]

## Commercially available information

Federal agencies in the United States have been purchasing massive amounts of information about U.S. citizens from data brokers — information that could potentially be used for blackmail, stalking, harassment and public shaming.[1] This material is classified as "commercially available information," or CAI, and its scale was documented in a report produced under Director of National Intelligence [[avril-haines|Avril Haines]].[1]

The [[office-of-the-director-of-national-intelligence|Office of the Director of National Intelligence]] report itself warned that "CAI Increases the Power of the Government," observing that "the government would never have been permitted to compel billions of people to carry location tracking devices on their persons at all times, to log and track most of their social interactions, or to keep flawless records of all their reading habits."[1] Badlands characterizes the justifying ethos as a new creed: "Those who have nothing to hide have nothing to fear."[1]

## Pandemic-era expansion

Bulk collection ratcheted up significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic. The [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|Centers for Disease Control and Prevention]] purchased private data from tens of millions of cell phones to check obedience to lockdown and curfew decrees.[1]

## Military tracking of dissent

The [[pentagon|Pentagon]] is reported to be using technology that can "covertly track, locate and identify anyone expressing dissent or even dissatisfaction with the actions of the U.S. military and its leadership."[1] In a report published by [[the-intercept|The Intercept]] on 17 June 2023, details emerged of a U.S. national security surveillance strategy to that end, undertaken by the [[army-protective-services-battalion|Army Protective Services Battalion]] under its remit of safeguarding top generals from "assassination, kidnapping, injury or embarrassment."[1]

## Historical precedent

Badlands situates these developments in a lineage running back to the [[church-committee|Church Committee]] of 1975, which established that the government and its intelligence apparatus were already taking excessive liberties in keeping tabs on the citizenry — inviting readers to extrapolate to the state of data collection today.[1] The concept is closely linked in the article to [[digital-identification|digital identification]] and [[central-bank-digital-currency|central bank digital currencies]], both of which would generate additional continuous streams of personal data.[1]
