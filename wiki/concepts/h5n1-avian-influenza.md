# H5N1 Avian Influenza

H5N1 avian influenza, commonly called bird flu, is a virus circulating in wild birds, poultry and dairy cattle that has produced sporadic human infections in North America. In December 2024 the [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|Centers for Disease Control and Prevention]] reported finding mutations in samples taken from a man infected with the first severe human case of avian influenza in the United States — mutations that were not present in specimens collected from his infected backyard flock.[1]

The patient was a resident of southwestern Louisiana aged over 65, confirmed the prior week as the first person in the United States with a severe case of H5N1.[1] In a December 18 statement, the CDC said the man was infected with the D1.1 genotype of the virus, recently detected in wild birds and poultry in the United States and in human cases in British Columbia, Canada, and Washington state.[1] That strain differs from the B3.13 genotype detected in dairy cows, human cases, and some poultry across the United States.[1]

The item appeared among the bonus stories in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] of December 30, 2024, presented without editorial commentary.[1]

## 2025 communications pause and suppressed MMWR reports

In January 2025 the [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|CDC]] had been scheduled to publish several Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report items, including three concerning the H5N1 avian influenza outbreak, according to a federal health official speaking anonymously.[2] Those publications were caught up in the [[2025-federal-health-agency-communications-pause|federal health agency communications pause]], a directive issued the day after [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s second inauguration instructing agencies within the [[department-of-health-and-human-services|Department of Health and Human Services]] to halt all external communications pending review.[2]

Badlands commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the narrative for a next pandemic — this time bird flu — had been seeded for months, and that official health-agency communication is a central instrument in selling a pandemic; she read the pause as frustrating that narrative control while routing health messaging through centralized review.[2]

## Coverage during the 2025 VRBPAC controversy

In February 2025 press coverage of the cancelled [[vaccines-and-related-biological-products-advisory-committee|VRBPAC]] meeting emphasized a "record-breaking bird flu outbreak" that had affected dozens of cattle herds along with poultry flocks and sent egg prices skyrocketing, with its rapid spread in animals raising concerns about broader spread to humans.[3] Badlands writer Ashe in America countered that the alarm was unwarranted, citing the [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|CDC]]'s position — itself citing the [[world-health-organization|World Health Organization]] — that "sporadic infections with highly pathogenic avian influenza A(H5N1) viruses in mammals have been reported in the United States, Canada, and other countries, but the risk to the general public from these viruses remains low."[3] She argued that the panic served the interests of the advisory committee rather than public health.[3]

## Gain-of-function allegations (2025)

In March 2025 Legal Insurrection published an argument that the H5N1 strain was a gain-of-function creation, revisiting a case the outlet had first made in 2022 during the early days of the bird flu outbreak.[4] The reasoning rested on the strain's high kill ratio — the contention being that a fatality rate approaching 100 percent would likely only be achieved if the virus's genetics were rearranged to target a host species — and on reports that Chinese researchers had been experimenting with H5N1.[4] The author, referencing the novel coronavirus attributed to Wuhan laboratories since 2019, concluded that while the [[gain-of-function-research|gain-of-function]] judgment may have been right, the laboratory attribution was wrong, and that the virus might instead be a joint American and Dutch creation.[4]

## First human A(H5N2) death (June 2024)

On June 5, 2024, the [[world-health-organization|World Health Organization]] confirmed that a death in Mexico had been caused by the first laboratory-confirmed human infection with the A(H5N2) subtype of avian influenza.[5] The patient, a 59-year-old resident of Mexico, died on April 24 after developing fever, shortness of breath, diarrhea, nausea and general discomfort.[5] The case was the first H5N2 human infection reported anywhere and the first H5 infection reported in a person in Mexico.[5]

## FDA preparedness (May 2024)

On May 8, 2024, [[food-and-drug-administration|FDA]] Commissioner [[robert-califf|Robert Califf]] told senators that the agency was preparing for a scenario in which highly pathogenic avian influenza starts spreading among humans, saying, "This virus, like all viruses, is mutating. We need to continue to prepare for the possibility that it might jump to humans."[6] At that point H5N1 had recently begun spreading among cattle and other species, with one confirmed human case in Texas that year.[6] U.S. officials said genetic sequencing and other data indicated the influenza posed little risk to people and that there were no signs of person-to-person transmission, but that treatments, tests, and vaccines were being readied in case that changed.[6]

The Texas patient primarily experienced a single symptom, inflamed eyes, and neither that patient nor many of the infected cows suffered respiratory symptoms, even though H5N1 commonly infects the respiratory tracts of birds.[6] Califf warned that "The real worry is that it will jump to the human lungs, where, when that has happened in other parts of the world for brief outbreaks, the mortality rates have been 25 percent."[6] From 2003 to April 1, 2024, 889 cases of H5N1 had been confirmed globally according to the [[world-health-organization|World Health Organization]], of which 52 percent of patients died.[6]
