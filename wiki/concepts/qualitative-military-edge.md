# Qualitative Military Edge

The "qualitative military edge" (QME) is the U.S. legal commitment that [[israel|Israel]] retain a technological and capability advantage over other militaries in the Middle East, and it governs American weapons transfers to the region.[1] The doctrine became a live controversy in November 2025 after President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced he would sell F-35 warplanes to [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]].[1]

## Rubio assurance to Netanyahu

Prime Minister [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]] said on Thursday, November 20, 2025 that Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] had guaranteed him in a phone call that Israel's qualitative military edge would remain intact despite the Saudi F-35 deal.[1] Speaking in a lengthy Hebrew-language interview with "Abu Ali Express," a popular Telegram account, Netanyahu said Rubio "reiterated his commitment that the United States will continue to preserve Israel's qualitative military edge in everything related to supplying weapons and military systems to countries in the Middle East," including specifically "Israel's advantage regarding the supply of F-35 aircraft," noting the advantage is legally guaranteed.[1] Netanyahu declined to elaborate further.[1] Israel is the only Middle East country currently operating the F-35, regarded as the most advanced warplane in the world.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] said he had anticipated QME becoming a headline but remained skeptical the story is even true — that is, that the United States is effectively sabotaging the F-35s sold to Saudi Arabia so Israel retains a strategic advantage in a potential conflict — and said he would be very disappointed if it were true.[1] He nevertheless welcomed the political effect: Republicans defending themselves against claims of being puppets of Israel now have to explain the arrangement publicly.[1]

## Codification and the 2025 Saudi F-35 decision

Congress officially codified the U.S. commitment to Israel's qualitative military edge into law in 2008, and administrations of both parties have worked to maintain it.[2] The doctrine became directly relevant in November 2025 when President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced he would approve the sale of [[f-35|F-35]] fighter jets to [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]], a step that would make the kingdom the first Middle Eastern state other than [[israel|Israel]] to operate the aircraft and would alter the regional military balance.[2] Israel did not oppose the sale outright but asked the Trump administration to condition it on Saudi normalization of relations with Israel.[2] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] framed Trump's willingness to supply the jets in spite of Israeli QME concerns as a major sign of the president's allegiance to the Arab states over Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.[2]

## Statutory origins

Badlands commentary traces the QME's legal lineage to the Foreign Military Sales Act of 1968, under which the United States approved the sale of F-4 Phantom II fighter jets to [[israel|Israel]], establishing a precedent for maintaining its qualitative edge over neighboring countries.[3] The [[arms-export-control-act|Arms Export Control Act]] of 1976 then set the framework governing US arms exports and military assistance, requiring transfers to align with US foreign policy, national security interests, and international peace and stability.[3] In 2008 Congress passed the Naval Vessel Transfer Act, which authorized transfers of excess US naval vessels to allies and, in an amendment to the AECA, explicitly established and defined Israel's Qualitative Military Edge under Section 36(h), codified at 22 U.S. Code § 2776.[3]

## Constraint on the 2025 Saudi deal

The QME was cited in May 2025 as the likely limiting factor on the [[2025-us-saudi-arms-deal|$142 billion US–Saudi arms deal]], particularly Saudi requests for [[f-35|F-35]] jets — echoing 2017, when a roughly $110 billion arms agreement yielded munitions but no F-35s for Riyadh.[3] [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that the QME hamstrings American diplomacy, underwrites a de facto unipolar order requiring global US military presence, and should be repealed to empower allies such as [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] to police their own region.[3]

## Erosion narrative, mid-2025

In commentary published June 30, 2025, [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] argued that the QME was already slipping away as a practical matter: with Saudi Arabia offered a record-setting military sales package, rumours circulating of lifting weapons sanctions against Turkey, and [[syria-sanctions-relief|sanctions on Syria]] being lifted, all of Israel's neighbours were gaining access to cutting-edge military technology.[4] He framed this alongside demands by Syria's new leadership that the IDF withdraw from Syrian territory, concluding that [[benjamin-netanyahu|Netanyahu]]'s vision of a dominant Israeli military empire in the Middle East was instead producing stronger, increasingly cooperative neighbours and an incipient regional economic boom — conditions unfavourable to empire-building.[4]
