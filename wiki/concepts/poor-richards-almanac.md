# Poor Richard's Almanac

*Poor Richard's Almanac* was the popular annual published by [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] from 1733 to 1759, described in the Badlands Media essay "In Defense of Benjamin Franklin" as his principal instrument of mass cultural education.[1]

As a leading printer and later post-master general, Franklin judged that the American population of the 1730s did not yet possess the moral or cognitive fortitude to induce a revolutionary positive change for the world.[1] He therefore created the almanac, which wrapped moral lessons and insights into poetry, science, astronomy and philosophy lessons in every single issue.[1] The essay argues the journal probably did more than anything else to empower Americans to think on a level sufficient to understand why concepts like freedom were worth dying for, noting that taxation without representation was merely one of 27 points enumerated in the [[declaration-of-independence|Declaration of Independence]].[1]

Franklin printed the almanac under the pen name Richard Saunders, one of several aliases — along with Silence Dogood, Martha Careful and Anthony Afterwit — under which he wrote countless humorous essays applying the techniques of the satirist-republican Jonathan Swift.[1] The essay appends a list of maxims credited to Franklin's writing, including "An investment in knowledge pays the best interest," "Well done is better than well said," "By failing to prepare, you are preparing to fail," and "They who can give up essential liberty to obtain a little temporary safety deserve neither liberty nor safety."[1]
