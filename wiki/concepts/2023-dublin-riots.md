# 2023 Dublin Riots

The 2023 Dublin riots erupted in [[ireland|Ireland]]'s capital on the evening of 23 November 2023, hours after a mass stabbing outside a school in the city.[1]

## The stabbing

Five people, including three children, were stabbed outside a Dublin school on a Thursday afternoon; the attacker, reported as an Algerian immigrant, was neutralized after the attack.[1]

## Riots

In response to the horror generated among the Irish public, Dublin locals rioted, rendering parts of the city impassable.[1] Coverage tied the unrest to widespread anger at the Irish government's approach to mass immigration, a sentiment amplified across Europe by displacement from the wars in Palestine and Ukraine.[1]

## McGregor's intervention

Hours before the stabbing, UFC fighter [[conor-mcgregor|Conor McGregor]] had posted a series of messages attacking the government, including a complaint that Ireland's government was proposing to allow non-residents to vote in elections: "Ireland, we are at war. Do not let any irish property be took over unannounced."[1] Following the attack, his remarks gained traction and many called for him to run for office.[1]

## Commentary

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described Dublin as "on fire," noted that media coverage blamed "the far right," and argued the violence illustrated the outcomes of open-borders immigration policy.[1] She contended that United States borders are less secure than Ireland's, that the absence of cultural assimilation — and officials' unwillingness to require it — makes a repeat in America likely, and closed with the observation that the situation is "a tinderbox, and there are way too many bad actors holding matches."[1]


## Aftermath: speech legislation

The riot, which led to the arrest of 34 people and extensive property damage during anti-immigration protests challenging government policy on undocumented migrants, became the stated justification for advancing Ireland's [[criminal-justice-incitement-to-violence-or-hatred-bill|hate speech bill]].[2] Legal scholar [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] argued that anti-free-speech advocates use periods of public panic or anger as opportunities to persuade the public to surrender speech rights to government censors and prosecutors, and described the Irish push as arriving "right on schedule."[2]
