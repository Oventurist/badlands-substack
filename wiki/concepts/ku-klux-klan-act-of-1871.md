# Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871

The Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871, also known as the Civil Rights Act of 1871 or the Enforcement Act, was passed during Reconstruction to provide federal civil remedies against conspiracies to deprive citizens of their rights, including conspiracies to interfere with elections. Its modern conspiracy provisions remain a vehicle for civil suits.

One of the three counts in [[naacp-v-useip|NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.]] was brought under the Act. The essay [[always-fight-back|"Always Fight Back"]] argues that the choice of statute was strategic rather than substantive, quoting the 2015 NYU Social Justice Law Review article "Voters Strike Back": "Another reason to bring a KKK Act claim is for the purpose of political messaging. Liability under the Act carries the additional stigma of conspiracy and its association with the KKK's legacy of politicalized racism," and noting that the Act "creates the opportunity to brand a particular ballot security group or party committee with these deeply embarrassing associations," a threat that "could strengthen the law's deterrent effect and help drive settlement."

Defendant [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] writes that the plaintiffs — the [[naacp|NAACP]], the [[league-of-women-voters|League of Women Voters of Colorado]], and [[mi-familia-vota|Mi Familia Vota]] — did in fact brand the [[useip|USEIP]] defendants with those associations, at lasting personal and professional cost, before the court entered judgment against them in July 2024.
