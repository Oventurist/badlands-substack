# Executive Privilege

**Executive privilege** is a doctrine, not spelled out in the Constitution, that developed to protect a president's ability to obtain candid counsel from advisers without fear of immediate public disclosure and to protect confidential communications relating to official responsibilities.[1] It is a privilege rather than a right, and it has limitations in extraordinary situations — as exemplified during the [[watergate|Watergate]] scandal, when the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruled that it could not be used to shield the release of secret Oval Office tapes sought in a criminal inquiry, and again following the September 11 terrorist attacks.[1] [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] stresses the consequence: the privilege will not protect a president from criminal investigations.[1]

## Statutory overrides

The 2014 amendment to the [[presidential-records-act|Presidential Records Act]] allows a former or incumbent President to block release of a presidential record by notifying the [[archivist-of-the-united-states|Archivist of the United States]] of a claim of constitutionally-based privilege.[1] That claim can be overridden in only two ways: the incumbent President withdraws a decision upholding the claim, or the Archivist is directed to release by a final court order not subject to appeal.[1] An executive order on presidential records reinforces this, providing that the Archivist "shall abide by any instructions given him by the incumbent President or his designee unless otherwise directed by a final court order."[1]

## The January 6 records dispute

President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] agreed to a congressional request for sensitive information on the actions of his predecessor [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and his aides during the January 6 events, over Trump's claim that the information was guarded by executive privilege — a move the Associated Press characterized as risky and liable to come back to haunt Biden and future presidents.[1] Biden's White House counsel wrote to the Archivist that the January 6 events belonged among the extraordinary situations in which privilege yields.[1]

The Supreme Court rejected Trump's bid to use executive privilege to block a House committee from accessing a trove of records created by Trump's White House, leaving in effect a ruling from the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals in which a three-judge panel unanimously concluded that Trump had not met his legal burden and could not assert privilege as a former president because the incumbent had agreed to hand the files over.[1] Trump had sought to block more than 750 pages of records that the [[national-archives-and-records-administration|National Archives]] determined were relevant to the committee's investigation.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Lange reads the outcome as intentional. In his account, Trump deliberately litigated the claim all the way to the Supreme Court in order to establish a precedent that former presidents cannot use executive privilege to conceal their conduct, thereby disarming in advance the defense arguments that would otherwise shield [[george-hw-bush|George H.W. Bush]], [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] and [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] from prosecution.[1] Biden, by acting as the incumbent who withdrew privilege, is said to have set the precedent that a sitting president can make a predecessor's classified documents public — a precedent Lange argues will be turned against past administrations by the next incumbent.[1] He frames the whole sequence in terms of game theory and connects it to Trump's prioritization of reshaping the Supreme Court.[1]
