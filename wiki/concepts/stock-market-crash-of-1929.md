# Stock Market Crash of 1929

The stock market crash of 1929 triggered the Great Depression, and in Joe Lange's account [[citibank|Citibank]] — then [[national-city-bank|National City Bank]] — played a major part in causing it.[1] The bank's chairman [[charles-e-mitchell|Charles E. Mitchell]] pursued incautious securities policies that facilitated the speculation leading to the crash, and Senator [[carter-glass|Carter Glass]] singled him out as more responsible than any fifty other men.[1]

Millions of Americans lost their jobs in the Depression, and one in four lost their life savings after more than 4,000 U.S. banks shut down between 1929 and 1933, leaving depositors with nearly $400 million in losses.[1] The [[pecora-commission|Pecora Commission]] hearings that followed exposed conflicts of interest in which banks lent to companies and then issued stock in the same companies without disclosure, and revealed the packaging of bundled bad loans as securities sold to unsuspecting customers.[1]

Lange presents the crash as the origin of a recurring "template" by which Wall Street banks transfer risk to ordinary Americans, repeated in the [[dot-com-bubble|dotcom bubble]] of 2000 and the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 financial crisis]], and, in his view, forming again around artificial intelligence stocks.[1] The crash led directly to the [[glass-steagall|Glass-Steagall Act]].[1]
