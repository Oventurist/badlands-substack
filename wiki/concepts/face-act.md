# FACE Act

The FACE Act (Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances Act) is a federal statute that, among other protections, shields houses of worship from interference with access and religious exercise.[1]

## Invocation after the Cities Church incident

In January 2026 the statute was invoked by Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights [[harmeet-dhillon|Harmeet Dhillon]] after dozens of left-wing activists stormed [[cities-church|Cities Church]] in St. Paul, Minnesota during a Sunday service, apparently targeting a pastor believed to be linked to the local field office operations of [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|Immigration and Customs Enforcement]].[1] Dhillon stated that the incident could violate both the FACE Act and the [[ku-klux-klan-act|Ku Klux Klan Act]], and emphasized zero tolerance, noting that disrupting religious services is not protected speech and that the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] Civil Rights Division, along with the [[fbi|FBI]], was investigating.[1]

Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] spoke directly with the affected pastor, affirming that attacks on law enforcement and the intimidation of Christians would face the "full force of federal law," while [[white-house|White House]] officials and religious leaders condemned the actions as a desecration of a sacred space.[1]

## Prosecution record and Trump pardons

As of President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s inauguration in January 2021, the federal government had brought fewer than 100 FACE Act prosecutions since the law's inception in 1994.[2] By 2024, the administration had opened 60 cases, only five of which targeted pro-abortion extremists; the other 55 targeted peaceful pro-life demonstrators.[2] In January 2025, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was reported to be preparing presidential pardons to get pro-lifers jailed for peaceful protest "out of the gulags," with pro-life activist [[bevelyn-williams|Bevelyn Williams]] — sentenced to 41 months over a June 2020 protest at a Manhattan abortion facility — among those expected to benefit.[2] Badlands commentator Ashe in America framed the pardons as ending the political persecution of Christian conservatives by the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]], at least as a matter of policy.[2]
