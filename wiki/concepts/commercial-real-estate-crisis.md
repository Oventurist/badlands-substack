# Commercial Real Estate Crisis

The commercial real estate (CRE) crisis refers to the deterioration in U.S. commercial property markets — most acutely the office sector — that came into focus in 2023 as borrowers faced higher debt-service costs, harder refinancing conditions, and roughly $400 billion of CRE debt maturities falling due in a single year.[1]

## Onset in 2023

With stress spreading through the regional banking sector, sentiment in CRE and especially offices turned negative as investors prepared for potential spillover effects.[1] [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan]], [[morgan-stanley|Morgan Stanley]] and [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] all joined what [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] termed the "gloom parade," while high-profile defaults continued to make headlines.[1]

The signature event of April 2023 was a fund belonging to CRE giant [[brookfield|Brookfield]] defaulting on a $161.4 million mortgage for twelve office buildings in Washington, D.C., a loan subsequently transferred to a special servicer to execute a pre-negotiation agreement and determine a path forward.[1]

## Underlying data

Real estate data firm [[green-street|Green Street]] reported that D.C. office space values had slid 36% through March 2023 compared with a year earlier, driven by rising vacancies amid the post-Covid shift to remote and hybrid work.[1] The card-swipe data provided by [[kastle-systems|Kastle Systems]] — described as the gold-standard measure of office occupancy trends — showed average office occupancy across Washington, D.C. at only 43%, still short of pre-pandemic levels.[1]

## Badlands commentary

In the *[[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]]*, [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that amid the many overlapping crises afflicting the United States and the world, "the percolating and threatening to boil financial crisis may be chief among them."[1] He argued that macroeconomic data such as office vacancies could be read as pointing to an impending crash with an "oddly-positive tilt," since many of the investment firms once deemed "Too Big to Fail" appear less well protected this time, while a surge of independent businesses has sprung up around the nation in the wake of what he called the engineered COVID-19 "Plandemic."[1] His conclusion was that the financial road ahead would be rocky but that the nation was "in the midst of a transition from excess to modest, small-scale and private success."[1]

## Empty offices in New York and San Francisco (May 2023)

By May 2023 the scale of vacancy in the largest American cities had become a standard illustration of the crisis: [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] reported that twenty-six Empire State Buildings could fit into New York City's empty office space, with occupancy hovering around 50 percent of pre-pandemic levels according to Harvard economics department chair Edward Glaeser and MIT's Carlo Ratti.[2] The proximate cause was the normalization of working from home across many industries after the pandemic, a shift that also battered mass transit systems in America's largest cities.[2]

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] characterized the emptying of office towers as part of a "controlled implosion of the US economy," arguing that the loss of office buildings signals lost liquidity in margin-thin enterprises whose largest players are "leveraged to the hilt" — a powder-keg situation.[2] He traced the underlying fragility to the [[pujo-committee|Pujo Committee]]-era Money Trust, which in the run-up to the [[federal-reserve-act-of-1913|Federal Reserve Act of 1913]] sought to shift business from saving to borrowing, so that nearly all major businesses now operate on loans and credit extension — "like trying to build a house next to a floodplain."[2]

In this reading, bankers use liberal credit issuance to inflate the boom phase of the business cycle, after which the economy must be shrunk for reasons such as inflation, regulation or a pandemic; the rising interest rates pushed by the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] earlier in 2023 all but guaranteed that some businesses would fail as the music stopped.[2] Deschamps argued that this is the point, since crisis lets the same actors buy up businesses cheaply — citing [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan]]'s acquisition of [[first-republic-bank|First Republic]] — gain political capital for reforms such as [[central-bank-digital-currency|central bank digital currencies]], and profit on both the downside and the recovery.[2] He compared the moment to the [[panic-of-1907|Panic of 1907]], after which the money trust absorbed large portions of the private sector and the military-industrial complex and technocratic reordering of society followed, concluding: "Those who forget history are doomed to repeat it."[2]

## "Ticking time bomb" (September 2024)

Politico reported in September 2024 that four and a half years after the pandemic sent workers home, the office property bill was finally coming due.[3] The office building market, already reeling from higher vacancy rates amid the rise of remote work, had been crushed by high borrowing costs, and although the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] was at last preparing to cut interest rates, it might be too little too late; investors, banks and property owners were beginning to accept that some commercial buildings would never recover their pre-pandemic value, producing a steady drumbeat of distressed sales.[3]

The troubles drew congressional attention, with one New York lawmaker calling the situation a "ticking time bomb" for banks as nearly $1 trillion in commercial real estate loans came due that year.[3] Facing both vacant office buildings and a shortage of millions of homes, a bipartisan group of lawmakers sought to make it easier for developers to convert underused properties into housing.[3] Representative Mike Carey (R-Ohio), who introduced a bill that summer establishing a temporary 20 percent tax credit for qualified property conversion expenditures alongside Representative Jimmy Gomez (D-Calif.), said the change "would absolutely help lenders recoup some of their investment while allowing them to align with the current needs of the market," adding that "the pandemic caused a seismic shift in work patterns" and that "we see these vacant office buildings as a well of untapped potential."[3]

## Record office vacancy rate (July 2024)

Axios reported in July 2024 that office vacancy rates had reached a record 20.1% in the second quarter — the highest since at least 1979, when Moody's began tracking the figure.[4] The outlet noted that vacancy rates typically rise in economic downturns, so it was outside the normal pattern for them to have climbed for several years while the economy remained, in its telling, on solid ground.[4] Offices were said to be sitting empty because the pandemic appeared to have permanently changed how many Americans work, leaving companies needing less space, while long-term leases were taking a long time to unwind, according to Moody's associate economist Nick Luettke.[4] Moody's projected vacancy rates would keep climbing for several years, peaking at the end of 2026, with worse outcomes if the economy entered a downturn; Luettke concluded there was "just going to be pain over the next coming years."[4]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the article said the quiet part out loud, contending that only administration officials and narrative peddlers believed the economic story being told, and that job gains, [[inflation|inflation]] metrics and talking points on housing, energy costs, homelessness and the markets were all manipulated.[4] In that reading, the media's confusion at indicators such as commercial occupancy rates stemmed from changed formulas used to game the metrics, and the underlying explanation was simply that the [[joe-biden|Biden administration]]'s economic narrative was fake.[4]
