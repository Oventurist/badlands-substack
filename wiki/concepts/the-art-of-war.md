# The Art of War

**The Art of War** is the ancient Chinese treatise on strategy attributed to [[sun-tzu|Sun Tzu]], and the subject of a thirteen-part Badlands Media series by [[absolutetruth1776|AbsoluteTruth1776]] begun on February 13, 2023 with an installment subtitled "Present Times and Present Plans."[1] The series takes one chapter of the book per article, with the stated goal of showing readers that the text is "incredibly relevant to current times" — not only because it is [[donald-trump|President Trump]]'s favourite book, but because its tactics and talking points are, in the author's view, apparent on today's multi-dimensional battlefield.[1]

The article describes the book as hailed by military leaders and scholars throughout history, widely accepted as a masterpiece on strategy, and still cited as an influence by many of the world's most successful people, including Donald J. Trump, who is characterised as a lifelong student of the work.[1] The author's central claim is that to understand the book is to understand how Trump has gone about draining the Swamp, a process he holds will lead to the eventual defeat of the [[deep-state|Deep State]].[1]

## Chapter 1 — Laying Plans

The first chapter opens with the proposition that the art of war is of vital importance to the State: a matter of life or death, a road either to safety or to ruin, and hence a subject of inquiry that cannot be neglected.[1] The Badlands commentary glosses this in terms of deterrence, arguing that to secure peace is to prepare for war and that any nation wishing to remain prosperous must maintain a strong military, if only as a deterrent.[1] It observes that throughout history the military machine has been used to secure a nation, to defend it from attack, or to take one over — from the Roman Empire to the operation in [[ukraine|Ukraine]], where the author states his personal belief that [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] is "taking out some trash."[1]

### The five constant factors

Sun Tzu holds that the art of war is governed by five constant factors to be weighed in deliberation: the Moral Law, Heaven, Earth, the Commander, and Method and Discipline.[1]

The **Moral Law** causes the people to be in complete accord with their ruler, so that they will follow him regardless of their lives, undismayed by any danger; the commentary reads this as a principle of harmony akin to the Tao of Lao Tzu in its moral aspect, and quotes Wang Tzu that without constant practice officers will be nervous and undecided and the general wavering and irresolute when the crisis is at hand.[1]

**Heaven** signifies night and day, cold and heat, times and seasons; the commentary takes this as a generalised reference to all surroundings and factors rather than the Biblical Heaven, citing Wang Hsi that "the general economy of Heaven, including the five elements, the four seasons, wind and clouds, and other phenomena must be taken into account when entering a war."[1]

**Earth** comprises distances great and small, danger and security, open ground and narrow passes, and the chances of life and death — knowledge of the terrain being every general's responsibility, the neglect of which almost always leads to defeat.[1]

The **Commander** stands for the virtues of wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage and strictness; the commentary argues that a leader lacking any of these has his odds of victory diminished by one count for each quality absent.[1]

**Method and Discipline** covers the marshalling of the army in its proper subdivisions, the graduations of rank among officers, the maintenance of supply roads, and the control of military expenditure — one cannot merely assemble an army and order it to fight, but must project all facets of the coming conflict.[1]

### The seven considerations

Sun Tzu poses seven comparative questions — which sovereign is imbued with the Moral Law, which general has most ability, with whom lie the advantages of Heaven and Earth, on which side discipline is most rigorously enforced, which army is stronger, on which side officers and men are more highly trained, and in which army there is greater constancy in reward and punishment — and states that by means of these he can forecast victory or defeat.[1] The commentary reads "which army is stronger" as a question of mental as much as physical strength, and stresses that while discipline and punishment are necessary, so too are rewards: merit must be properly rewarded and misdeeds properly punished.[1]

The chapter adds that the general who heeds this counsel should be retained in command and the one who does not should be dismissed, while urging commanders to avail themselves of helpful circumstances beyond the ordinary rules and to modify plans as circumstances become favourable.[1] The commentary glosses this as remaining fluid and open-minded, since no situation in warfare remains static, and cites Chang Yu's caution against pinning one's faith to abstract principles.[1] It illustrates the point with the anecdote of Lord Uxbridge on the eve of the Battle of Waterloo asking the Duke of Wellington for his plans, and Wellington replying that since Bonaparte would attack first and had disclosed nothing of his projects, his own plans must depend on them.[1]

### "All warfare is based on deception"

The commentary calls the maxim that all warfare is based on deception the most simple, direct and accurate passage in the entire manual, holding that war begins, is fought, and ends under conditions of deception — psychological warfare included.[1] It offers as a prime example [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s seeming support of the [[covid-19-vaccines|vaccines]], arguing that on its face the move appears questionable but at a deeper level deprived the enemy of a valuable weapon and may have averted a far worse fate for Americans.[1]

The associated precepts — appear unable when able to attack, inactive when using one's forces, far away when near and near when far; hold out baits to entice the enemy; feign disorder and crush him; prepare for the enemy secure at all points and evade him when he is in superior strength; irritate an opponent of choleric temper and pretend weakness that he may grow arrogant — are read as an argument for misdirection and for never forecasting one's moves.[1] Wang Tzu's line that the good tactician "plays with his adversary as a cat plays with a mouse, first feigning weakness and immobility, and then suddenly pouncing upon him" is applied directly to Trump's habit of trolling and baiting his enemies, which the author treats as grounds for taking his statement "We caught them all" literally.[1]

Sun Tzu's instruction to give the enemy no rest when he takes his ease and to separate his forces when united is applied to [[congress|Congress]]'s actions against the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration and to the [[twitter-files|Twitter Files]], both framed as attacks launched by patriots at a moment when the administration had settled in and grown comfortable.[1] Division is described as a primary tactic of any aggressor, visible within the movement itself through infiltrators seeking to split it.[1] The precepts to attack where the enemy is unprepared and appear where one is not expected, and never to divulge these devices beforehand, are illustrated by Trump's improbable run to the Presidency, which the Deep State did not expect him to make, much less win; the author adds his recurring maxim that "operators never divulge" and that secrecy is paramount to any military plan.[1]

### Calculations

The chapter's closing passage — that the general who wins makes many calculations in his temple before the battle while the loser makes few — is glossed with the note that in ancient times temples served as operations centres for military leaders, and read as a call for constant assessment of the battlefield and the enemy, since due diligence, hard work and repetition breed success while resting on one's laurels invites defeat.[1]

## Reception and framing

The installment concludes that as the series unfolds it will become clearer how the book has served as a guideline for patriots in what the author insists is a genuine war, and that everything being witnessed has been calculated by Trump and his team as a long-term dismantling of the enemy.[1] The author frames the study as practical: understanding the text will help readers grasp both why "things are taking so long" and how they can best play a role.[1] The article carries [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]]'s standard contributor disclaimer and directs readers to the author's own Substack, Beyond the Veil.[1]
