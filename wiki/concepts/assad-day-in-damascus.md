# Assad Day in Damascus

"Assad Day in Damascus," subtitled "Controversy and Intrigue Swirl Amidst Assad's Fall," is a Badlands Media article published on December 12, 2024 and written by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]]. Written days after the collapse of the Syrian government, the piece argues that Western corporate media coverage of the fall of [[bashar-al-assad|Bashar al-Assad]] systematically avoids the questions that matter most: why the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] and the [[cia|CIA]] have funded militant proxy groups in [[syria|Syria]], whether all Syrians in fact welcomed the change, how [[al-qaeda]] became "hip and chic," and whether the timing of the offensive was a coincidence or a convenience for what the author calls the Greater Israel Project. The article proceeds in three movements: a factual recap of the offensive, a critique of Western reporting, and speculation about who benefits.

## The offensive and the collapse

According to the article, an unexpected offensive by rebel forces led by [[hayat-tahrir-al-sham]] (HTS) and its allies produced the dramatic collapse of Assad's government. The rebels launched their offensive on November 27, 2024, rapidly capturing Aleppo, Syria's second-largest city, before moving south toward Damascus. By December 8, 2024 they had taken Hama, Homs and finally Damascus, leading to Assad's reported resignation and departure from Syria to Moscow.

The source attributes the speed of the advance to the withdrawal of Syrian government forces, possibly caused by the absence of support from allies such as [[russia]] and [[iran]], both preoccupied with other conflicts. The article notes that the fall of the regime raises open questions about future governance and stability given HTS's links to al-Qaeda, and about the management of the power transition among Syria's competing factions — Kurds, Turkish-backed groups, and other opposition forces.

International reactions are summarized: [[turkey]] denied supporting HTS despite aiding some rebel factions, while Russia called for United Nations Security Council consultations to manage the fallout, particularly concerning the buffer zone near the Golan Heights. The article cites United Nations reporting that the conflict has displaced millions and remains a humanitarian crisis with significant need for assistance. It also observes that the shift in power has implications for regional security — for Iran's influence exerted through [[hezbollah]] and Syrian territory, and for [[israel|Israel]], which has been targeting Iranian assets in Syria.

## Who is Abu Mohammad al-Jolani?

The article argues that Western coverage of HTS's rise is predictable once viewed from a particular vantage point, one requiring knowledge of the CIA's history with al-Qaeda and [[isis]], of the American mythos of "Pax Americana", and of the Project for a New American Century (PNAC).

PNAC is described as a neoconservative think tank with strong ties to the American Enterprise Institute, created in 1997 by william kristol and robert kagan — the latter identified as [[victoria-nuland|Victoria Nuland]]'s husband — and populated by figures including dick cheney, paul wolfowitz, john bolton and donald rumsfeld. Of the 25 original signatories, the article states, 14 received positions in the administration of George W. Bush. Its vision of a New American Century, according to the source, entailed the takeover of countries including Libya, Iraq, Iran and Syria in the name of democracy.

The article quotes the PNAC report Rebuilding America's Defenses: "The American peace has proven itself peaceful, stable, and durable. Yet no moment in international politics can be frozen in time: even a global Pax Americana will not preserve itself." It notes the report's conclusion that the global order "must have a secure foundation on unquestioned U.S. military preeminence," and highlights the passage observing that "the process of transformation is likely to be a long one, absent some catastrophic and catalyzing event — like a new Pearl Harbor." Since the report predates September 11, 2001, the author invites readers to "draw your own conclusions."

The piece also recounts General Wesley K. Clark's 2007 admission regarding a Pentagon memo he saw after 9/11 outlining a plan to "take out seven countries in five years, starting with Iraq and then Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Somalia, Sudan, and finishing off with Iran." Going further back, it describes the CIA's construction of the [[mujahideen]] — characterized as the precursor to al-Qaeda — to fight the Soviets at the close of the Cold War, presented as an example of a newer era of warfare in which localized proxy groups replace direct deployment of U.S. troops.

From this the article asks whether the rebels chasing Assad from power are genuine, or whether this is "yet another cookie-cutter regime change operation using proxy armies and facilitated by the two-headed dragon of the CIA and [[state-department|State Department]]." Its answer is that HTS appears to be a rebranded al-Qaeda with "woke" trappings designed to be palatable to Western audiences — the same audiences, the author writes, who cheered the Ghost of Kyiv and the Azov Battalion.

The article notes that [[abu-mohammad-al-jolani|Abu Mohammad al-Jolani]], now portrayed as a "blazer-wearing revolutionary" rather than a radical jihadist, spent five years in Abu Ghraib and other prisons and remains on a State Department wanted list as a "Specially Designated Global Terrorist" carrying a reward of up to $10 million.

## CIA and Pentagon programs in Syria

The source treats U.S. support for Syrian proxy groups as public knowledge, citing a 2017 headline. The CIA, through programs such as timber sycamore, armed and trained groups aimed at pressuring the Assad government, while the Pentagon's efforts focused more on fighting ISIS, particularly through support for the predominantly Kurdish syrian democratic forces. The termination of the CIA program in 2017 under President [[donald-trump]] is described as a shift in U.S. policy toward explicit counter-terrorism rather than regime change.

The article rejects the explanation that the United States is involved merely because Assad is a dictator, noting that Washington remains "quite cozy" with dictators in Egypt and Saudi Arabia — the difference being that "those are *our* dictators."

## Critique of Western reporting

The second half of the article argues that the U.S. security state has turned the media into a psychological conditioning engine, tracing the lineage from the CIA's "Mighty Wurlitzer" and [[operation-mockingbird]] through the country's emergence as "police of the world." The same tactics used at the outset of wars with Iraq, Iran, Libya and Egypt — and, going back to the 1970s, North Vietnam — are said to be at play again.

Ukraine is offered as the central illustration. The article contends the conflict could not have occurred without hundreds of billions of U.S. tax dollars, much of it landing in the pockets of corrupt bureaucrats and military officials, and that the war was on the verge of resolution in April 2022 before being thwarted by the Western hegemon via Boris Johnson. Public support, and the flow of money [[congress|Congress]] rushed out the door, depended on shaping consensus perception through images of bombing and grieving grandmothers and repetition of the claims that the invasion was unprovoked and that [[vladimir-putin|Putin]] is the "new Hitler." The key omitted context, according to the source, is that the State Department and its NGO network orchestrated a color revolution and regime change in 2014, installing a Western-sympathetic leader, prohibiting the teaching of Russian language and mythology in schools, ignoring that large portions of the country prefer rule from Moscow, and presiding over the slaughter of hundreds of thousands of fighting-age men — outcomes the article says could have been prevented but for the interference of Western leaders such as [[joe-biden]] and Boris Johnson.

The same template, the article argues, was applied to Saddam Hussein, [[muammar-gaddafi|Muammar Gaddafi]], the Taliban, Putin and now Assad: leaders who go from collaborator to uniquely evil, with the population suffering in the power vacuum that follows.

Once Assad fled to Moscow, the article says, corporate media "dusted off the 'overthrown tyrant' template." It quotes at length from [[new-york-times|The New York Times]], which described five decades of silence and fear under the al-Assad family, celebratory gunfire crackling around Damascus, crowds shouting in the squares, and thirteen years of bloodshed since those opposed to Assad "hoped to follow revolutions in Tunisia, Egypt and Libya by overthrowing their own autocratic leader."

The article's objection is that this framing presents Syrians as unanimously ecstatic. It acknowledges that many Syrians were happy to see relatives released from prison, but points to religious strongholds that hold Assad in high regard — particularly the Alawites, from whom Assad hails, along with Christians and other minorities historically wary of Sunni Islamist opposition and fearful of persecution under different rule. Many of these groups, it notes, are withdrawing support or adapting to the new political landscape. The author also singles out the Times' invocation of "Tunisia, Egypt and Libya," all countries the United States "helped" and all of which received identical media and presidential treatment.

Presidential rhetoric is treated as interchangeable: Joe Biden "shambled out from his crypt to read a prepared message from a teleprompter," in a narrative nearly identical to statements following the defeat of the Taliban in Afghanistan, the Vietcong in South Vietnam, Saddam Hussein in Iraq and Gaddafi in Libya — "We vanquished the tyrant, the people are finally free, we stand with them." British Prime Minister keir starmer offered similar remarks while abroad in Dubai meeting with the leaders of the United Arab Emirates, prompting the author's remark that Starmer is "very pleased" that al-Qaeda is running Syria.

## Tulsi Gabbard

The article presents Tulsi Gabbard as a case study in how dissenters are slandered as Russian agents. Gabbard met with Assad in January 2017 during what she described as a "fact-finding" mission, becoming the first member of the U.S. Congress to meet with Assad since the Syrian civil war began in 2011. She then, in the article's framing, committed an unforgivable sin by calling for the United States to stop arming and funding proxy groups in Syria. The Democratic establishment immediately distanced itself, and in the wake of her selection as Trump's nominee for Director of National Intelligence the story was revived to suggest she is a Putin puppet — described as the official slander for anyone deviating from the security state's multi-decade project of making Russia a permanent enemy.

## Conclusion

The article closes by hoping that as the Biden administration transfers power to "Team Trump," the United States stays out of the situation and avoids another aid package filling rebel pockets and bolstering forces for a future conflict. Trump's approach, "often referred to—derogatorily—as 'noninterventionist,'" is called the ideal disposition, with the author arguing that one reason for Trump's November victory was that Americans are tired of funding conflicts in places most could not locate on a map. Who benefits most from the timing of events remains uncertain, the author concludes: "My money is not on that being the Syrian people."

The piece carries the standard [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] disclaimer that articles represent the opinions of contributing authors, and directs readers to DeLarme's other work at the Post-Liberal.
