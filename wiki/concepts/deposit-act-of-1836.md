# Deposit Act of 1836

The Deposit Act of 1836 ordered the distribution of the federal budget surplus into various state banks throughout the country.[1] It was enacted during the presidency of [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]], after his dissolution of the [[second-bank-of-the-united-states|Second Bank of the United States]] and his payoff of the entire national debt in 1835 had left the U.S. Treasury with a surplus and no public debt.[1]

Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] emphasizes that the geographical orientation of the transfers was the key feature of the act.[1] Importers and exporters in New York City normally required hard money to settle foreign balances, but the Deposit Act moved precious metals from the coast to the interior of the country.[1] Monetary reserves in New York City deposit banks fell from $7.2 million in September 1836 to $1.5 million in May 1837.[1] Because the surplus was distributed in gold and silver rather than fiat money, the act removed the concentration of wealth in New York and dispersed it to western and southern states.[1]

Lange notes that wealthy bankers did not approve, and that in New York — where [[citibank|Citibank]] was located — deposit reserves dropped by almost $6 million.[1] Together with the [[specie-circular|Specie Circular]], the act drained specie from eastern and British-linked financial channels and is treated in his account as a principal cause of the [[panic-of-1837|Panic of 1837]].[1] Deposits under the act were halted during that panic.[1]
