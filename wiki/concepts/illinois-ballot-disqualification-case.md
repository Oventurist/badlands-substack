# Illinois Ballot Disqualification Case

The **Illinois ballot disqualification case** was a 2024 state-court challenge seeking to remove former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the Illinois Republican presidential primary ballot over his role in the attack on the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021.[1]

## Cook County ruling

A Cook County judge, Tracie Porter, ruled that Trump be kicked off Republican primary ballots in the state.[1] Porter held that the Illinois State Board of Elections had been "clearly erroneous" in January when it allowed Trump to remain on the ballot.[1] She ruled that when Trump filed a statement of candidacy for the Illinois Republican primary on January 4, he "falsely swore" that he was "legally qualified" to run for president again, because the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] had already ruled that he "had been found to engage in insurrection" in a similar challenge to his eligibility.[1]

## Appeal

On Thursday, February 29, 2024, Trump appealed the ruling, with his attorneys asking the Illinois Appellate Court to overturn Porter's decision.[1] Trump's name remained on ballots for the March 19 primary while the appeal was pursued.[1]

## Clarification of the stay

Judge [[tracie-porter|Tracie Porter]] had initially stayed her removal order only until March 1, 2024, anticipating an appeal to the Illinois Appellate Court, First District, or the Illinois Supreme Court, and/or a ruling by the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] in the parallel case from [[colorado]].[2] The language of that stay was deemed vague, prompting Trump's attorneys to seek clarification on Thursday and to request a ruling by 12 p.m., failing which they would file an emergency motion to stay the ruling in the appeals court.[2]

In response to the notice of appeal, Porter modified her original stay on Thursday, so that the removal order was stayed until the appeal is "finally resolved by the Illinois Appellate Court, First District, the Illinois Supreme Court, and/or the U.S. Supreme Court."[2] The practical effect was that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] would remain on the Illinois primary ballot pending appeal, and that any votes cast for him would not be voided in the interim.[2]

## Later Badlands commentary

In a March 4, 2024 brief, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the removal campaign as "clearly election interference, despite the regime referring to claims of election interference as 'right wing disinformation,'" and reported having argued the point with a local leftist editor who insisted the critical legal theorists behind the cases were correct and that all nine justices were misreading the Constitution.[2] She noted that while many removal efforts were pending, the [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|Colorado challenge]] had gone further than any other, that the Colorado primary fell on March 5 and the Illinois primary on March 19, and that a decision for Trump in the Colorado case would effectively render the remaining challenges moot.[2] She predicted the removal efforts would fail and that by November 5 the electorate would fully grasp the level of election interference in which "trusted institutions" had engaged.[2]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the state election board had voted not to remove Trump from the ballot but that "a county judge wants her 15 minutes, so she decided she knows best."[1] She noted that the case joined many other challenges awaiting the outcome of Trump v. Anderson, the [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|Colorado 14th Amendment case]], before the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]].[1]

Ashe noted that the Colorado primary fell on Tuesday, March 5, and that the Supreme Court would release another order list on Monday, March 4, expressing hope that the justices would decide before Republican voters cast primary ballots.[1] She added that Colorado's RNC delegates would be awarded in April at the party Assembly, giving the court room to delay until then, but warned that "the longer the uncertainty of this charade, the greater the harm to the general peace, and the worse the impact to the public trust."[1]
