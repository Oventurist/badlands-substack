# 2025 Federal Judiciary CM/ECF Breach

The 2025 CM/ECF breach was a sweeping compromise of the United States federal judiciary's electronic case filing system, disclosed in August 2025, that officials feared had exposed sealed court data to Latin American drug cartels.[1]

## Scope

At least a dozen district courts across several U.S. states were believed to have been directly affected by the ongoing compromise of the case filing system known as CM/ECF, in what was described as one of the most serious hacks of the federal court filing system in years.[1] Judicial officials and investigators worried that stolen data could be weaponized to identify cooperating witnesses in prosecutions against cartels, or to reveal impending and ongoing investigations such as sealed wiretap orders and arrest and search warrants.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described PACER, the public-facing side of the system, as "ancient technology" — "duct tape and popsicle sticks" — and said she was surprised it had not been hacked earlier or proven inadequate for holding sealed records such as witness names.[1] She noted that many criminal elements would want such records but that cartels were likely near the top of the list, and raised the possibility that the intrusion was "a hack or a trap."[1] She argued the national legal records system should be more secure and modern, since it constitutes the literal official legal record of the nation, even if the issue is unglamorous for most Americans.[1]

## Attribution to Russia and Badlands response

In August 2025, investigators were reported to have uncovered evidence that [[russia|Russia]] was "at least partly responsible" for the hack of the federal court document management system, which holds sensitive records capable of revealing confidential sources and defendants charged with national security crimes.[2] Reporting indicated it was unclear whether an arm of Russian intelligence was behind the intrusion or whether other countries participated, with people familiar describing a yearslong effort to infiltrate the system; some searches touched midlevel criminal cases in the New York City area and other jurisdictions, including matters involving people with Russian and Eastern European surnames.[2] The disclosure surfaced days before President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was scheduled to meet [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] in Alaska to discuss ending the war in Ukraine.[2]

Badlands hosts treated the story as an information operation timed to disrupt the summit. Chris Paul compared its hedged sourcing to the "51 intelligence officials" letter on the Hunter Biden laptop.[2] Ashe in America emphasized the qualifier "at least partly responsible" and questioned whether the government could forensically examine such a breach at all, describing PACER as an aging, bug-ridden patchwork of local solutions that an ordinary teenager could compromise, and arguing the coverage served to undermine US foreign policy and prolong the war.[2]
