# North American Water and Power Alliance

The North American Water and Power Alliance (NAWAPA) was a proposed continental water management and power generation scheme described in Badlands Media commentary as one of the "great projects" that "once animated the best of western nation building."[1]

## Abandonment

The source attributes the death of the NAWAPA paradigm to the assassinations of John F. Kennedy and Robert F. Kennedy more than fifty years ago, which it says ended Kennedy's comprehensive continental water strategy.[1] The consequence, in this telling, was several generations of California politicians who placed "saving desert ecosystems" ahead of saving people, producing chronic drought, a collapse in water infrastructure investment, and one of the largest food crises in US history even before COVID-era supply chain disruption.[1]

No new hydroelectric dams have been built in North America since the early 1980s, and California energy policy has directed roughly $500 million toward the removal of four dams while depriving farmers and residents of water reserves and power.[1]

## Comparison

NAWAPA is presented as the western counterpart to China's [[south-north-water-transfer-project|South-North Water Transfer Project]], a $64–76 billion diversion begun in 2001 that will move 44.8 billion cubic meters of water per year from the Yangtze basin to the north.[1] The contrast between a completed Chinese megaproject and an abandoned American one is offered as the clearest physical evidence of two opposing definitions of "sustainability."[1]

## Origins and design

NAWAPA emerged in the early 1960s, sponsored by the [[parsons-corporation|Parsons company]] and endorsed by leading politicians of both parties, including [[robert-f-kennedy|Robert Kennedy]].[2] The concept became federal legislation by 1965 and won the support of numerous Canadian and American engineers and statesmen, who fought for it for several years before the project drifted into obscurity under the fog of the Vietnam War — a history recounted in the 2012 film *NAWAPA 1964*.[2] Water management works built in British Columbia under Premier [[wac-bennett|W.A.C. Bennett]] are described as having set the stage for the NAWAPA concept.[2]

Under the 1964 Parsons feasibility studies, the continental project would have involved 95 dams, 8 lifts, 39 major tunnels and 4,515 miles (7,266 km) of canals, collecting and moving between 10 and 20 percent of the fresh water runoff of northern Alaska and Canada into the southwestern United States and northern Mexico.[2] A second circuit would take 19 million acre-feet of water per year from the Peace River dam in northern British Columbia across the Canadian prairies into Lake Superior, both flushing and replenishing the depleted and polluted lakes.[2] Part of that Canadian circuit would serve as a barge canal and generate over 35 GW of hydroelectric power, opening the under-developed mid-Canada development corridor with new rail lines, roads and cities linking Hudson Bay and James Bay to the Great Lakes, while adding flood control for Alberta and the prairies.[2]

In the collection phase on the Susitna, Copper, Yukon and Taku rivers, water would be pumped 300 feet up into a reservoir 2,400 feet above sea level at Stikine; from there 70 million acre-feet per year would move into the [[rocky-mountain-trench|Rocky Mountain Trench]], a naturally occurring reservoir 500 miles long with a capacity of 650 million acre-feet, of which 100 million acre-feet could be released annually to the parched south.[2] The water would then be lifted by the Sawtooth lifts in Idaho and Oregon and carried through canals and tunnels — tunnels being used to avoid evaporation — across Utah, Nevada, California, Arizona, Texas and northern Mexico.[2] Proponents projected that farmland would double from 21 million to 44.3 million acres, that aquifers such as the [[ogallala-aquifer|Ogallala]] would be replenished, and that desertification would be reversed.[2] A modernized version of the program was produced by EIR in 2010.[2]

## Political context

The article situates NAWAPA within the water-development policy of [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]], who reversed Eisenhower's "No New Builds" agenda and backed accelerated federal funding for water, hydroelectric and irrigation projects.[2] Kennedy's death is said to have derailed many of the projects he and close collaborators such as Senator [[frank-moss|Frank Moss]] and Robert Kennedy were advancing, as a Malthusian anti-development paradigm took hold in national policy.[2] Writing in December 2024, [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presented a revival of NAWAPA as the non-zero-sum answer to the looming North American water wars.[2]
