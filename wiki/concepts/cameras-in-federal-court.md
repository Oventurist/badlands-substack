# Cameras in Federal Court

**Cameras in federal court** refers to the long-standing prohibition on broadcasting proceedings in United States federal courtrooms, and to the debate over whether that prohibition should be relaxed for the federal criminal trials of former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]].[1]

## The Trump trials question

Trump was charged by the [[justice-department|Justice Department]] in two federal cases: one arising from his handling of classified documents after leaving the [[white-house|White House]], and the other over his efforts to remain in office following his loss in the 2020 election, including his actions leading to the events of January 6, 2021.[1] Trump pleaded not guilty to all charges and both cases were considered likely to reach trial.[1]

Reporting in August 2023 framed *United States of America v. Donald J. Trump* as almost certainly the most-watched criminal trial in American history — if it were televised.[1] Because cameras are prohibited in federal court, however, no broadcast was possible absent a change in the rules, and a coalition including House Democrats, constitutional law experts, and even one of Trump's own attorneys pressed for that change.[1]

[[john-lauro|John Lauro]], the attorney representing Trump in the January 6 case, told "Fox News Sunday" that he would support a televised trial, saying "I would love to see that" and adding, "I'm shocked, actually, that all the networks haven't lined up and filed pleadings already objecting to this."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] noted the long-held expectation within the [[truth-community|Truth Community]] that "the Revolution would not be televised," but suggested the opposite was coming to pass.[1] He observed that anons had been seeded with the concept of televised trials since 2017, when most anticipated seeing [[deep-state|Deep State]] figures on legal parade rather than Trump himself.[1]

Burning Bright argued that Trump was being used as a "cognitive keystone" in more ways than originally understood — setting not only legal precedent but mass psychological precedent as well — and characterized the prospect as the "Trial of the Century."[1]
