# Socialism

Socialism is the political and economic doctrine central to the Badlands Media essay "Glimpses into Hell — Part 1," which uses [[cuba|Cuba]] as its principal case study.[1]

## The appeal and the critique

The essay concedes that the emotional appeal of socialism, [[communism|communism]], and the broader progressive left is understandable, since these systems "appear to draw their fundamental motivations from a place of compassion."[1] Young Americans, it argues, see socialism as a chic new system promising equality and fairness — admirable aims, but attached to an argument more than a century old.[1] Author [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] writes that whether the model is the rigid Marxism-Leninism of the Soviet Union or the contemporary conditions in Venezuela, Cuba, Nicaragua, Zimbabwe, and North Korea, the idea "has never delivered equality, more opportunity, or better lives."[1]

DeLarme locates the failure not in the desire to provide for basic needs but in human nature: he argues that the roughly three to five percent of the population exhibiting sociopathic tendencies has long exploited the compassion of ordinary people, so that "all the good intentions in the world will equate to nothing when there are madmen at the wheel."[1] He also faults American education for leaving young people, himself included, largely ignorant of the history of the left — an ignorance he suggests feels "eerily by design."[1]

## Socialism versus crony capitalism

The essay acknowledges that disillusionment with the existing Western order is rational, since businesses often thrive not through free enterprise but through collusion between the business class and the political class — a condition it names [[crony-capitalism|crony capitalism]].[1] Nonetheless it maintains that residents of the first world remain free to denounce their system, a liberty absent under socialist governments.[1]

## American advocates

The article names [[bernie-sanders|Bernie Sanders]] and [[alexandria-ocasio-cortez|Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez]] as "pop-culture politicians" who hail Cuba as a highly successful example of the "socialist experiment," citing its healthcare system and literacy program.[1] It counters that the healthcare system is among the grievances of Cuban protesters, and that, according to [[antonio-garcia-martinez|Antonio Garcia-Martinez]], Cuba's literacy rate was already high before the revolution.[1] The essay closes by arguing that the Cuban uprising is a microcosm of American politics, with socialism "covertly wrapping its slimy tendrils around what's left of the foundations of our constitutional republic."[1]
