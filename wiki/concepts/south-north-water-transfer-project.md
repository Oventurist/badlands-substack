# South-North Water Transfer Project

The South-North Water Transfer Project — rendered in the source as the "Move South Water North Project" — is a Chinese water diversion megaproject described as the largest such programme ever devised in human history, with a cost given variously as $76 billion and $64 billion and construction beginning in 2001.[1]

## Purpose and scale

The project moves water from the flood-prone, low-population-density headwaters of the Yangtze River in the south to the heavily populated, drought-prone industrial zones of the north and the Yellow River basin.[1] Its design target is the transfer of 44.8 billion cubic meters — about 1.5 trillion cubic feet — of water per year.[1]

## The three routes

The Eastern Route, the first to begin construction, upgraded the Grand Canal to carry 12.6 billion cubic meters per year from the Yangtze through massive tunnels — used to avoid evaporation losses — across 760 miles to Tianjin, passing 23 pumping stations and generating 45.4 MW of power along the way; it was completed in 2013.[1]

The Central Route, built between 2004 and 2014, diverts 9.5 billion cubic meters per year of surplus water from the Danjiangkou Reservoir on the Han River to Beijing, 765 miles away, and required two tunnels carrying the flow 65 meters beneath the Yellow River.[1]

The Western Route, not scheduled for completion until 2050, will bring water from the Yangtze and its tributaries to the Yellow River, passing through the Tibetan Plateaus.[1]

## Assessment

Critics of the project focus on the roughly 330,000 people displaced by its construction; the source argues that the resulting flood control, expanded irrigation and water availability for urban and industrial use "will save countless lives over the coming centuries."[1]

## Contrast with the West

The article uses the project as the central illustration of China's "anti-zero-sum paradigm" of sustainability, contrasting it with California, where years of drought and an absence of new water infrastructure produced one of the largest food crises in US history.[1] It notes that no new hydroelectric dams have been built in North America since the early 1980s, while California has directed $500 million toward removing four dams to "liberate the rivers and restore the natural deserts."[1] The [[north-american-water-and-power-alliance|North American Water and Power Alliance]], the continental analogue to China's programme, was abandoned after the assassinations of John F. Kennedy and Robert F. Kennedy.[1]

China has additionally built four of the ten largest hydroelectric dams in the world over the past 25 years, and on 14 June 2022 Power China unveiled plans for 200 new pumped hydro stations generating 270 GW by 2025.[1] The source cites a NASA finding that global biomass has increased by over 5%, driven in large measure by Indian and Chinese economic activity, as evidence that growth and ecological health are not in conflict.[1]
