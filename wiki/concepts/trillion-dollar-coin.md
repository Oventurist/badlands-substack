# Trillion-Dollar Coin

The "trillion-dollar coin" is a proposed monetary maneuver under which the United States Treasury would exercise its statutory authority to mint platinum coinage by striking a single coin, arbitrarily denominated at one trillion dollars, and depositing it in the Treasury's account at the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] in order to fund government operations without issuing new debt and thereby avoid breaching the [[debt-ceiling|debt ceiling]].[1] Badlands Media contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] treated the idea as an absurdity that had been recycled by establishment economists across more than a decade, framing her coverage around a single Twitter plea: "Make it make sense."[1]

## Origins and the 2013 revival

The concept did not originate in mainstream commentary. It began on finance blogs in the summer of 2011 as the suggestion that Treasury Secretary Timothy Geithner should mint a trillion-dollar platinum coin to avert the debt ceiling, and by January 2013 it had been taken up widely enough that [[business-insider|Business Insider]] declared the "arcane idea" to be "seriously taking off."[1] A flurry of articles in 2013 pushed the theory into general circulation.[1]

An earlier December 2012 piece in the same outlet laid out the mechanism plainly — that the Treasury is allowed to unilaterally mint platinum coins, could create a $1 trillion coin, and could deposit it at its account at the Federal Reserve — and endorsed the plan on the grounds that it was "even crazier to think that the U.S. would default arbitrarily just because of a dumb law."[1]

Krugman's professional peers were markedly less enthusiastic at the time. Millan Mulraine, an analyst at TD Securities, described the coin in 2013 as a "plausible alternative to a default" that would be "akin to debt monetization," but assigned it a probability of only 5 percent.[1] Ashe in America argued that what was most striking about the 2013 coverage was that financial journalists did not ask basic questions about how the scheme would actually work, treating it as an early instance of a durable "trust the experts" reflex in the press.[1]

## The 2023 revival by Paul Krugman

In May 2023, with another debt ceiling standoff underway, Nobel laureate [[paul-krugman|Paul Krugman]] revived the proposal and added the claim that minting the coin would not be inflationary.[1] The account [[wall-street-silver|Wall Street Silver]] mocked the argument on Twitter: "They gave this guy a Nobel Prize ... We can just mint six of the $1 trillion coins and pay all of the govt bills for a year. No taxes needed. It's magic."[1] Ashe in America noted that the idea was "recirculating with mockingbird precision," invoking [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]] as a frame for the synchronized media push.[1]

## The metal-supply problem

Attempting to take the proposal literally exposes its physical impossibility. In the article's exchange, the Twitter account BTC Citadel XXl invoked [[greshams-law|Gresham's Law]] to argue that a coin of genuine intrinsic value would be "good money that's paying bad debt," while conceding it amounted to "a small band aid on a wound with fatal bleeding."[1]

Using palladium — a rare silvery-white metal, chemical symbol Pd, atomic number 46, then trading near $1,427 per ounce — a coin actually containing a trillion dollars of metal would require roughly 700,770,847.933 ounces and cover about 3,743,434 square feet, or 86 acres at the thickness of an ordinary coin.[1] Global palladium supply was forecast at about 7.12 million ounces in 2022 and totaled roughly seven million ounces in 2021, meaning the coin would consume at least one hundred times the world's annual supply.[1]

Platinum, the metal actually specified in the underlying statute and in most coverage, is worse. At about $1,052.01 per ounce, a trillion dollars of platinum would require 950,561,306.451 ounces, against a preliminary 2022 global supply of roughly 5.2 million ounces — some 183 times the world's yearly output for a single coin.[1]

## "The Treasury gets to decide"

The physical constraint is, in the theory's own terms, irrelevant. A 2013 discussion in Quartz stated that "a coin can be any size," that "the Treasury gets to decide a coin's worth," and that "the value of the metal that comprises the coin doesn't have anything to do with its value in currency."[1] The plan therefore reduces to minting ordinary-sized platinum coins and declaring each to be worth one trillion dollars — a declaration of value out of thin air, which Krugman maintained would not be inflationary.[1] Ashe in America connected this directly to [[currency-debasement|currency debasement]], observing that because modern currency is "backed by nothing but hopes and dreams," debasement now simply means printing money and diluting the purchasing power of every existing dollar.[1]

## Official rejection and the Federal Reserve's discretion

In January 2023, Treasury Secretary [[janet-yellen|Janet Yellen]] dismissed the trillion-dollar coin as a "gimmick," telling the Wall Street Journal that "it truly is not by any means to be taken as a given that the Fed would do it," that "the Fed is not required to accept it," and that "it's up to them what to do."[1] Ashe in America argued that Yellen's dismissal was largely beside the point, since the decision to accept the coin and monetize it rests not with the elected government but with the Federal Reserve — evidence, in her framing, that the United States is "hostage to the private central banks."[1] She closed by noting that the [[nobel-prize-in-economic-sciences|Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences]] whose prestige underwrites Krugman's argument was itself established in 1968 by Sweden's central bank, adding that Alfred Nobel "is rolling in his grave."[1]
