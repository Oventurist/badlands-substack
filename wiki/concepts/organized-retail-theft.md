# Organized Retail Theft

Organized retail theft refers to the surge in shoplifting and coordinated merchandise theft that has reshaped American retailing, prompting stores to lock ordinary goods behind Plexiglas and, in some cases, to close locations entirely.[1]

## The phenomenon

An October 2024 account in [[the-atlantic|The Atlantic]], excerpted by Badlands Media, described a Target store in Washington, D.C. where 21 aisles of goods — including toothpaste, body wash, underwear, earbuds and air fresheners — were secured behind plastic, requiring shoppers to press a call button and wait for a clerk.[1] The writer recounted waiting six minutes alongside two other customers before all three abandoned their purchases, calling them "three paying customers sacrificed to the War on Shoplifting."[1]

Most shoplifting is petty by definition — candy bars, baby formula, lipstick — but the aggregate losses run to billions of dollars, and viral videos have documented shelves of deodorant or cold medicine swept into bags by thieves who do not bother to run.[1] While some observers contend the alarm reflects mainly a race-tinged social panic, the piece argued that retailers are not locking up goods, irritating customers and shuttering stores over a few videos, but because merchandise is genuinely walking out the door.[1] Big corporate retailers, mom-and-pop shops, police, prosecutors and lawmakers have tried a range of responses — tough enforcement, leniency, new surveillance technology, fortress-like pharmacies — with little apparent success, as stolen items are increasingly resold on the street or online.[1]

## Commentary and framing

Badlands' commentary attributed the trend to decades of decriminalizing crime and inverting its moral burden, arguing that narrators have changed hearts and minds toward the very concept of theft and citing the article's own line that stealing was a way of "fighting for dignity."[1] The analysis pointed to the 1973 animated film *Robin Hood* as an example of multigenerational cultural conditioning, and quoted [[alexandria-ocasio-cortez|Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez]]'s response to criticism of widespread looting in Philadelphia: "So, they go out and they need to feed their child and they don't have money...they need to shoplift some bread or go hungry that night."[1]

The brief argued that reformers habitually reframe thieves — whether of bread or earbuds, formula or flat screens — as benevolent Robin Hood figures redistributing from the rich to the poor in defiance of systemic injustice.[1] It countered that in the pre-modern legend, dating to the late 15th or early 16th century, Robin Hood stole from the treasury and from the Sheriff who collected taxes for the king, returning to the people what was rightfully theirs.[1] By this reading, social justice reformers are "the Sheriff of Nottingham, cosplaying Robin Hood, while at the same time demanding the King's tribute."[1]

As an illustration, Badlands cited Colorado Democrats — governing what the author called a one-party state for nearly a decade — for repeatedly attempting to erode the state constitution's Taxpayer Bill of Rights on the grounds that it hampers care for the poor, while in practice, the author argued, funneling millions to favored NGOs.[1] The piece concluded that decriminalization through "weaponized empathy," paired with the reframing of government as a benevolent public good, is "working as designed."[1]
