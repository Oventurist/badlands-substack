# CRISPR

CRISPR is a gene-editing tool that allows researchers to take DNA regarded as more "desirable" and artificially introduce it into other organisms, including livestock.[1] In May 2023 the technology entered the American food-supply debate when the [[food-and-drug-administration|US Food and Drug Administration]] approved the sale of pigs genetically modified using CRISPR for supermarket shelves.[1]

## The gene-edited pork approval

Scientists at [[washington-state-university|Washington State University]] developed the altered pork using CRISPR, and the resulting GMO pork was to be sold as German-style sausages, reportedly providing adequate nutrition for human consumption.[1] Washington State University professor Jon Oatley, who led the research, said the project's critical goal was not only to improve the quality of meat but also to improve the ability of livestock to thrive in harsh environments, which he argued would help increase protein sources in underdeveloped countries.[1] The story was reported by [[the-post-millennial|The Post Millennial]], drawing on Daily Mail coverage.[1]

## Commentary and criticism

[[johnny-q|Johnny Q]], writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], called the approval "madness," arguing that beyond being told to "eat the bugs," Americans would now have food "modified and poisoned like never before" through DNA modifications to livestock whose long-term results and side effects could not possibly have been tested.[1] The commentary conceded that CRISPR "sounds like a good idea on its face" but pointed to Recombinetics — one program of this nature — which took only one year to win FDA approval.[1]

Johnny Q urged a hard look at what is being introduced into the food supply, warning that the situation "could get out of hand quickly" and that neither US citizens nor people worldwide can deduce the long-term effects.[1] The commentary called for heeding what farmers and ranchers say about such advances, for caution regarding any addition to livestock DNA that could carry unidentifiable health risks, and for supporting local farmers and exercising care over what consumers purchase and consume.[1]
