# Russian Naval Base in Sudan

The Russian naval base in Sudan is a planned logistical support facility for the Russian Navy on Sudan's Red Sea coast, confirmed as agreed in February 2025 by Sudanese Foreign Minister Ali Youssef Ahmed Al-Sharif.[1]

## Agreement

Speaking at a press conference in Moscow with Russian Foreign Minister [[sergey-lavrov|Sergey Lavrov]] on Wednesday, Al-Sharif confirmed that discussions had successfully concluded, saying, "therefore, the matter is quite simple. I have nothing to add. We have agreed on everything."[1] According to TASS, the agreement first surfaced in December 2020 and outlines the creation of a logistical support facility serving as a hub for ship repairs and resupply.[1] Personnel at the facility are to be limited to 300, with a maximum of four Russian vessels stationed there simultaneously.[1]

Following the talks, Lavrov reaffirmed Russia's position on the need for a swift cessation of hostilities and the launch of an inclusive national dialogue regarding the conflict in [[sudan|Sudan]] between the [[sudanese-armed-forces|Sudanese Armed Forces]] and the paramilitary [[rapid-support-forces|Rapid Support Forces]], stressing the need for a resolution without external interference.[1]

## Strategic context

The base would sit across the Red Sea from [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]].[1] Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] characterized it as the first formal Russian military base on African soil, and argued that the drive to remove Russia from Syria is motivated by the fact that the Russian military and the [[wagner-group|Wagner Group]] Africa Corps have been using the naval base at [[tartus-naval-base|Tartus]] and the air base at [[khmeimim-air-base|Khmeimim]] to stage their African operations.[1]

He framed these operations as vital to liberating the African continent from what he called oligarchic imperialism, and tied the stakes to the continent's resource wealth: rare earth metals in the [[democratic-republic-of-the-congo|Democratic Republic of the Congo]] alone are estimated to exceed $24 trillion in value, including coltan, of which the DRC supplied roughly 50% of world supply according to 2023 reports.[1] He noted longstanding accusations that [[rwanda|Rwanda]] smuggles coltan and other metals out of the DRC to sell as its own exports, and suggested that militia groups such as [[m23|M23]] represent a unipolar effort to regain control of resources frozen by Trump in 2017 when he sanctioned Israeli oligarch [[dan-gertler|Dan Gertler]] under [[executive-order-13818|Executive Order 13818]].[1] He described these developments as central to the emerging [[multipolar-world|multipolar world order]].[1]

## Russian withdrawal from Syria

The Sudan agreement coincided with Britain's announcement that it had tracked six Russian naval and merchant ships carrying ammunition used in Syria as they sailed through the English Channel, shadowed by the Royal Navy and Royal Air Force.[1] The British defence ministry said the ships were withdrawing from Syria following the December ousting of President Bashar al-Assad, a close Russian ally, and described the evacuation as a "blow to Moscow's ambitions in the Middle East."[1] Defence minister John Healey said the ships "were retreating from Syria after Putin abandoned his ally Assad, yet they were still armed and full of ammunition," adding, "This shows Russia is weakened but remains a threat."[1] Russia hoped to retain the use of naval and air bases in Syria under the new Islamist leadership that took power after Assad fled to Moscow, following thirteen years of civil war in which Russian troops intervened on his behalf.[1]
