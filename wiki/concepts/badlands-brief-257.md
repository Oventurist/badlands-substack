# Badlands Brief: Bicameral Deal-Making and Accelerationism Acknowledged

"Bicameral Deal-Making and Accelerationism Acknowledged" is an edition of the [[badlands-brief|Badlands Brief]] published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on May 29, 2026. The Brief describes itself as "your daily drop of Badlands' takes on the narratives dominating the info war." Commentary in this issue is contributed by [[ghost-of-based-patrick-henry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], and [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]]. The edition covers five subjects: a reported United States–Iran ceasefire extension, the media treatment of the Iran war narrative, a federal court ruling on President Trump's election executive order, the Justice Department's stand-down on the Delcy Rodríguez investigation, and reporting that the administration's Cuba policy is internally described as "accelerationism."

## The reported 60-day ceasefire extension with Iran

The Brief's lead item summarizes reports that the United States and [[iran|Iran]] reached a tentative agreement on Thursday to extend the existing ceasefire by a further 60 days and to ease shipping restrictions through the [[strait-of-hormuz|Strait of Hormuz]]. Under the reported terms, the United States would lift its blockade of Iranian ports and some sanctions on Iranian oil sales in exchange for unrestricted shipping through the waterway. The agreement was described as still pending approval from President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and from Iranian leadership, and Iranian state media reported that the text had not been finalized or confirmed. Vice President [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] said the two sides were "not there yet, but we're very close," while a U.S. official characterized recent American strikes as defensive measures intended to preserve the ceasefire.

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry frames the episode as "The Art of the ~~Deal~~ Story." He argues that throughout Trump's second term, [[steve-witkoff|Steve Witkoff]] has made clear that when the president dispatches envoys to negotiate peace he empowers them with the autonomy to maneuver and secure agreements. In this reading, Trump deliberately sends close friends and confidants rather than "career diplomats from the [[state-department|State Department]]," because he trusts the former and does not trust the latter. The commentary contends that the notion of envoys returning with terms unacceptable to the president "flies in the face of everything we have come to understand about the dynamic between the President and his envoys," since they know his endgame and what he can and cannot accept before entering the room.

The writer therefore interprets the pending-approval framing as intentional: Trump is said to be "giving himself 'veto' power over a deal that surely he has already approved," creating a due-diligence period during which "all the snakes and jackals" have time to emerge and attempt to thwart the agreement, exposing themselves in the process, while reminding observers who holds the cards. Beyond the mechanics, the piece describes a "narrative quest," a "Dark Night of the Soul" functioning as a Hero's Journey for public perception of the Islamic Republic of Iran, comparable to but longer than the arc previously seen with [[venezuela|Venezuela]]. Because "the story of a 'scary' Iran has been deeply embedded in the modern zeitgeist," the author argues the American mythos must be rewritten before lasting peace between the American and Iranian peoples is possible, and predicts Trump will soon reverse his posture and rhetoric on Iran, dragging neoconservatives and Zionists "kicking and screaming into this peace" just as he dragged the populist-nationalist movement into the conflict.

## The Iran narrative "doom loop"

Burning Bright's segment argues that the [[media-industrial-complex|Media Industrial Complex]] immediately refuted the reported deal "using the ghost of Little Khamenei." He describes a recurring weekday dialectic in which reports that U.S. and Iranian negotiators have finalized, or are hours from finalizing, some framework, memorandum of understanding, or extension are followed within hours by denials attributed to the Iranian side but always translated through Western media. The alternation, he writes, sustains and ratchets "a low-grade whiplash that is entirely engineered."

Reading this pattern as favorable requires, in his account, two premises. The first is that baseline truth in the theater is functionally inaccessible to the public, and that every major media organ translating it operates at roughly the same level of reliable information as its audience—while having demonstrated that even if it held better maps of reality it would not translate them accurately, but often the opposite, which is itself useful information. This, he argues, explains why Trump has been "alternatingly-bombastic and dismissive" about a war "replete with fake Ayatollahs, fake navies, and, in this writer's estimation, a fake war whose actual phase was concluded before most of the current story beats kicked off." The Media Industrial Complex, meanwhile, is said to reach for "increasingly-threadbare justifications," including recent suggestions that when the supreme leader reappears he may be unrecognizable in voice and visage—a claim the author calls "patently absurd."

The second premise is that both sides of the "Mind War" believe prolonging the story cycle inflicts greater damage on the opponent, though only one can be correct given the zero-sum nature of what the author calls the Multipolar War. Media institutions are said to believe that sustaining the Iran storyline bleeds Trump's mandate and threatens his congressional map ahead of the [[2026-united-states-midterm-elections|midterms]]—elections Trump has publicly said he does not care about—treating each escalation or contradictory leak as a day's attrition. Trump, by contrast, signals that he is unbothered by either metric, because he has spent more than a decade forging a mandate the other side cannot replicate and continues to behave as though he holds the decisive cards on timing, off-ramps, and the codification of whatever settlement emerges from "the Iranian Knot."

## Federal ruling on the voter citizenship executive order

The third item reports that a federal judge cleared President Trump's executive order on voter citizenship checks and tighter mail-in voting rules to move forward. U.S. District Judge [[carl-nichols|Carl Nichols]] ruled that Democratic plaintiffs had failed to demonstrate [[legal-standing|legal standing]] or sufficient harm to justify a preliminary injunction. The judge found that the order does not directly require the plaintiffs to take any action and that no federal agency had yet implemented steps under it that could cause them harm. He rejected as speculative the argument that the order would disenfranchise millions of voters, noting that it mandates procedures allowing individuals to access, review, and correct information in any state-by-state citizenship lists used to maintain voter rolls. Privacy objections to the sharing of basic voter data such as name, age, and residence between government agencies were also dismissed as not rising to the level of injury required for Article III standing. The ruling removes a key legal obstacle to implementation, though no rollout timeline was detailed.

Ashe in America notes that her state attorney general has a reported 65 lawsuits against the Trump administration and its agenda and is counting them as campaign accomplishments in his run for governor, which she suggests could be understood as using state funds for a campaign, "but that would never be prosecuted in Colorado." She quotes the opinion directly: "Plaintiffs have failed to show that they are likely to have Article III standing or that they will suffer imminent and irreparable harm absent an injunction. The Court recognizes that the Postal Service may ultimately issue a final rule that directly affects Plaintiffs or their members, or that the Government may develop State Citizenship Lists that omit specific individuals due to particularized flaws. Plaintiffs may, of course, renew their motions if and when those future actions occur. Until then, however, Plaintiffs cannot show that preliminary injunctive relief is warranted." Her conclusion is that such suits are "not about justice, it's about obstruction," that states like Colorado are pursuing frivolous actions against a lawfully elected president's agenda, and that they are losing. The segment ends with the word "Accelerate."

## Stand-down on the Delcy Rodríguez investigation

The Brief reports that the Trump administration directed federal prosecutors in Miami to stand down on criminal investigations into Venezuela's acting president [[delcy-rodriguez|Delcy Rodríguez]]. The move is described as intended to support the stabilization of Venezuela following [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]]'s ouster and to facilitate U.S. investment in the country's oil sector, with prosecutors told to avoid actions that could upset ongoing diplomatic and economic initiatives with Rodríguez's government. Rodríguez, a longtime target of the [[drug-enforcement-administration|DEA]] previously sanctioned over alleged involvement in drug trafficking, gold smuggling, and money laundering, has never been criminally charged in the United States; sanctions against her were lifted after she was recognized as Venezuela's sole head of state. She has hosted U.S. delegations and participated in ceremonies with American oil executives as oil flows increased. The directive is said to align with a broader pattern of pausing high-profile probes into certain foreign leaders during the current administration.

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry, calling her "Our Girl, Delcy 'Kokomo' Rodriguez," observes that it was the first Trump administration that sanctioned her and opened an investigation, and asks whether she was ever the actual target or whether the sequence was misdirection intended to mislead certain parties. He attributes the sanctions on Rodríguez and others in the Maduro circle to [[john-bolton|John Bolton]], noting Bolton served from April 2018 until September 2019, and observes that in November 2019 [[erik-prince|Erik Prince]] secretly traveled to Caracas to meet Rodríguez on Trump's behalf, later reported by The Guardian as "outreach" to the Maduro government.

Citing Colonel [[douglas-macgregor|Douglas Macgregor]], who served as a senior military advisor during the first administration, the commentary states that Trump wanted nothing to do with Bolton until Israeli tycoon [[sheldon-adelson|Sheldon Adelson]] asked that Bolton be brought aboard, after which Trump assented. The author speculates that Bolton was "a spy sent by the Israeli oligarchy to infiltrate the Trump cabinet," and that administration behavior from April 2018 to September 2019 suggests Trump was, perhaps unwittingly, executing neoconservative foreign policy objectives in Venezuela. He then traces a timeline: Bolton is fired; Prince—who had previously offered 5,000 mercenaries for a ground invasion—is dispatched to meet Rodríguez in secret; and on January 3, 2020, Trump kills [[qassem-soleimani|Qassem Soleimani]], whom the author believes was a Mossad asset. COVID followed, and Trump's relationship with Israel is said to have become rocky as he wrestled with [[benjamin-netanyahu|Netanyahu]] over construction of settlements in the West Bank and pressed for the Abraham Accords. The author adds that the 2020 election was then stolen from Trump and that Netanyahu was the first to call Biden "to consecrate the coup." With the [[department-of-justice|DOJ]] now standing down, he concludes that "the timeline is the story, and the story makes sense," and asks whether a similar dynamic may be in play with Maduro.

## Newsom, Senate Bill 73, and the anti-weaponization fund

The Brief reports that California Governor [[gavin-newsom|Gavin Newsom]] signed [[california-senate-bill-73|Senate Bill 73]], which sharply restricts law enforcement access to ballots and election records. The law limits peace officers from accessing ballots, voter lists, rosters, and certified voting technology, and generally bars officers from interfering with election administration except in cases involving urgent public health or safety threats. A court order is required before law enforcement may take possession of key election materials, and removing packages containing voted ballots from election officials is classified as a crime. Newsom said the measure is intended to "clarify the rules of engagement," noting that violations can carry criminal penalties, fines, and up to three years in jail.

Newsom also vowed to impose a 100% tax on any payouts California residents receive from the $1.776 billion "anti-weaponization" fund established through Trump's settlement with the Department of Justice. He described the fund as a "slush fund" and linked it to Trump's pardons of January 6 defendants, saying recipients are being "rewarded" after being pardoned for "beating up cops."

Ashe in America calls the move "hilarious," observing that if a Californian receives a payout for having the government weaponized against them, the state government will be weaponized to take the payout through a 100% tax. She questions how "this taxation come through representation," calls the measure "petty and punitive and intended to preserve a false narrative," doubts it will survive legal challenge, and urges Californians to take the governor to court. She also quotes the ballot-custody provision—"Removing packages containing voted ballots from election officials is also classified as a crime, with civil penalties of up to $50,000 for violations involving ballot custody"—and remarks that, given that ballot chain of custody is "essentially non-existent," Newsom may have "just made status quo election administration a crime."

## Cuba policy described as accelerationism

The final item summarizes reporting that the Trump administration is applying economic pressure to [[cuba|Cuba]]'s government through expanded sanctions, a strategy described internally as "[[accelerationism|accelerationism]]" to hasten societal collapse in stages. On May 1, Trump signed an executive order imposing secondary sanctions on companies doing business with [[gaesa|GAESA]], Cuba's military-industrial conglomerate. The measure had already prompted foreign firms—including the Canadian miner [[sherritt-international|Sherritt International]] and the European shipping companies [[cma-cgm|CMA CGM]] and [[hapag-lloyd|Hapag-Lloyd]]—to suspend operations in Cuba, with further pullouts expected from banks and hotel operators.

Additional measures include the cutoff of Venezuelan oil shipments to Cuba, a Justice Department indictment of [[raul-castro|Raúl Castro]] over the 1996 [[brothers-to-the-rescue|Brothers to the Rescue]] shootdowns, and the termination of a GAESA executive's U.S. green card. The administration is also enforcing existing sanctions more aggressively and conducting multiagency planning for potential unrest on the island. Senior officials say the goal is to methodically constrict Cuba's economy and create conditions for a peaceful transition away from the current regime without immediate regime elimination, with limited humanitarian assistance routed through non-government channels such as the [[catholic-church|Catholic Church]] and charities to demonstrate alternatives to the existing system.

GhostofBasedPatrickHenry quotes the Axios report—"The best way to describe it is 'accelerationism,'" one senior administration official said—and jokes: "Am I tripping on acid, or has the Trump administration been reading the Badlands News Brief? Hey guys: if you need a consultant on this subject, I know a guy." The segment, and the edition, close with the word "Accelerate."
