# NATO Expansion

NATO expansion is the post-Cold War eastward enlargement of the Western alliance, treated in "A Bright Red Line" as the underlying cause of the war in Ukraine.[1]

## The "one inch" assurance

During negotiations over German reunification it was agreed that [[nato|NATO]] would not expand "one inch" eastward, an assurance given to [[mikhail-gorbachev|Mikhail Gorbachev]] as the Soviet Union fell.[1] Russian distrust was rooted in decades of enmity with the NATO states and in the memory of German invasion; a reunified Germany was among Russia's worst nightmares, given the tens of millions of Russians killed by German forces across the two world wars.[1]

## Sequence

The United States honored the agreement for a time, but the article argues it never intended to honor it indefinitely: when the security state sets its sights on something it bides its time and sets the stage until the moment is right.[1] Expansion began under the [[bill-clinton|Clinton]] administration, moved forward through the Bush administration — when Condoleezza Rice and [[victoria-nuland|Victoria Nuland]], then ambassador to NATO, discussed including Ukraine specifically — and reached a fever pitch during the Obama era.[1] It culminates in Secretary of State [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]]'s statement that Ukraine will become a member.[1]

## Russian perception

The 2008 cable of [[william-burns|William Burns]] recorded that Ukrainian and Georgian aspirations touched a raw nerve, that Russia perceived encirclement and efforts to undermine its regional influence, and that Ukrainian entry was "the brightest of all redlines" for the Russian elite.[1] The article stresses that Ukraine occupies Russia's most sensitive border, one that played a critical role in how Russia was attacked in both world wars, and that the Russian people, though not always in agreement among themselves, are united against NATO encroachment.[1] Alongside expansion, the article cites U.S.-sponsored regime change in neighboring countries despite promises to the contrary, and the sending of arms to those neighbors.[1]
