# 2024 East and Gulf Coast Dockworker Strike

Union dockworkers represented by the [[international-longshoremens-association|International Longshoremen's Association]] began striking against employers at East and Gulf Coast ports early on Tuesday, October 1, 2024.[1]

## Scope and demands

More than 45,000 ILA members from over three dozen facilities across 14 Gulf and East Coast ports went on strike early Tuesday, October 1, 2024, marking the largest labor action at US ports in nearly 50 years and the union's first since 1977.[3] The action, driven by disputes over automation and wages in a new multi-year labor contract, hit 36 ports and threatened to disrupt supply chains nationwide; if it persisted more than a week, retailers could face shortages of certain goods and potentially spark another wave of inflation.[3] Workers walked off the job at the Port of Philadelphia minutes after midnight when the ILA and the [[united-states-maritime-alliance|US Maritime Alliance]] (USMX) — a coalition of port operators and carriers — failed to agree on a contract that would have boosted wages 50% over six years and pledged limits on port automation; the union demanded a 77% pay bump.[3] The ILA said USMX's latest offer "fell far short of what ILA rank-and-file members are demanding in wages and protections against automation," with union president [[harold-daggett|Harold Daggett]] vowing to "stay out on strike for whatever period of time it takes."[3]

## Trump op-ed and Badlands reading

On the same day the strike commenced, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] published an opinion piece in [[newsweek|Newsweek]] laying out his plan to "Make America Affordable Again," pledging tariffs on foreign goods, the lowest taxes, energy costs and regulatory burdens for manufacturers that produce in the United States, and the use of tariff revenue to benefit American citizens.[3] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] combined this with [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]]'s research into Trump's ties to current ILA leadership — including Daggett's call for members to pray for Trump after the Pennsylvania rally shooting and his recollection of a productive November meeting with Trump — to argue the sequence was not coincidental.[3] The commentary contended that Trump's plan would reward foreign manufacturers for producing in America, forcing foreign brands to compete to hire American workers, build American manufacturing infrastructure and use American materials such as steel, creating what it called an infinite economic feedback loop in which foreign countries fund an American restoration.[3]

## Panic buying

Reports of panic-buying at supermarkets began spreading across social media almost immediately after the walkout began.[1] Video clips posted by users on X and Facebook showed people rushing to buy water, toilet paper, paper towels and other items at supermarkets and retailers across the United States.[1]

Photographer and author Denise Van Patten posted on X: "Are people already panic buying because of the Port Strike? Here are the grocery shelves in the water section at my local Kroger this morning. I realize that we are probably also low on water due to the Helene aftermath in East Tennessee, but still not great to see this already."[1]

The item appeared among the bonus items in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] for October 3, 2024, presented as part of an ever-escalating news cycle.[1]

## Settlement

On Thursday, October 3, 2024, U.S. dockworkers agreed to return to work after port operators sweetened their contract offer, ending a three-day strike that had threatened to disrupt the American economy.[2] Port employers offered a 62% wage increase over six years, up from an earlier proposed raise of 50%, according to people familiar with the matter.[2] The agreement ended a walkout that had closed container ports from Maine to Texas and threatened everything from the supply of bananas in supermarkets to the flow of cars through American factories.[2] The improved offer came after the [[white-house|White House]] privately and publicly pressed the large shipping lines and cargo terminal operators who employ the longshore workers to return to the table.[2] President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] applauded the agreement, saying "Collective bargaining works, and it is critical to building a stronger economy from the middle out and the bottom up."[2] The [[international-longshoremens-association|International Longshoremen's Association]] and port operators said in a joint statement that they had reached a tentative agreement on wages and that members would return to work, extending the prior contract — which expired at the start of that week — through January 15, 2025 while the parties negotiated remaining issues, including automation on the docks.[2]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that "the Regime got quite the scare there," and argued the episode made Americans newly aware that reliance on foreign trade had turned the United States into a "passthrough economy," citing a trade deficit of nearly $700 billion.[2] He framed energy, trade and jobs as three legs of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s campaign platform and the areas where the incumbent administration was weakest, predicting Trump would enter January 2025 with ready-made solutions to another manufactured problem.[2] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] separately noted the strike's suspension as one of three converging crises — with [[hurricane-helene|Hurricane Helene]] and the Middle East — being used to frame [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]]'s crisis-management credentials weeks before Election Day.[2]

## Pre-strike warnings and electoral framing (September 2024)

Days before the contract deadline, the CEO of [[flexport|Flexport]], one of the largest US supply-chain logistics operators, warned that "the biggest wild card in the presidential election that nobody's talking about" was "the looming port strike that could shut down all East and Gulf Coast ports just 36 days before the election."[4] The [[port-authority-of-new-york-and-new-jersey|Port Authority of New York and New Jersey]] said it was "coordinating with partners across the supply chain to prepare for any impacts" from a work stoppage by workers represented by the [[international-longshoremens-association|International Longshoremen's Association]] as they negotiated with the [[united-states-maritime-alliance|United States Maritime Alliance]], urging both sides "to find common ground and keep the cargo flowing for the good of the national economy" and noting that $240 billion in goods pass through its two ports each year, supporting more than 600,000 local jobs.[4]

Badlands Media argued that labor was, after abortion, Democrats' top 2024 issue and a strategic misstep: more than 300 labor strikes occurred in 2023, yielding only short-term deals, while the administration touted "15 million jobs" without acknowledging that many were second and third jobs taken because wages had lost purchasing power.[4] The commentary noted that the [[teamsters|Teamsters]] had just declined to endorse [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] after their members broke for [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], and predicted Democratic messaging on corporate greed would intensify and backfire: "Don't bullshit a longshoreman in NY or NJ and expect it to go well for you."[4]

## Eve of the strike deadline

On September 30, 2024, with the existing contract between the [[international-longshoremens-association]] (ILA) and the [[united-states-maritime-alliance]] (USMX) — a coalition of port operators and carriers — set to expire at midnight, thousands of longshoremen at three dozen facilities across fourteen Gulf and East Coast ports were poised to walk out at 12:01 a.m. EST on Tuesday, October 1.[5] A no-deal scenario was forecast to trigger a major supply chain disruption and an inflation surge.[5]

[[goldman-sachs]] analyst Jordan Alliger told clients that "upwards of $4.9bn per day is at risk in international trade along the East and Gulf coasts, along with the potential for supply chains to likely become less fluid due to emergent congestion, which in turn could result in a re-emergence of transport price inflation."[5] [[flexport]] chief executive [[ryan-petersen]] called the looming shutdown "the biggest wild card in the presidential election that nobody's talking about," noting it would close all East and Gulf Coast ports just thirty-six days before the vote.[5]

Badlands commentary framed the strike as a potential turning point that could destroy globalist influence over American business, arguing that transnational corporations would be unable to move product into U.S. markets while firms dependent on foreign parts would be forced to pivot to domestic suppliers — with real economic pain expected along the way.[5]

## Q for Dummies reading

Chapter 20 of the [[q-for-dummies|Q for Dummies]] series describes the walkout as "a very short-lived port strike" that had, for the moment, been averted and suspended until January 15.[6] The author frames the episode as a set of Q communications rather than an ordinary labour dispute, citing [[harold-daggett|Harold Daggett]]'s prior meeting with [[donald-trump|President Trump]] at Mar-a-Lago and a cluster of numbers — a $1.7 million home of 7,163 square feet, a 76-foot yacht, 17 port deaths in three months, and 45,000 striking workers — as evidence of hidden significance.[6] The chapter closes the passage by recalling the Q instruction to [[watch-the-water|watch the water]].[6]

## Carlson's white-hat interpretation

In "Three Possible Election Outcomes," published October 10, 2024, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] treated the strike's timing as suspicious, writing that it seemed "too coincidental" that the walkout was set to occur immediately before the election.[7] He noted that the closures temporarily locked down the ports of the East and South, drastically reducing what moved in and out of the country.[7]

Carlson's central speculation is that the shutdown would have crippled drug running, gun running and human trafficking had it continued, and he asked whether containers were inspected during the stoppage — or would have been had the strike lasted longer.[7] He also weighed the opposite reading, that a longer strike was intended to disrupt the supply chain, cause shortages and price increases and a temporary stock market drop, none of which would have reflected well on the incumbent administration.[7]

Explicitly labelling the section as speculation, he proposed that the strike may have been a [[white-hats|white hat]] operation meant to run far longer and shut down trafficking networks, but forced to end prematurely.[7] In the same passage he suggested that Deep State actors may have manipulated the weather to intensify the hurricane that struck North Carolina and neighbouring states, and then withheld assistance, in order to create pressure to reopen the ports and restore trafficking operations.[7]
