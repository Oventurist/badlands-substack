# A New Hollywood

"A New Hollywood," subtitled "It's Already Happening," is an opinion essay published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on February 6, 2025 and written by contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]]. The piece argues that the American film and television industry — long, in the author's view, an instrument of ideological conditioning — is in the process of being taken over by allies of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], and that the resulting shift in programming is already visible in recent Paramount and Showtime releases. The article received 292 likes on Substack.

## Personal framing

Carlson opens by acknowledging the criticism he receives for typos and grammar, for not home-schooling his children, and for continuing to consume Hollywood entertainment. Writing as a member of Generation X, he describes cinema as central to his adolescence, recalling in vivid detail the theaters in which he saw *Star Wars*, *The Empire Strikes Back*, *Return of the Jedi*, *Road Warrior*, *E.T.*, *Rocky III*, *Rambo*, *Top Gun*, *The Terminator* and *Ghostbusters*, and choosing films such as *Raiders of the Lost Ark*, *Die Hard* and *Back to the Future* on the strength of their posters alone. He observes that these films share a common structure: brave heroes who risk life and limb against a corrupt and evil enemy or system in order to save humanity, and he asks whether he was "being conditioned by Hollywood … in a good way?"

Contrasting himself with Vivek Ramaswamy, Carlson writes that he does not believe most young children should dream of writing software code or winning math contests; instead they should dream "about being brave and selfless" — of pulling someone from a burning building, fighting off an invading army, standing up for a person being bullied, or scoring a 90-yard touchdown. He clarifies that this does not preclude later aspirations to work at Microsoft or Google, only that six-year-olds "should still be dreamers and not overly pragmatic." He later states that "young boys should aspire to someday be Batman, not an accountant," and recounts having once saved a man from being beaten to death by five other men — an act he attributes to "dumb courage," the grace and protection of God, and the influence of the movies he watched growing up, in which moment he "became Dirty Harry, Johnny Rambo and Rocky all rolled into one."

## The critique of contemporary Hollywood

The article argues that Hollywood has changed markedly, especially over the past decade: "Gone are the heroic tales, and in is the woke conditioning." Standing up to the system, Carlson writes, has been replaced with succumbing to it, and even when a brave hero is the protagonist, the hero must often be a "woke version — female instead of male, black instead of white, gay instead of straight, transgender instead of ... cisgender." He asserts that boys are taught their masculinity is toxic while girls are taught their femininity is weak, with both conditioned to exist "somewhere in the middle, a very Baphomet concept." He also notes that theatergoing has become too expensive and that few current films justify leaving the house.

Carlson says he understands and does not disagree with the sentiment that Hollywood is evil and "run by a bunch of Satanic pedophiles," and that many at the top are truly evil while many beneath them succeeded by submitting to that evil. He nonetheless insists there remain "plenty of decent people in the Hollywood entertainment system," and that the medium of film storytelling is not inherently evil.

## Trump's Hollywood ambassadors

The essay cites Trump's recent appointment of Jon Voight, Sylvester Stallone and Mel Gibson as "ambassadors to Hollywood." Carlson asks why Trump would make such appointments unless he believed Hollywood had been, and could again be, an important part of what makes America America — "why not just burn it to the ground if there is no hope for it?" In his view, Trump understands that Hollywood can be used as it was used on him in his youth: to inspire and shape while entertaining.

## Evidence from recent programming

Carlson offers three recent shows as evidence. Watching the third episode of the second season of *Lioness* on Paramount+ — a series about [[cia|CIA]] operatives infiltrating a Mexican drug cartel to rescue a kidnapped American politician — he and his wife encountered a breakfast-table scene in which two daughters call their physician father transphobic for questioning how a friend identifies. The father replies that a phobia is an irrational fear, and that disagreeing with someone, or not feeling one has enough information to accept a statement, is not fear; the mother, a CIA agent, adds that everyone must have the right to disagree and challenge another's point of view, and that she fights to protect that right. Carlson writes that he "almost fell off the couch," calling it "the most anti-woke thing I've seen come out of Hollywood in years, if ever."

He then cites *The Agency*, a new [[showtime|Showtime]] drama about a CIA officer coming in from the cold after years undercover, released less than two months earlier, and *Landman*, a Paramount+ series about the Texas oil fields, which he glosses with the phrase "drill, baby drill." Each prompts the same question: who owns these networks?

## Ownership and the Skydance merger

The article traces the ownership chain. Showtime is owned by [[paramount-global|Paramount Global]], which the source says is run by Trump ally [[shari-redstone|Sheri Redstone]], who took over from her father [[sumner-redstone|Sumner Redstone]] in 2016; Sumner, also described as a Trump ally, died in 2020. Paramount Global is in the process of merging with, and will eventually be controlled by, [[skydance-media|Skydance Media]], run by [[david-ellison|David Ellison]], son of [[larry-ellison|Larry Ellison]], cofounder of [[oracle|Oracle]] and a well-known, very wealthy Trump ally and financial supporter.

Paramount Global also owns [[cbs|CBS]]. Carlson recalls that during the most recent presidential campaign, *60 Minutes* aired an interview with Kamala Harris that was discovered to have been edited to make her look better by removing as much "unintelligible word-salad" as possible. Trump treated this as election tampering and sued CBS for $10 billion; critics and CBS leadership called the suit frivolous, yet CBS has recently discussed settling out of court. Carlson notes that Paramount Global is currently worth around $8 billion — less than the $10 billion sought — after fluctuating between $20 and $40 billion in the seven pre-Covid years and spiking to $60 billion three years ago on the launch of its streaming service before falling back. He asks how one sues a company for more than it is worth, and what might induce a plaintiff to settle: "Control over the company?" His implied answer is that Paramount does not want the lawsuit to interfere with the Skydance deal.

## Larry Ellison, Stargate and the "CIA stooge" objection

Larry Ellison is described as currently in the news through Project Stargate, Trump's plan for the United States to invest $500 billion in AI infrastructure in partnership with Oracle, OpenAI and SoftBank. At the same time, Trump suggested that either [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] or Ellison buy [[tiktok|TikTok]]. Carlson concludes Ellison is "a big part of Trump's plan going forward."

He addresses those in the "truther community" who call Ellison a CIA stooge, responding that "everyone within the American tech space has some past connection to DARPA and/or the CIA," which does not mean they are currently working against Trump or "haven't had their strings cut." He criticizes those for whom Trump oscillates "between being a genius and being mentally challenged, or between being a superhero and a super villain," noting that it took only two days of Trump's presidency for some to become unhinged, reminiscent of the backlash over his cabinet picks. Some of this reaction, he writes, stems from trauma abuse, "but some of it is contrived."

## Tom Cruise and predictive programming

Skydance produced *Top Gun: Maverick*, the *Mission Impossible* franchise and *Jack Reacher* — all featuring [[tom-cruise|Tom Cruise]] — and Carlson observes that Paramount+ has for years seemed "like a Tom Cruise streaming app." Skydance's television output includes *Reacher*, about an ex-military police major who solves crimes, and *Jack Ryan*, in which a CIA analyst goes into the field to save America from foreign threats.

The article recalls that the original *Top Gun* (1986), released under President Ronald Reagan, was "the biggest military recruiting tool ever created." It notes that *Mission Impossible: Dead Reckoning*, released in summer 2023 — roughly six months after the AI boom began with ChatGPT's late-2022 release — turns on a team saving the world from a powerful [[artificial-intelligence|AI]] system that could be used to take over the world if it fell into the wrong hands. Carlson says production must have begun well before AI became a popular talking point, and asks whether Tom Cruise is "one of the good guys," whether a faction of Hollywood is trying to warn audiences of future events, and whether "we are being told America needs to be the one wielding the AI sword, and not its adversaries." This reading is an instance of what the corpus calls predictive programming.

Returning to *Lioness*, he emphasizes that its second season involves rescuing an American politician and trafficked children from a Mexican drug kingpin, showing the cartel's ruthlessness and its use of "the carrot or the stick": officials are offered large sums to protect the cartel, and if they refuse, their whole family will be killed. "It's not as simple as corrupt people taking money to do corrupt things."

## Conclusions

Carlson concludes that Hollywood "is not only being saved, but that it has already been infiltrated by Trump's people," and that just as it was used for predictive programming to prepare audiences to accept [[deep-state|Deep State]] narratives, it is now being used to prepare them for what Trump has in store. He predicts Trump will take down the Mexican drug cartel, and infers from the volume of intelligence-agency programming produced by Trump allies that Trump does not intend to "scatter the CIA into a thousand pieces" but to reform it.

Describing Trump as "Reagan 2.0" — not a continuation of Reagan but "a vastly improved version ... that doesn't include compromising with the enemy" — he writes that Reagan took control of Hollywood during his eight years in office and pushed for films promoting strong American characters such as Rambo, making the U.S. military appear powerful, inspiring Americans and intimidating foreign adversaries.

The essay closes with a general argument that instruments of evil can be redeemed: film, AI, the financial system, and the medical industry "since the Rockefellers took it over a hundred years ago" have all been used to control, brainwash, enslave and sicken, but all can be transformed to do good. "We must allow ourselves to envision how things could be, and should be, rather than how they have been, and currently are. Everything is being transformed. The patriots are now in control. What a glorious time to be alive."
