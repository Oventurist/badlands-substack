# America Has a CIA-Run Economy

"America Has a CIA-Run Economy," subtitled "So ... What Now?", is an opinion essay by Erik Carlson published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on January 18, 2025. The piece combines a statistical argument about the extreme concentration of the American stock market with the claim that the largest U.S. technology corporations were created, funded, or captured by the [[cia|Central Intelligence Agency]] using technology originating at [[darpa|DARPA]]. Written in the days surrounding Donald Trump's second inauguration, it closes with the author's expectation that the intelligence community's control over these firms is being severed, and with an argument that the American economy cannot be demolished in the pursuit of justice.

## The concentration argument

Carlson opens with what he calls groundwork, warning readers that "the technical information will quickly give way to conspiracy." He describes the [[sp-500|S&P 500]] as the 500 largest U.S. companies by market capitalization, weighted so that larger companies occupy a larger share of the index. In 2025, he writes, the top five companies account for 33% of the index weighting, compared with 11% in 2010 — a tripling of concentration in fifteen years, so that one third of the weighting sits in 1% of the constituent companies. Because only about 1% of American businesses are publicly traded at all, he concludes that "the stock market doesn't represent the economy, and the S&P 500 doesn't properly represent the stock market."

The essay reproduces a comparison of the five largest American companies by market capitalization across four snapshot years. For 2025 it lists [[apple-inc|Apple]], [[nvidia|Nvidia]], [[microsoft|Microsoft]], [[alphabet-inc|Alphabet]], and [[amazon|Amazon]]; for 2020, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and [[meta-platforms|Meta]]; for 2010, Exxon, Apple, Microsoft, [[berkshire-hathaway|Berkshire Hathaway]], and Walmart; and for 2000, Exxon, GE, Cisco, [[pfizer|Pfizer]], and Walmart. Carlson observes that the 2025 top five are all artificial-intelligence-focused big-tech firms, the 2020 five all pre-AI big-tech firms, while the 2000 list was a diversified mix spanning energy, aerospace engines, technology, pharmaceuticals, and retail.

The article offers a specific explanation for Pfizer's size in 2000: pharmaceutical advertising was permitted on broadcast television beginning in 1997, and Viagra reached the public in 1998. Carlson generalizes from this that the pharmaceutical industry will "pump us full of toxins, then sell us drugs [to] fix what it caused."

To underline the degree of top-heaviness, the piece notes that in 2025 Apple is roughly ten times larger than the twentieth-ranked company, Home Depot, whereas in 2000 Exxon was only three times larger than the twentieth-ranked company, Eli Lilly. Carlson raises the 1911 [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] decision breaking up the Rockefeller monopoly [[standard-oil|Standard Oil]] into 34 separate entities as a possible model, asking whether antitrust dissolution "is this the path forward to limit the power and influence the top 1% of publicly traded American companies have."

## Covid, convenience, and social atomization

The essay argues that the concentration was not organic. During Covid, Carlson writes, smaller companies "went belly up while the mega corporations became even bigger," and he cites the common description of the period as "the Amazon pandemic" because of the market share Amazon captured during lockdowns.

He offers [[starbucks|Starbucks]] as an anecdotal case, recalling that in the years immediately before the lockdowns Starbucks locations without drive-throughs were being replaced by locations with them, and that the company pushed order-ahead functionality that minimized in-store interaction. In the five years after the lockdowns, he says, seating has been permanently removed from many locations, which he reads as a deliberate discouragement of community in small towns where the store functions as a town center.

From this Carlson extrapolates a broader thesis he says he has argued before: that "part of the technology push we have experienced the past 20 years is to minimize the human interactions we have with each other on a daily basis," reducing connection, empathy, and ultimately procreation. He describes the end goal as populations rarely leaving their apartments in "15-minute cities," working from home, having everything delivered, and spending Saturday evenings "with our AI robot girlfriend or boyfriend." He anticipates and dismisses the counterargument that these changes are merely about convenience.

## DARPA and the origins of big tech

A section headed "DARPA" describes the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency as a research and development arm of the [[department-of-defense|U.S. Department of Defense]] that develops new military technologies with taxpayer money, technologies that then migrate into American society and the economy. The internet is named as probably the biggest and most important example.

Carlson contrasts DARPA's public relations with that of [[nasa]], recalling childhood claims that Tang and Velcro were NASA inventions — untrue, he writes, but never corrected because the agency "wanted to seem more important." DARPA-derived products, by contrast, were marketed to Americans through founder mythology: "a hoody wearing tech nerd from Harvard," "a couple of friends working out of a mom's garage," and "a guy selling books online out of a small, one-person office," origin stories he says were reinforced by films.

The essay then makes its central intelligence claim: in some cases the CIA has taken DARPA technology and used it to influence and monitor Americans, and because Americans would not voluntarily hand information to the agency, "the CIA creates companies and assigns people to be the faces of the companies."

## Operation Mockingbird and the loss of media control

As precedent, Carlson recounts the 1975 [[church-committee|Church Committee]] revelations, describing how the congressional committee led by Senator Frank Church questioned CIA Director [[william-colby|William Colby]] under oath, and how Colby revealed the existence of [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]] and the placement of agency assets inside media companies. The article states that the CIA had manipulated and controlled American media since 1960 and possibly earlier.

Before the 1990s, the essay argues, control was straightforward because the agency needed only to manage Hollywood, the music industry, television, radio, books, magazines, newspapers, and broadcast news. The arrival of the internet was "like the Wild West" and destroyed that control at a time when anti-government sentiment was rising. By 2000, with 52% of Americans online, the solution Carlson attributes to the CIA was to create and control social media platforms and search engines.

## LifeLog

The article quotes Wikipedia at length on [[darpa-lifelog|LifeLog]], a project of DARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office intended to "compile a massive electronic database of every activity and relationship a person engages in" — credit card purchases, websites visited, the content of telephone calls and emails, scans of faxes and postal mail, instant messages, books and magazines read, television and radio selections, GPS location from wearable sensors, and biomedical data — in order to identify "preferences, plans, goals, and other markers of intentionality." A predictive component sought to "find meaningful patterns in the timeline, to infer the user's routines, habits, and relationships." The quoted passage notes the program's cancellation on February 4, 2004 after privacy criticism.

Carlson observes that February 4, 2004 was also the day Facebook launched. He further asserts that much of Facebook's early funding came from CIA shell companies; that Google Earth is a repurposed CIA satellite given to the company; and that one of [[amazon-web-services|Amazon Web Services]]' first major contracts was with the CIA. He dates that contract to early 2013 at $600 million, and notes that in August 2013 [[jeff-bezos]] bought "the CIA run newspaper," [[washington-post|The Washington Post]], for $250 million.

On Microsoft, the essay recalls that by the late 1990s [[bill-gates]] was "one of the most hated men in the world," that he founded the [[bill-and-melinda-gates-foundation|Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation]] in 2000, and that he was rapidly recast as a great philanthropist even as his net worth doubled in a year while he was believed to be giving his money away. Carlson repeats a rumor that Gates's mother hired IBM engineers to produce early Microsoft software, and adds that DARPA "also probably created SIRI." He states that iPhones listen to everything said around them and track everywhere their owners go.

## Trump, the inaugural fund, and the "real pardoning"

The essay's final movement concerns the realignment of technology executives toward [[donald-trump]]. Carlson notes that many CEOs, especially in big tech, have endorsed Trump or at least stopped working against him: [[elon-musk]] has been the most supportive both vocally and financially; [[mark-zuckerberg]] of Meta has agreed to open his platforms to more free speech; and Jeff Bezos has said the Post should be less biased and more trustworthy.

Big tech companies and CEOs donating one million dollars each to Trump's inaugural fund is described as symbolic and "a drop in the bucket." Carlson calls this "the real pardoning that is happening in America," contrasting it with Joe Biden's pardons, which he considers "purely optics," and describing Trump as allowing economically valuable companies to "kiss the ring." He lists Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Apple, and [[openai]] as donors, noting the common thread that all are massive big-tech firms heavily involved in [[artificial-intelligence|AI]] development. He flags Nvidia's conspicuous absence from the donor list and asks whether Nvidia is "not a part of Trump's future plans."

Acknowledging that many within [[maga|MAGA]] distrust "these Deep State puppets who seem to be having their strings cut," Carlson argues that Trump evidently regards them and their companies as necessary to the American economy, since "it's hard to imagine an America without Amazon, Apple, FaceBook, Microsoft or Google." He contends that what emerges from Seattle and Silicon Valley via DARPA constitutes an advantage other nations cannot match: other countries can compete in oil production, mining, and manufacturing, but not in the creation of intellectual property, an area in which America excels because it "is founded on principals that lead to creativity."

## Prognosis

Carlson catalogues recent signs he reads as favorable: Zuckerberg's free-speech commitments and termination of DEI programs; Bezos's pledge to address bias and misinformation at the Post and his order returning Amazon employees to the office, which Carlson says both alleviates "the commercial real estate ticking time bomb that working from home has caused" and forces human interaction; and Gates's plan to fund nuclear power plants to run Microsoft's AI.

Looking forward, he expects that some companies and CEOs will not be chosen to take part in "the American Renaissance," that some mega corporations will be broken into smaller companies, that CIA and [[deep-state|Deep State]] Cabal strings will continue to be cut, and that America will continue to lead in innovation with more of it coming from American-born citizens.

The essay ends on a note of qualified reconciliation. Carlson writes that the American economy is not yet out of the woods but that "the future is brighter than we've ever known," that he does not expect figures like Zuckerberg and Bezos to escape entirely unscathed, but that he will not let his "blood boil" at the sight of them seated with Musk at the inauguration. "The cure can't be worse than the disease," he argues; the U.S. economy cannot be allowed to collapse merely to bring down everyone deserving of a treason trial. Trump, he concludes, "is a businessman, and he understands that this is business." If the CIA's strings have been cut, "I for one am going to take the win. Gladly."
