# 2024 US Political Violence Surge

The 2024 US political violence surge refers to a wave of politically tinged assaults, shootings and property crimes documented in the run-up to the November 5, 2024 presidential election, and to the media framing built around it. [[reuters|Reuters]] reported in October 2024 that it had identified at least 300 cases of political violence since Trump supporters stormed the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021, including at least 51 incidents in 2024 alone — described as the biggest and most sustained increase in U.S. political violence since the 1970s.[1]

## Documented incidents

In York, Pennsylvania, a man accosted a group rallying for Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]], punching a 74-year-old man in the head and hurling a racial slur at another as he fled.[1] In northern Michigan, an assailant enraged by his hatred of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] used an all-terrain vehicle to run over and injure an 81-year-old man who was putting up a yard sign for Trump's reelection bid.[1] Other high-profile incidents included two assassination attempts on Trump and three shootings at a Democratic campaign office for Harris in Arizona.[1]

Reuters also documented scores of cases arising from election disputes, LGBTQ+ rights and the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, ranging from small fights over political signs to violent brawls and property destruction at rallies.[1] Most of 2024's violence was non-fatal, the exceptions being a spectator killed during July's attempt on Trump's life and the shooter himself.[1] The pace of cases had remained consistent since rising in 2016, with 93 cases in 2021, 79 in 2022 and 76 in 2023.[1] Political extremism experts warned that the charged atmosphere of the 2024 campaign had created a highly volatile situation, and Reuters noted Trump's incendiary rhetoric about prosecuting political enemies and deploying the military against "the enemy within."[1]

## Badlands critique

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that a sober reading of Reuters' own examples pointed to a mental health problem rather than an organized political threat.[1] The catalogue she reviewed included [[thomas-matthew-crooks|Thomas Crooks]], the Butler shooter whom federal investigators concluded had "no definitive ideology"; Joshua Kemppainen, an avowed Trump hater in Michigan who vandalized Trump supporters' property, hospitalized an 81-year-old Vietnam veteran and then killed himself; a Florida man who told his wife he was preparing for war, was found burying ammunition in a park and was involuntarily committed; [[ryan-routh|Ryan Routh]]'s alleged second Trump assassination attempt; Robert Trotta, who attacked 74-year-old Pennsylvania borough councilman Alan Vandersloot while he held a Harris sign; Caleb Gannon, who tackled Scott Hayes at dueling Palestine–Israel protests in Massachusetts and was shot in the abdomen; Russell Valleau, who allegedly attacked a Black female postal worker with a knife over a Harris campaign mailer; and four white men in North Carolina who shot three Black teenagers with a BB gun after asking whether they liked Trump.[1]

The critique noted that police did not believe Trotta's attack was politically motivated, describing him instead as a known racist with a history of randomly hitting people, even though Reuters emphasized his 2020-era pro-Trump social media account.[1] Aside from the Michigan assailant's suicide, the only deaths cited were [[cory-comperatore|Cory Comperatore]] and Crooks.[1]

## Comparative framing

Ashe in America contrasted Reuters' coverage of 51 political violence cases with the outlet's silence on migrant crime in the United States, citing Customs and Border Protection statistics of 15,608 reports of criminal noncitizen encounters in 2024 year-to-date, including 27 homicides and 202 sexually-based offenses.[1] She argued that migrant crime appears in Reuters coverage mainly when the story concerns Europe or when the outlet is fact-checking Trump.[1]
