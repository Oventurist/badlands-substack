# Hurricane Helene

**Hurricane Helene** was a major Atlantic hurricane that struck Florida in late September 2024 before plowing through several states in the Southeastern United States, flooding towns and killing more than 160 people.[1] Its aftermath became a central political story of the final weeks of the 2024 presidential campaign, driving both a federal disaster-funding crisis and a wave of grassroots volunteer relief activity in the affected mountain communities of western North Carolina and East Tennessee.[1]

## Federal response and funding shortfall

As the [[fema|Federal Emergency Management Agency]] worked with states to assess damage and deliver meals, water, generators and other critical supplies, Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] told reporters on October 2, 2024 that the agency could meet immediate needs but did "not have the funds to make it through the season."[1] His remarks, delivered aboard Air Force One, underscored concerns voiced by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and lawmakers that [[congress|Congress]] might need to pass a supplemental spending bill that fall to help states with recovery efforts.[1] Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30, with most storms typically occurring in September and October, and Mayorkas noted another hurricane was expected.[1]

## Obstruction of volunteer relief

Reports circulated widely on social media of government officials at multiple levels impeding private citizens attempting rescue and resupply flights into flood-cut communities.[1] South Carolina pilot [[jordan-seidhom|Jordan Seidhom]] — a former head of the Chesterfield County Sheriff's Office narcotics unit — was ordered out of Lake Lure, North Carolina under threat of arrest after flying volunteer supply and rescue missions in his own helicopter.[1] Special Forces veteran [[tim-kennedy|Tim Kennedy]] reported a comparable experience.[1]

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] wrote that it was "difficult, in this moment, to find the words to describe what kind of miscreant you must be to threaten to arrest a guy flying his own helicopter into dangerous floods to rescue people who desperately need help," particularly while the government itself did little to assist.[1] He posed a hypothetical in which 99% of government employees are good people, asking what it says about a government if the remaining 1% can "wield enough power to implement this much evil and chaos" while the majority appears powerless to stop them, and invoked the Declaration of Independence's provision for altering or abolishing a government that becomes destructive of its ends.[1] He called for "a true political reckoning of biblical proportion" achieved not through violence but through exposure of the establishment's conduct "in full and on the record."[1]

## Political framing

Helene was one of three simultaneous crises — with the [[2024-east-and-gulf-coast-dockworker-strike|dockworker strike]] and Middle East escalation — that legacy outlets described as converging on Vice President [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] and the [[white-house|White House]] five weeks before Election Day.[1] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the coverage functioned as a campaign contribution rather than criticism, casting crisis management as an opportunity for voters to see Harris "at work."[1] Badlands commentary situated the storm within the broader [[polycrisis|polycrisis]] framework, tying disaster-relief shortfalls to border spending and energy policy.[1]

## Recovery funding delays in Western North Carolina (2025)

In early 2025, the slow rollout of Helene recovery funds in Western North Carolina became a point of contrast with congressional Democrats' protests over cuts to foreign aid.[2] Federal disaster money routed through agencies such as FEMA and the [[department-of-housing-and-urban-development|Department of Housing and Urban Development]] required states to prepare and submit spending plans for HUD approval before disbursement, a cycle that officials quoted by Cardinal & Pine on January 31, 2025 said would likely delay actual funding until the summer.[2]

In December 2024, both chambers of the North Carolina legislature passed a hurricane relief measure, the governor vetoed it, and both chambers then overrode the veto.[2] The 131-page bill transferred $252 million to the state's Hurricane Helene relief fund while also enacting significant changes to state government, most notably placing the State Board of Elections under the control of the newly elected Republican State Auditor Dave Boliek and curtailing the powers of the Attorney General's office.[2] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the veto fight had been driven less by the relief funding than by the elections-administration provisions, and noted accompanying allegations of voter suppression.[2]

## Cumulative strain with Hurricane Milton

Helene's flooding across the Southeast, followed less than two weeks later by [[hurricane-milton|Hurricane Milton]]'s approach to Tampa Bay, left federal disaster programs facing financial instability in October 2024.[3] [[fema|FEMA]]'s disaster fund and flood insurance program, along with [[small-business-administration|Small Business Administration]] disaster loans, were reported as possibly weeks from running dry, based on remarks by President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]], Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] and insurance analysts, even as FEMA insisted it could meet its "life-saving" responsibilities for both storms.[3]
