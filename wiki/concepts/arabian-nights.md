# Arabian Nights — The Redemption Arc of the House of Saud

"Arabian Nights" is a long-form essay published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on August 2, 2023, written by the pseudonymous contributor Ghost of Patrick Henry. It is presented as the first installment of a multi-part series intended to argue that Saudi Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman]] is engaged in a genuine reform project aimed at freeing the Middle East from foreign manipulation. The author frames the piece explicitly as preparatory background — "chopping some wood," in a phrase he attributes to Steve Bannon — before a promised follow-up article covering the First World War, the Hashemites, and the founding of the modern Kingdom of [[saudi-arabia]].

The article combines personal memoir, a primer on Arabic naming conventions, a geographic and tribal survey of Arabia, a retelling of Abrahamic and early Islamic narrative history, and an account of the eighteenth-century alliance between [[mohammed-bin-saud]] and [[muhammad-ibn-abd-al-wahhab]]. Badlands Media appends its standard disclaimer that contributors' articles represent the opinions of the authors rather than the outlet.

## Personal preface

The essay opens with the May 2017 sword dance in Riyadh, an event the author calls "iconic in our movement" and notes was spotlighted by [[q|Q]]. He recounts that it fell on his birthday, one week before his wedding in the Dominican Republic, and describes reading the reaction of the mainstream press from the beach with a sense of vindication for having supported [[donald-trump]] "from the moment he stepped on that escalator."

He then recounts two family deaths. His younger brother died of a Stage 4 glioblastoma after a three-year illness — the author notes in passing that this is the same affliction attributed to [[john-mccain|"No Name"]] — choking in their mother's arms. About eighteen months later his older brother's body was found in the Rappahannock River after falling from a boat and being left for dead; the author draws a parallel to the July 2023 reporting on the death of Tafari Campbell, a member of [[barack-obama]]'s staff. He describes learning that the man he had invited to read scripture at the funeral was subsequently investigated for the killing, and a five-year fight for justice in which one of three judges rejected a plea agreement struck between what he calls a corrupt prosecutor and an "overprivileged defendant" — a sequence he likens to a judge's refusal to accept Hunter Biden's plea deal.

The author recalls texting his brother and father on Election Day 2016 that General Michael Flynn was waging a "silent civil war" against [[hillary-clinton]] and her associates, a message later reviewed during the criminal investigation into his brother's death.

## Q, Las Vegas, and Mohammed bin Salman

The article dates the author's interest in Saudi Arabia to the appearance of the anonymous poster later known as Q on 4chan's /pol/ board, weeks after the October 2017 Las Vegas shooting. He notes that the poster met the same derision as earlier figures such as FBIAnon and WHInsiderAnon, but that his emphasis on "SA" — Saudi Arabia — rather than the promised arrests of American political figures is what held the author's attention. He quotes the drop: "Get the popcorn, Friday & Saturday will deliver on the MAGA promise. POTUS knows he must clean house (gov't) in order to 'free up' and demonstrate who has authority in order to pass important legislation."

The author describes searching for livestreams of the November 4, 2017 royal ceremony and confirming a later Q post claiming martial law had been declared in Saudi Arabia. On that date Mohammed bin Salman, who had served as defence minister for the preceding five years, detained roughly forty relatives and officials in the Riyadh Ritz-Carlton on charges of corruption and treason. The author observes that "40" functions in Arabic idiom much as "dozens" does in English — forty years in the desert, forty days and forty nights, "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves." He also relays the widespread online speculation at the time that the crown prince was somehow connected to, or the intended target of, the Las Vegas shooting.

Drawing on a 2020 New York Times article by ben hubbard — whose biography of the prince he credits for data-gathering while calling its portrait of MBS as a malevolent figure "hot garbage" — the author notes that two of MBS's half-brothers died almost exactly a year apart between 2001 and 2002, and argues that bereavement drew MBS closer to his father and instilled in him an attachment to his country and people. He contrasts the coverage of Trump's warmth toward MBS with the treatment of alwaleed bin talal, whom the Times, he says, attempts to separate from the royal family, and notes the arrest and stripping of powers of muhammad bin nayef, the displaced crown prince later accused of plotting to murder king salman.

The article's central interpretive claim about the present day is that MBS is not Trump's agent. His path, the author writes, began at the age of sixteen and specifically on the morning of September 11, 2001, when the future prince reacted with the same shock, fear, and desire for retribution as most Americans — an effect the author compares to that described by Noor bin Ladin. He characterises MBS as a figure who rejected the "elitist, globetrotting lifestyle" of his young relatives in favour of shadowing his father, who consumed 1980s and 1990s American culture, and who is best understood alongside Trump as "two warriors meeting on the road, each on their own quest to slay the same dragon." Interpreting an image caption, he presents MBS as "The Punisher," and invokes Alexander and the Gordian Knot as the model for cutting through an intractable problem.

## Arabic names

A short section explains the patronymic system, which the author compares to medieval European and *Game of Thrones* conventions: "I am John, son of Roger, of the House Smith." He explains the *laqab*, an honorary title analogous to the Roman agnomen, citing Francis "the Swamp Fox" Marion, Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson, and Dwayne "The Rock" Johnson. He notes that the family name of Syrian president [[bashar-al-assad]] means "the lion," and that a nineteenth-century ancestor bore the *laqab* al-Wahsh, "the wild beast," changed in 1927 to the nobler form.

The article distinguishes "Al" — a dynastic designation, as in Al Saud — from "Ahl," a broader term, as in *Ahl al-Bayt*, "People of the House [of the Prophet]," which Shia Muslims limit to Muhammad's cousin, daughter, son-in-law, two grandsons and eleven wives, while Sunni Muslims extend it to the whole of the Banu Hashim. Saudi Arabia, the author notes, is alone in retaining these articles in names, and because Arabic is written in its own alphabet, romanised spellings vary widely.

## Geography and the Middle East

The essay argues that geography is the foundation of historical understanding, and that british intelligence — and later what the author calls its protégés, the [[cia]] and [[mossad]] — have manipulated the Middle East for more than two hundred years, often as gamesmanship between rival factions inside the British imperial hierarchy. He calls the region's history among the most complicated and tragic in the world, second only to Russia's.

The author presents a map of his own making that defines Arabia as the 1916 Arab Revolt leaders King Hussein and Prince Faisal defined it, cross-checked against the membership of the arab league, and emphasises that Arabia is "the natural geographic axis of the entire region." He also reproduces a Vanity Fair map of the modern Middle East, adding that forty different maps would yield forty different designations "because all history is interpretation."

## The Bedouin

The bedouin — *badawī*, "desert dweller" — are described as the nomadic tribes of the Arabian peninsula, the Levant, Africa, Syria and Iraq, originating in the Syrian and Arabian deserts and spreading during the Golden Age of Islam. They are contrasted with the *ḥāḍir*, the sedentary population, though the article notes the general acceptance that all sedentary peoples were once nomads. It gives a global Bedouin population of about 25 million with roughly 2 million in Saudi Arabia, whose total population it puts at over 32 million.

The author dwells on what he calls the mystical aspect of Bedouin life: a decentralised, collaborative oral history in which tribes carry forward the stories of tribes that vanish, and which cannot be edited by any authority the way a centralised written record can. He offers as an example Bedouin accounts of ancient Babylon — the origins of blood magic and the gods worshipped there — which he says entered the Quran without corroboration in the Bible or older texts and were only confirmed by twentieth-century archaeology.

He finds in the Bedouin a parallel to the American spirit: fiercely independent, self-reliant, militant yet God-fearing, and pragmatic about trade and diplomacy. High-born Saudi boys, he writes, were traditionally sent to live with an allied Bedouin tribe on coming of age, much as American boys joined the Boy Scouts, and the most elite Saudi military units recruit directly from Bedouin tribes, organised by tribal affiliation to build camaraderie and to prevent feuds from degrading effectiveness. On traditional dress, he argues that desert peoples covered themselves against sun and windblown sand for thousands of years before Islam, and adds that the Prophet Muhammad had red hair and a fair complexion, as did many of his kin.

## Nimrod, Abraham, and the Kaaba

The article retells the confrontation between nimrod and abraham. Nimrod, great-grandson of Noah, is described across the three major religions and in Greek and Roman historians as a wicked Babylonian king who claimed divinity, promoted idolatry and blood magic, and built the Tower of Babel. Warned by astrologers that a descendant of Noah would end idolatry, he ordered the slaughter of newborns in Mesopotamia; Abraham's mother escaped to the wilderness to give birth.

Abraham later confronted Nimrod, was ordered burned at the stake, and emerged unscathed, prompting witnesses to accept him as a prophet. In the ensuing public debate Nimrod executed one prisoner and pardoned another to demonstrate his own power over life and death; Abraham replied, "Well, my lord God makes the sun rise from the East. Can you make it rise from the West?" Humiliated, Nimrod banished him. Abraham is said to have settled in Mecca and built a stone sanctuary on the foundation laid by Adam — the kaaba, considered by Islam mankind's first house of worship. The article presents the episode as the triumph of logic and reason over narcissism and as the archetype of monotheism against polytheism.

The essay also recounts the visitation of Abraham and Sarah by three angels, and the hospitality Abraham showed them — washing their feet and offering his best bread, milk and finest calf — as the model of hospitality urged on all believers.

## Hashim, the Quraysh, and the Prophet

The section on Arabian history opens with a Bedouin warrior, a *ḥanīf* and descendant of Abraham, entering Mecca more than 1,500 years ago. His grandfather qusayy ibn kilab had earlier united nomadic clans to retake the Kaaba from what the article calls a syndicate of godless criminals; the clans swore an oath and became the tribe of quraysh.

The warrior, born Amr, used his scimitar to defend Meccan villagers from raiders, organised bi-annual Bedouin merchant caravans to the Mediterranean to fund food and supplies against seasonal famine, and devised a broth mixed with crumbled bread that he distributed to pilgrims — a tradition of hospitality the article says continues in Mecca and was adopted by all great desert sheikhs. He became known as Hashim, a *kunya* meaning both "he who feeds the starving" and "the pulverizer of evil." His grandson was Muhammad.

## The Banu Hashim and early Islam

Hashim's and Muhammad's descendants became the Hashemites, the banu hashim, who the author says will be central to the next article's treatment of the First World War. A map of Arabian tribes in 600 AD shows the Quraysh holding Mecca and no settlement of significance at the site of modern Riyadh. The [[house-of-saud]], the article notes, descends from the Banu Bakr, counted by modern Arab scholars within the Hanifa tribe, though the Banu Bakr was a Christian clan before Muhammad and among the more significant resisters of his conquest.

Muhammad left no dictated line of succession. Most followers supported his companion Abu Bakr, and for thirty years abu bakr and three other companions were successively elected to lead the rashidun caliphate, which expanded from modern Libya to India and became the dominant military, cultural and economic power of West Asia. The Quran was written under Abu Bakr.

The article gives particular weight to the constitution of medina, written by Muhammad in 622, which it describes as a formal agreement between Muslims and the Jewish clans that had historically ruled Medina, establishing the city as a *haram* or sanctuary, prohibiting inter-clan violence and guaranteeing religious freedom to non-Muslims and specifically Jews. Muhammad, the article says, established similar protections in Jerusalem, where Christian authorities had banned Jewish worship — a record it presents as contradicting modern narratives about Islam, while conceding that his successors, especially under Wahhabism, were far less tolerant.

The fourth caliph, Ali, was raised by Muhammad from the age of five and was, at ten, the first to believe Muhammad's account of the visitation of the archangel Gabriel. Muhammad called him brother, guardian and successor, and once called him *Mawla*, a word variously rendered as lord, trustee, or helper. Dispute over that word underlies the split: those who held that only Ali's descendants could lead Islam became the Shia, roughly 10–15 percent of Muslims and concentrated in Iran and the wider Persian region; the Sunni majority holds that Muhammad appointed no heir and that the community should choose the leader best able to unite it under the *hadiths*.

## Islam's view of Jesus

A dedicated section argues that Western misconceptions about Islam and Jesus of Nazareth are the product of gaslighting. It states that Islam is the only religion besides Christianity to recognise Jesus — *Isa* — as a holy figure, that the Quran names him the penultimate prophet and messenger of God and devotes one of its four books, the *Injīl*, to him. It affirms the virgin birth, that his Father is God, that he was aided by believing disciples, and credits him with at least six miracles while noting that the Quran says Muhammad performed none. It further states that Jesus was betrayed by the Pharisees, saved by God and raised to rule the Kingdom of Heaven, and will return in a Second Coming to bring peace and justice. The article calls the belief that Islam and Christianity cannot coexist "a devious deception" whose twentieth-century propagation it promises to examine.

## Wahhabism

By the early 1700s, roughly 1,100 years after Muhammad's death, the article says Arabia had circled back to pre-Islamic idolatry: nominal Muslims who had deviated from the hadiths, worshipped hundreds of secular idols, and practised occult magic and mysticism. Into this setting came a young preacher from a family of jurists in the Najd, raised in strict orthodoxy, with a firebrand personality and a passion for what he regarded as restoration. He rejected *taqlid* — conformity or group-think — in favour of *ijtihad*, independent legal reasoning from scripture. His abrasiveness toward the status quo led his father and brother to excommunicate him. He became known as al-Wahhab, and although neither he nor his followers named their doctrine, the world calls it [[wahhabism]].

## Mohammed bin Saud and Diriyah

At the same moment, Mohammed bin Saud Al Muqrin — also abbreviated MBS in the article, and distinguished from the modern crown prince — was growing discontented in the Najd. The article stresses that he was neither a Bedouin nomad nor a tribal warlord but the *emir* of a small farming community outside Riyadh ("The Gardens"), named for the oases on which both settled farmers and passing nomads depended. It describes the symbiosis between oasis farmers and warlike nomads as the origin of government in the region.

The House of Saud is traced to the Banu Bakr and the Hanifa, a tribe pre-dating the Prophet. The article recounts the Banu Bakr's defeat by Muhammad in 627, when their Christian emir, tied to a pole and threatened with execution, refused to renounce Christ and instead recited a wisdom three times demonstrating his devotion to God and his fearlessness of death; Muhammad released and embraced him as a brother, and he later converted of his own volition.

Mohammed bin Saud became emir of Diriyah after the collapse of the previous ruling family, the al-Diriyah, in a power struggle that required him to fight and defeat all three of his own brothers in battle. He was also a capable desert traveller, a skill that won him the loyalty of the Bedouin — the article observes that where settled people saw savages, the Bedouin may have seen men who had lost their human spirit — and he organised and funded long-distance caravans that expanded his influence across the Najd.

## The Pact of Diriyah

Al-Wahhab, having travelled to Mecca, Medina and Basra in search of knowledge of the Prophet, returned repulsed by what he saw as Arabia's secular self-indulgence and settled in the oasis town of Uyayna near Diriyah. There he persuaded the emir to destroy the venerated tomb of a companion of the Prophet, to cut down trees worshipped as sacred, and to institute stoning for women who confessed to adultery. When word reached a more powerful emir who taxed Uyayna, al-Wahhab and his followers were expelled.

Mohammed bin Saud invited him to Diriyah, offering an alliance combining "might, reason, and faith." He set two conditions: that al-Wahhab settle in Diriyah as his advisor, and that he not oppose taxation. Al-Wahhab accepted the first and refused the second, arguing that taxation would create animosity between ruler and people and that more wealth would come from conquest of enemies than from bleeding one's own subjects. The article notes that no tax is imposed or collected by the Saudi government to this day.

The two men embraced as brothers. The article quotes Mohammed bin Saud: "This oasis is yours, do not fear your enemies. By the name of God, if all Nejd was summoned to throw you out, we will never agree to expel you." Al-Wahhab replied: "You are the settlement's chief and wise man. I want you to grant me an oath that you will perform jihad against the unbelievers. In return, you will be imam, leader of the Muslim community and I will be leader in religious matters." The alliance was sealed by a *bay'ah*, a mutual oath of loyalty, in 1744 — the [[pact-of-diriyah]] — and cemented by the marriage of bin Saud's son to al-Wahhab's daughter.

## The First Saudi State

The resulting polity, the [[emirate-of-diriyah]] or First State of Saud, was named in honour of Mohammed bin Saud's father, Saud bin Muhammad Al Muqrin. The article dates it from 1727 to 1814, when bin Saud's son and successor Abdullah attempted to take the Hashemite kingdom of Hejaz, held by the ottoman empire since the sixteenth century. Abdullah and his kinsmen were captured, taken to Istanbul and publicly beheaded, and Abdullah's head thrown into the Bosphorus.

## Conclusion and forward look

The essay closes under the heading "Evil does not create; It can only corrupt," arguing that Hashim, Muhammad, and the original Mohammed bin Saud were men of honour and reason rather than the ruthless ideologues the author says Western media has portrayed. The promised sequel is to cover the Hashemite Kingdom of Hejaz, the struggle for Arabian independence from the Ottomans, what the author calls the British Cabal's betrayal of the Hashemites, and the usurpation of Hashemite stewardship over the Islamic holy places through Abdulaziz bin Abdul Rahman Al Saud, founder of the modern kingdom and grandfather of the current crown prince. This, the author says, connects directly to the creation of the *Ikhwan* or Brotherhood, the export of Wahhabism across the Middle East, and the rise of radical Islamic terrorism in the later twentieth century. He states his belief that the modern MBS is "the Prodigal Son of the Islamic world," on a redemption arc to liberate the Middle East from what the corpus elsewhere calls the Puppet Masters.
