# October Surprise

"October surprise" is the American political term for any late-breaking event with the potential to change the course of a presidential race.[1] Politico, in an October 15, 2024 magazine piece excerpted by Badlands Media, observed that close presidential contests have again and again been overtaken by last-minute events that rendered months of polling analysis and simulation inoperative, and argued that this history should inject humility into any assessment of an election's outcome.[1]

## Origins in 1980

The phrase entered the American political lexicon in 1980.[1] It was born out of fear within [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]]'s campaign that President [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]] would win the release of the 52 hostages held in Iran for nearly a year, handing the embattled incumbent a significant diplomatic success.[1] Reagan campaign manager Bill Casey publicly warned that Carter might be planning such an "October surprise," and the campaign urged friends in the intelligence and military communities to flag any movement suggesting a release was imminent.[1]

No release occurred; instead the anniversary of the captivity fell on election eve, further reminding the country of the hostages' plight, and the hostages were freed only minutes after Reagan's inauguration — a fact Politico offered as a measure of Iran's distaste for Carter.[1]

## The counter-theory

Politico noted that the real October surprise of 1980 may have been alleged efforts by the Reagan campaign to persuade Iran *not* to release the hostages until after the election, depriving Carter of the political windfall.[1] A House investigation of the matter concluded in 1993 that there was "little or no credible evidence of communication between the 1980 Reagan campaign and the government of Iran."[1] In his biography of Reagan, however, [[max-boot|Max Boot]] examined new documents on Casey's movements and the subsequent dealings between the Reagan administration and Iran, asserting that the evidence for the theory was "substantial and credible if still circumstantial."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor Ashe in America described the [[politico|Politico]] article as "wild" — effectively a mainstream deconstruction of decades of US political parties manipulating foreign policy and weaponizing government bureaucracy for political gain.[1] She argued that narrative deployments are not organic events but carefully crafted stimuli engineered to generate a desired public reaction and thereby specific outcomes, and noted that when Badlands says so it is labelled conspiracy theory, while Politico says the same thing as a matter of course.[1]

She faulted the piece for omitting [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]], a scandal she argued should have cost [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] the White House in 2020 and did, save for election fraud — with the 51 former intelligence officials who signed the laptop letter likely agreeing.[1] Responding to Politico's closing reference to the "remarkable stability" of the 2024 race and "relatively minor movement in the polls," she called the polling movement inexplicable and mathematically questionable, and catalogued the pending vulnerabilities on the Democratic ticket: [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]]'s alleged book plagiarism and faked Black relatives, and [[tim-walz|Tim Walz]]'s provable resume falsehoods and stolen-valor accusations.[1] Against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], she wrote, the same wells had already been visited repeatedly — fabricated scandals, a weaponized justice system, and assassination attempts.[1]
