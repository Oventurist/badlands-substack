# 2026 Midterm Elections

The **2026 United States midterm elections** are the focus of intensive pre-election legal, procedural, and political preparation on both sides of the aisle, as reported in June 2026.[1]

## Democratic scenario planning

Politico reported that Senate Democrats were conducting private strategy sessions and scenario-planning exercises focused on potential election disruptions, foreign interference, federal law-enforcement activity at polling places, and post-election legal disputes.[1] Democratic lawmakers, election officials, and outside advisers were developing litigation, communications, and response plans, and complained of receiving limited election-security briefings from the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]].[1] Senate Minority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] convened experts including [[eric-holder|Eric Holder]], [[marc-elias|Marc Elias]], [[norm-eisen|Norm Eisen]], and the heads of [[democracy-forward|Democracy Forward]] and [[protect-democracy|Protect Democracy]] to prepare senators for three scenarios and coordinate with state and local officials.[1] Critics compared the effort to the 2020 [[shadow-cabal|"fortified" election]] described in *Time* magazine.[1]

## Postal ballot rules

The [[united-states-postal-service|United States Postal Service]] separately proposed new regulations governing mail-in and absentee ballots for federal elections, citing [[executive-order-14399|Executive Order 14399]] on election integrity.[1] The proposal would mandate USPS-approved envelope designs with the official Election Mail logo and unique Intelligent Mail barcodes on outbound and return envelopes, and require election officials to submit ballot-mailing and participation data through a new Federal Ballot Mail Portal.[1]

## Fundraising investigations

Congressional investigations into the online fundraising platforms [[actblue|ActBlue]] and [[winred|WinRed]] were also expected to continue into the election cycle, with donor-verification concerns remaining a live subject of debate.[1]

## Trump's cancellation remarks, January 2026

In a January 2026 Reuters interview, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] drew national attention by suggesting that the United States "shouldn't even have an election" that year because of what he described as the success of his administration — remarks his press secretary [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]] later characterized as made jokingly, saying he was referring to his view that the country was "on the right track."[2] In the same interview Trump acknowledged concerns within his party about potential losses in November, noting that historically the party of an incumbent president loses seats in [[us-congress|Congress]].[2]

Public opinion data ahead of the midterms indicated challenges for Republicans: a national poll aggregated by RealClearPolitics showed Democrats holding a narrow lead on the generic congressional ballot, with Democratic candidates at about 46 percent and Republicans at about 41 percent, while concurrent surveys placed Trump's job approval below 50 percent with higher disapproval in several recent polls.[2] Trump also emphasized policy initiatives in the run-up to the elections, including a $50 billion rural health plan aimed at addressing challenges in rural healthcare systems.[2] Separately, he told House Republicans in early January that the party must win the midterms or risk impeachment proceedings if Democrats retook control of Congress.[2] Control of the House and Senate after the elections will shape the final two years of Trump's presidency.[2]

## Badlands interpretation

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the same people who still support [[ukraine|Ukraine]] — which cancelled elections years ago "for democracy" and never looked back — were now very concerned about Trump cancelling the midterms.[2] She characterized the argument advanced by [[cnn|CNN]], based on work by the Congressional Research Service, as holding that cancelling fake elections is worse than elections being fake, which she dismissed as silly.[2] In her framing, fake elections are treason: they are not fake by accident but on purpose, with many participants, and the same people who have obstructed and character-assassinated those pointing this out are complicit in service of globalism.[2] She concluded that real elections are impossible until the public is honest about how fake they are, that accountability must fall on Americans rather than a foreign nation, and that any postponement by Trump and Congress would be in service of due process for those involved in [[2020-election-fraud-claims|fake elections]].[2]

## Democratic primary infighting

By March 2026 Democrats were locked in an expensive internal conflict ahead of the midterms, with more than $64 million already spent on House primaries where 30 incumbents faced well-funded challengers who had each raised at least $100,000, nearly a dozen of them out-raised.[3] Party leaders warned the infighting was draining resources needed for competitive general-election races to retake the House majority, while many insurgent and progressive candidates refused to commit to supporting Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]].[3] Early tests unfolded on March 3 in the North Carolina and Texas primaries, including challenges to Representatives Valerie Foushee, Al Green, and Julie Johnson, driven largely by generational change and anti-establishment frustration.[3] These dynamics are treated in detail at [[2026-democrat-primary-civil-war|the 2026 Democrat primary civil war]].[3]

## Foreign policy as an election issue

The [[iran-war|U.S.–Iran war]] became an early campaign issue, with Democrats condemning the strikes, pushing war powers restraints, and highlighting the risk of rising gas prices before the midterms.[3] Scrutiny of Democrats' ties to [[israel|Israel]] and to AIPAC intensified in primaries in Illinois, Michigan, and Texas amid a base shifting toward sympathy with Palestinians.[3]

## Republican fractures and the "midterm trap" critique

By June 2026 attention had shifted to divisions on the Republican side, with [[donald-trump|President Trump]] calling a small group of Republican lawmakers who opposed portions of his agenda "unpatriotic" and "grandstanders" after [[united-states-senate|Senate]] votes on spending and executive authority in which several joined Democrats.[4] Most congressional Republicans continued to support the administration's major legislative priorities, and defections remained limited enough for Trump-backed measures to advance.[4]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that framing the movement's future around capturing or holding the 2026 midterms is "the same trap as 2022, repackaged," since the results of another engineered cycle cannot restore what was never fully present.[4] In his view, treating the midterms as decisive reduces the project of American sovereignty to the base function of electioneering and hands initiative back to the very mechanisms that produced systemic tyranny, while those in the [[truth-community|Truth Community]] who keep pointing to the structural falsity of American elections are mocked for refusing to outsource discernment to partisan outcomes.[4]


## Election integrity mobilisation and public distrust

In May 2026 President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] announced that Republicans would field an [[election-integrity-army|Election Integrity Army]] for the midterms that would be "much bigger and stronger" than the network he said the party deployed in every state in 2024, framing it as a counter to a Democrat-led "Election Integrity Group" involving [[eric-holder|Eric Holder]] and [[marc-elias|Marc Elias]] under [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]], and declaring that "this Election will be fair."[5] Badlands commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the midterms might instead expose systemic corruption in US elections and deliver a reckoning, since a fair election cannot be run on the same systems.[5]

A [[2026-politico-stolen-election-poll|Politico-highlighted poll]] published the same month found that only about 40% of respondents thought the 2026 midterms were unlikely to be stolen, and only about 39% said without reservation that the 2020 election was not stolen, with Republicans and Democrats citing opposite mechanisms of theft.[5]

## Messaging debates in both parties (March 2026)

By March 2026 both major parties were publicly arguing over how to campaign in the midterms.[6] [[republican-party|Republican]] strategists and officials were divided over whether President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] should continue emphasizing [[2020-election-fraud-claims|claims about the 2020 election]] or pivot to current policy issues, with multiple officials telling [[politico|Politico]] that a continued focus on election fraud could distract from policy ahead of the midterms; others viewed election integrity as important to many Republican voters.[6]

On the [[democratic-party|Democratic]] side, strategist [[jim-messina|Jim Messina]] told [[axios|Axios]] that the midterms would be "85-90% driven by voter opposition to Trump and maybe 10-15% based on what Dems stand for," a calculation he warned could not be repeated in 2028.[6] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed both anxieties as evidence that the underlying systems remain unreformed, arguing that "no one wins a fake election."[6]

## Socialist primary insurgency (July 2026)

House Democrats began reassessing their political strategy after Rep. [[diana-degette|Diana DeGette]] of Colorado lost her primary to [[justice-democrats|Justice Democrats]]-backed challenger [[melat-kiros|Melat Kiros]], an upset party members described as a warning that even established progressive incumbents are vulnerable from the left.[7] Progressive organizations pledged to keep targeting Democratic incumbents, while reports indicated [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] had renewed outreach to donors and allies as she weighed her next moves.[7]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] predicted the midterms would be "hilarious," writing that "around the nation, the commies are winning," and described the Colorado primary as one in which voters chose change down ballot while retaining party leadership — [[phil-weiser|Phil Weiser]] winning the gubernatorial nomination and [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] the nomination for attorney general.[7] On the Republican side she disputed the reported result of a contested Colorado primary, arguing the winner had no momentum, no ground game, no polling, no policy depth, refused to debate except with DSA moderators, and had campaign finance violations and amended FEC filings, while holding connections to the same law firm as the Republican establishment, [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]], and his opponent.[7] She linked the candidate to a "weird operation" with [[candace-owens|Candace Owens]] and noted he was described as [[charlie-kirk|Charlie Kirk]]'s mentor.[7]


## Red Mirage framing

Ahead of the midterms, media outlets revived the 2020-era [[red-mirage|Red Mirage]] framing — the pattern in which Republicans lead on election night before Democrats prevail as late mail ballots are counted — as an explanation for public distrust in elections.[8] Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the pattern was already visible in the California primaries and would be run again at the midterms, alongside a Glenn Beck / Billy Bush media operation she described as a rerun of the film *Rigged*, whose call to action was ballot chasing and legal reform rather than genuine election security.[8]

## Legitimacy as the central question (January 2026)

Entering 2026, Badlands framed the midterms primarily as a contest over legitimacy.[9] Republicans held a narrow majority in the US House of Representatives, and political analysts raised the possibility that House control could change before the elections through special elections, member resignations and vacancies.[9] Former Republican National Committee chairman and White House chief of staff [[reince-priebus|Reince Priebus]] predicted President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] would personally involve himself — "He likes to have his fingerprints on things" — while conceding that "nothing makes sense anymore" after a string of cycles that defied forecasts from 2016 through 2024.[9]

Election integrity dominated the conversation. Responding to a claim that states without voter ID requirements are sixteen times more likely to offer welfare benefits to illegal immigrants, [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] wrote, "Yes, at massive scale! It is the biggest vote-buying fraud scheme in the history of America."[9] Researcher [[peter-bernegger|Peter Bernegger]] reported that the [[wisconsin-elections-commission|Wisconsin Elections Commission]]'s motion to dismiss had been denied in *[[janel-brandtjen|Janel Brandtjen]] v. Wisconsin Elections Commission, [[meagan-wolfe|Meagan Wolfe]]*, opening full discovery and a planned deposition of [[david-becker|David Becker]] over allegations that [[electronic-registration-information-center|ERIC]] unlawfully transfers voters' personal data to Becker's Center for Election Innovation and Research.[9]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued ERIC states are intentionally padding voter rolls, citing the 2022 Colorado episode under Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] in which postcards encouraging voting were mailed to nearly 30,000 foreign nationals, only three of whom were eligible, and sworn testimony from the state's deputy secretary of state that ERIC forwards non-citizens as "eligible but unauthorized" voters.[9] Her conclusion — "Fake Voters. Fake Ballots. Fake Counts. Fake Elections." — was paired with the argument that the People have been building an evidentiary record since 2020 and that the time for justice had arrived.[9] On [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]] episode 156, [[canncon|CannCon]] hosted former Maricopa County Recorder [[stephen-richer|Stephen Richer]] and Election Oversight's [[kevin-moncla|Kevin Moncla]] to discuss Georgia's 2020 election and 315,000 unsigned poll closing tapes.[9] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] took a harder line still, writing "We chose the hose. We chose 2024," and arguing that Americans could have admitted elections are fake and taken a different approach but had "not learned the basic lessons of 2020."[9]

## Trump's dismissal of the midterms

At a cabinet meeting in late May 2026, President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said of [[iran|Iran]] that its leadership "thought they were going to out wait me, you know, we'll out wait him, he's got the midterms," adding, "I don't care about the midterms, look what happened last night, that was the prelude to the midterms."[10] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] interpreted the remark as reflecting a set of premises: that elections are fake, that the GOP establishment is Trump's actual enemy, that the system cannot be defeated from within its controlled framework, that Congress and the Senate are illegitimate — or at minimum feckless and useless — and that the United States is post-constitutional or "retro-constitutional."[10] He added that nearly all of MAGA still refuses to grasp what Trump is saying, often because supporters are heavily incentivized to protect the system in opposition to Trump.[10]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] endorsed that reading and offered the [[colorado|Colorado]] gubernatorial contest as illustration, calling the pageantry of democracy a bad reality TV show.[10] She noted a field including state representative and pastor Scott Bottoms, who claims there are pedophile rings in state government; state Senator Barb Kirkmeyer, who excoriated Bottoms for those claims and whose former staffer was arrested for pedophilia; and Victor Marx, who claims to have decapitated a cat at age three and been forced to wear its carcass.[10] She argued that if elections were real a Republican could win Colorado, given that 51% of the state has defected from the two-party system, yet the main debate topic remains the roads and whether fraud is fraud or merely mismanagement.[10] Her conclusion was that the midterms can be entertaining so long as one does not treat them as real, since doing so prolongs collective pain.[10]

## Election integrity coverage, December 2025

Ahead of the midterms, Global News reported that President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s popularity was slumping in the polls and that analysts warned he could change the election process, including how ballots are cast, with correspondent Reggie Cecchini explaining what officials were bracing for and how the situation could test American democracy.[11] Badlands host [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] responded to the segment by quipping that "the best way to spread Christmas cheer is talking about election fraud for all to hear."[11]

Badlands host [[canncon|CannCon]] used the same coverage to restate his long-held position that the 2020 election was stolen, writing that over five years he had concluded that "NONE of elections are legitimate" and that the problem probably extends back at least 25 years, and that after years of social media and financial deplatforming and ridicule from [[mockingbird-media|Mockingbird Media]] outlets, "vindication is upon us."[11]

## Reform timeline pressure (February 2025)

Election analyst [[seth-keshel|Seth Keshel]] used the 2026 midterms as the fixed deadline in a February 13, 2025 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay applying [[smart-goal-setting|SMART goal setting]] to election reform, observing that one-eighth of the period between [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 2024 victory and the November 2026 races had already elapsed while Trump had been back in office barely three weeks.[12] He estimated that a 90-day [[election-reform-committee|Election Reform Committee]] cycle, three weeks of presidential deliberation, 90 days of injunctions and hearings, and 90 days of Supreme Court review would consume close to 300 days — reaching December 10, 2025, less than eleven months before Election Day 2026 and less than ten months before [[early-voting|early voting]] began.[12] He warned that if grand-scale overhaul did not begin as soon as humanly possible, reformers would have to give up on 2026 and pivot to protecting the 2028 presidential election.[12]
