# Agenda-Setting Theory

Agenda-setting theory holds that mass media, while often unable to dictate the conclusions audiences reach, is highly effective at determining which subjects audiences consider at all. The formulation most often cited comes from the political scientist Bernard C. Cohen, who wrote in his 1963 book *The Press and Foreign Policy* that the press "may not be successful much of the time in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling its readers what to think about."[1]

## Use in Badlands media criticism

Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] invoked Cohen's formulation in analysing the saturation coverage of [[alexei-navalny|Alexei Navalny]]'s death in February 2024, arguing that the primary function of the modern mainstream media is not informing the public but shaping civil discourse, and that cable news anchors function less as journalists than as social engineers.[1] He treated the uniform emotional intensity and identical talking points across outlets and across both political parties as a signature of coordinated messaging in pursuit of a specific objective — in this case, he argued, unblocking the $60 billion Ukraine aid package stalled in [[congress|Congress]].[1]

DeLarme applied the same lens to the Politico anecdote of a Ukrainian soldier in a muddy trench scrolling his phone for news of a U.S. House vote, describing such imagery as classic war propaganda designed to direct public attention and emotion toward a predetermined policy outcome.[1] He contrasted the attention devoted to Navalny with the near-silence surrounding the deaths of [[gonzalo-lira|Gonzalo Lira]] and others in states friendly to the Western establishment, presenting the disparity as evidence of agenda selection rather than news judgment.[1]
