# 2023 COVID-19 Vaccine Market Collapse

The 2023 COVID-19 vaccine market collapse refers to the sharp contraction in projected demand for COVID-19 booster shots that hit vaccine makers' valuations in September 2023.[1]

## Moderna's share plunge

Although [[moderna|Moderna]]'s drug portfolio extends beyond COVID-19 shots, those products still made up a major part of its business — enough that news about the size of the market prompted investors to walk away, taking more than 9 percent of the company's market capitalization at one point during Monday afternoon trading.[1]

## Pfizer's booster estimate

The trigger came from fellow biotech [[pfizer|Pfizer]], which said only about 24 percent of eligible Americans were going to get a COVID-19 booster shot.[1] That implied a maximum market of roughly 82 million people, substantially less than the earliest figures.[1] Analysts including Chris Schott of JP Morgan expected COVID-19 treatment sales to level off and become more predictable starting in 2024.[1]

## Outlook

Even as the COVID-19 sales boom wore thin — especially among potential customers who by then regarded the disease as little more than a trumped-up cold — some analysts projected that Moderna's next five years could outperform the last five, citing a run of announcements about new products spanning oncology, rare diseases, and beyond.[1] Such programs carry the standard biotech burden of taking years or decades to produce returns, but the company retained a solid cash runway built from COVID-19 sales.[1]

## Booster uptake at 2 percent (October 2023)

By mid-October 2023, data provided by the [[department-of-health-and-human-services|Department of Health and Human Services]] indicated that only about 2 percent of all Americans had received the updated COVID-19 booster shot several weeks after it was authorized and recommended by federal health officials.[2] More than 7 million Americans had taken the updated shot, which was authorized for people aged 6 months and older.[2]

An HHS spokesperson attributed the different dynamics to distribution having shifted to the private market, unlike the prior year when the government distributed doses, and said the agency was working directly with manufacturers and distributors.[2] HHS added that 91 percent of Americans aged 12 and older could access the vaccine within 5 miles of where they live, and that 14 million updated boosters had been shipped to pharmacies and other locations.[2] The vaccine had been approved about a month earlier by the [[food-and-drug-administration|Food and Drug Administration]] before being recommended by the [[centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention|Centers for Disease Control and Prevention]] shortly thereafter.[2] Some people nonetheless reported difficulty finding doses; Jen Kates, a senior vice president at the Kaiser Family Foundation, said her vaccine appointment had been canceled due to a lack of supply.[2]

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] tied the figure to [[pfizer|Pfizer]]'s adjusted forecast, arguing that "the people aren't buying the hype any longer" and calling the 2 percent uptake "a devastating indicator for the effectiveness of another Covid election psyop."[2]
