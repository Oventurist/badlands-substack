# United States v. Google Antitrust Trial

The United States government's antitrust case against [[google|Google]] over the dominance of its search engine went to trial in September 2023 in a federal courtroom in Washington, D.C.[1] Reporting described it as the biggest U.S. antitrust trial since regulators pursued [[microsoft|Microsoft]] over its dominance of personal computer software a quarter century earlier.[1]

## The case

The government's attack targeted what was characterised as an indomitable empire: Google's ubiquitous search engine, which had become the internet's main gateway.[1] The trial was scheduled to run ten weeks before U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta.[1] Observers expected potentially revelatory testimony from top executives at Google and its corporate parent [[alphabet|Alphabet]], as well as from other powerful technology companies.[1]

Alphabet chief executive Sundar Pichai, who succeeded Google co-founder [[larry-page|Larry Page]] in 2019, was among the most prominent expected witnesses.[1] Court documents also indicated that Eddy Cue, one of [[apple|Apple]]'s highest-ranking executives, might be called to the stand.[1] Commentators noted that the case mirrored the Microsoft action in many ways, including the existential threat it posed to a renowned technology giant whose products are relied upon by billions of people.[1]

## Commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]], writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], argued that while the contemporaneous Trump prosecutions had absorbed most public attention, the Google trial could prove one of the most impactful in history.[1] He emphasised that the investigation had been initiated under [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s [[justice-department|Department of Justice]] and continued under the Biden administration, suggesting that Big Tech was having a rough time "under regime control" and inviting readers to find the continuity curious.[1]

## August 2024 ruling and remedies phase

On August 5, 2024, [[amit-mehta|Judge Amit Mehta]] ruled that [[google|Google]] had illegally monopolized the online search and search text advertising markets.[2] Following the ruling, the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] began weighing measures to restore competition, and [[bloomberg|Bloomberg]] reported that a breakup of the company was under consideration — the first significant attempt to break up a firm for monopolization since the failed effort against [[microsoft|Microsoft]] two decades earlier.[2] Less drastic options included forcing Google to share more data with competitors and imposing safeguards against unfair advantage in AI, while a ban on the exclusive contracts central to the government's case was expected in any scenario.[2] A breakup would likely target the Android operating system and Chrome browser for divestment, with a possible forced sale of AdWords.[2] Google said it would appeal, but Mehta directed both parties to prepare for the remedies phase.[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] took what she called an unpopular position against the breakup, objecting to Mehta's involvement, to the Biden DOJ deciding which parts of Google survived, and to placing Search, AdWords, Play and Cloud — "core enablers of tyranny in the hands of a criminal regime" — under such an administration's control.[2] She noted the government's position was tenuous, having failed against Microsoft when it was, in her framing, "less overtly tyrannical."[2]

## Ad-tech trial, September 2024

A second landmark [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] case, charging [[google|Google]] with violating antitrust laws in its ad-tech business, went to trial in a federal court in Virginia on Monday, September 9, 2024.[3] Axios noted that the stakes were elevated because Google had already lost a major antitrust case earlier that year, when a federal judge ruled that the company illegally abused its search monopoly; a second loss could force sweeping changes to its core business as it competes for leadership in the AI era.[3]

The Justice Department had sued in early 2023, alleging that the company engaged "in a systematic campaign to seize control of the wide swath of high-tech tools used by publishers, advertisers, and brokers, to facilitate digital advertising," and then used its resulting dominance to bar competitors from the market.[3] Google contested the charges, arguing that the DOJ was "doubling down on a flawed argument that would slow innovation, raise advertising fees, and make it harder for thousands of small businesses and publishers to grow."[3]

## Google's December 2024 remedies counterproposal

The [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]]'s list of proposed remedies for Google's illegal antitrust behavior in the search market began with forcing the company to sell Chrome, and late on a Friday in December 2024 [[google|Google]] responded with a proposal of its own.[4] Rather than breaking off Chrome, Android or Google Play as the DOJ filing contemplated, Google's proposed fixes targeted the payments it makes to companies such as Apple and Mozilla for exclusive, prioritized placement of its services, its licensing deals with Android handset makers, and its contracts with wireless carriers.[4] The counterproposal did not address a DOJ suggestion that Google might be forced to share its valuable search data with competitors to help their products catch up.[4]

Google regulatory vice president Lee-Anne Mulholland wrote on the company blog that because the ruling concerned search distribution contracts — arrangements with Apple and Mozilla for their browsers, Android phone makers and wireless carriers — the proposed remedies were directed to that.[4] For three years, the proposal would block Google from signing deals linking licenses for Chrome, Search and the Google Play app store with placement or preinstallation of its other apps, including Chrome, Google Assistant or the [[google-gemini|Gemini]] AI assistant.[4] It would still allow Google to pay for default search placement in browsers while permitting multiple deals across different platforms or browsing modes and requiring that the deals be revisitable at least once a year.[4]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued from personal experience that the DOJ "cannot be trusted at all," citing a statement of interest and an amicus brief the department filed in her own court case that she said sided with an unconstitutional interpretation of two civil rights statutes and the First Amendment.[4] She contended that Google search is not a true monopoly because the search market is saturated and Google, while dominant, is not the only player, and that the federal government should not have authority to force Google to divest of search.[4] She considered even Google's own proposed agreement to go too far, though less offensive to the concept of free markets than the government's proposal.[4] Modern narrators, she wrote, have convinced Americans that federal oversight exists to prevent companies from rigging markets, but the federal government is itself possibly the most corrupt, wasteful, fraudulent and abusive entity in the realm, and the corrupt will never hold themselves accountable.[4] She concluded that the case should not move forward until [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] takes her seat as Attorney General and a full DOJ case review has occurred, and that the lame-duck DOJ — whose interpretation of justice was roundly rejected by voters — must be refused in its final weeks.[4]
