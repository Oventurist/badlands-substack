# Fourteenth Amendment

The Fourteenth Amendment to the [[united-states-constitution|United States Constitution]] is the Reconstruction-era amendment whose Citizenship Clause — guaranteeing citizenship to all persons "born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof" — is the textual foundation of birthright citizenship in American law. In the Badlands corpus it appears as the battleground of the 2026 legal dispute over President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order seeking to limit birthright citizenship.

## The Citizenship Clause and the 2026 challenge

The executive order challenged in [[trump-v-barbara|Trump v. Barbara]] interprets the amendment as guaranteeing citizenship only to persons born in the United States and "subject to the jurisdiction thereof." Lower federal courts blocked the order and related efforts, finding that the Constitution's text and long-standing precedent — principally [[united-states-v-wong-kim-ark|United States v. Wong Kim Ark]] — protect birthright citizenship regardless of the parents' immigration status.

During oral argument before the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] on April 1, 2026, Justice [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]] pressed the question of whether a child born in the United States to foreign-national parents — who is automatically a national of the parents' country and owes that country military service — is "subject to a foreign power," while Justice [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]] argued that language in the Court's opinions sometimes functions as public relations "to help the public accept the outcome of this case."

## Related Reconstruction amendments

The Badlands Brief of April 2, 2026 links the amendment to the broader Reconstruction settlement, noting that the [[fifteenth-amendment|Fifteenth Amendment]] — prohibiting denial of the right to vote based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude — was ratified on April 1, 1870, exactly 156 years before the SCOTUS argument. Badlands commentary by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] and Chris Paul treats the Reconstruction amendments as the bedrock of the legal system whose precedent language, they argue, was at times a "public relations effort to calm and distract the public."
