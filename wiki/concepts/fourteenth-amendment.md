# Fourteenth Amendment

The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution is a Reconstruction-era amendment whose Citizenship Clause extends citizenship to persons born in the United States and "subject to the jurisdiction thereof."[1] That qualifying phrase became the focus of litigation in 2026.[1]

## The Jurisdiction Clause Dispute

President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s executive order limiting [[birthright-citizenship|birthright citizenship]] reads the "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" language as excluding certain US-born children of non-citizen parents.[1] Lower federal courts rejected that reading, finding that the amendment's text and long-standing precedent protect birthright citizenship regardless of parents' immigration status.[1] The [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] took up the question in [[trump-v-barbara|Trump v. Barbara]], with the controlling precedent being [[united-states-v-wong-kim-ark|United States v. Wong Kim Ark]].[1]

During oral argument Justice [[samuel-alito|Samuel Alito]] suggested that a US-born child who automatically acquires another state's nationality — and its duty of military service — might be considered "subject to a foreign power" and thus outside the clause.[1]

## Reconstruction Context

Commentary in Badlands Media framed the amendment as part of the Reconstruction amendments alongside the [[fifteenth-amendment|Fifteenth Amendment]], and argued that the accompanying body of judicial precedent had functioned partly as public relations to calm the public during a period critics describe as an overthrow of the original constitutional order.[1]

## Section 3 and the 2023 Trump disqualification cases

In 2023, Section 3 of the amendment — the Reconstruction-era clause barring former Confederates from holding office — became the basis for a nationwide wave of challenges seeking to disqualify [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from the 2024 presidential ballot, culminating in the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]]'s ruling removing him from the Colorado ballot.[2] Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] noted that the underlying theory had previously been rejected by jurists in various states, including many Democrats.[2]

The litigation drew heavily on the historical figure of [[jefferson-davis|Jefferson Davis]]; during the Colorado trial a significant portion of the discussion was devoted to Davis, and the court's ruling reflected the view that the comparison to Trump was apt.[2] [[new-york-times|New York Times]] editorial board member [[mara-gay|Mara Gay]] extended the analogy on [[msnbc|MSNBC]], asking critics of the ruling, "Why are you standing with confederates who betrayed this country?" and arguing that Americans after the Civil War "clearly saw a threat in ex-confederates running for office. So much so they amended the Constitution to prevent those traitors from running for office."[2] Badlands Media writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that this rhetoric implied January 6 had been a civil war and predicted such framing would escalate through 2024.[2]

Section 3 also underpinned the roughly two dozen ballot-removal suits filed by Texas candidate [[john-anthony-castro|John Anthony Castro]], which argued that anyone who engaged in insurrection against the United States is barred from being a presidential candidate.[3] Those suits were dismissed in state after state — including West Virginia, where Judge [[irene-berger|Irene Berger]] found Castro lacked standing, and Arizona — while the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] declined in October 2023 to hear his Florida appeal.[3] In the [[colorado-ballot-disqualification-case|Colorado case]], constitutional commentator [[horace-cooper|Horace Cooper]] predicted the Supreme Court would resolve the matter by holding that the Section 3 disqualification clause does not apply to the presidency, thereby avoiding the insurrection question entirely.[3]

## The Maine disqualification and the insurrection predicate

At the end of 2023 the amendment's Section 3 disqualification clause was invoked administratively by Maine Secretary of State [[shenna-bellows|Shenna Bellows]], who removed [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] from [[maine|Maine]]'s 2024 primary ballot without a criminal conviction or adversarial adjudication.[4] Critics argued the precedent allowed officials to remove candidates on accusation alone under a "guilty until proven innocent" standard, and warned it could later be applied to any conservative candidate framed as a "threat to Democracy."[4]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the key issue in the Section 3 cases was not ballot access but the building-block findings of fact: the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]]'s affirmation that January 6 was an insurrection is what enabled the Maine action, and could, if left uncorrected by the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] in [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]], bar military-veteran January 6 attendees such as [[ron-hanks|Ron Hanks]] from running for office.[4]

## Twenty-seven-state amicus brief (January 2024)

In early January 2024, twenty-seven states filed with the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] to oppose the Section 3 theory underlying [[colorado|Colorado]]'s disqualification of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], an unusual posture in which states argued against the expansion of their own authority vis-à-vis [[congress|Congress]].[5] The brief was led by the attorneys general of Indiana and West Virginia, joined by twenty-five other states, and warned the Court that the novel theory would produce "chaos" in the country.[5]

Quoting Evenwel v. Abbott and Purcell v. Gonzalez, the brief argued that "the Colorado Supreme Court has cast itself into a 'political thicket' ... and it is now up to this Court to pull it out," contending that if the Colorado decision stood, confidence in the integrity of electoral processes would be harmed and many Americans would conclude "that a few partisan actors have contrived to take a political decision out of ordinary voters' hands."[5] Commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]], who highlighted the filing, wrote that it reinforced the view of states such as Colorado as outliers in embracing what he called an anti-democratic theory.[5]

Polling at the time showed a deeply divided public: a majority of Americans opposed the Colorado and Maine disqualification decisions in some surveys, while others put the balance slightly in favor.[5] The Maine decision was set for review by that state's courts, while oral argument in the Colorado case was scheduled within weeks.[5] Ashe in America argued the Colorado high court's overreach was capturing the national conversation across narrative layers and predicted the Supreme Court would rule before Colorado's primary on Super Tuesday, March 5, 2024.[5]

## The multi-state disqualification campaign (October 2023)

By the end of October 2023 the Section 3 theory was being litigated simultaneously in several states.[6] A group of Colorado voters sought to keep [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] off the 2024 ballot under what was described as the Constitution's post-Civil War "insurrectionist ban," while Trump filed a lawsuit to shut down a similar case in Michigan and a major hearing in a Minnesota candidacy challenge was scheduled for November 2.[6] Observers emphasized that although the amendment disqualifies officials who took an oath to uphold the Constitution and later "engaged in insurrection" or aided the country's enemies, the Constitution provides no enforcement mechanism, and the clause has been applied only twice since the 19th century — grounds on which many experts regarded the challenges as a legal long shot.[6]

## Section 3 challenges to Trump's ballot eligibility (2023)

Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment, which bars from federal or state office those who engaged in insurrection after swearing an oath to support the Constitution, was invoked in more than a dozen states to challenge [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 2024 candidacy over his conduct surrounding the January 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol.[7] Enacted after the Civil War and designed to exclude former Confederate civil and military officials from office, the rarely used disqualification clause was argued before the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]] on December 6, 2023 in [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]].[7] A threshold question was whether the president is an "Officer" within the meaning of Section 3, and the Colorado argument featured extended discussion of Confederate president [[jefferson-davis|Jefferson Davis]].[7] The outcome was expected to affect 22 other Section 3 challenges around the country and to be appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court.[7]
