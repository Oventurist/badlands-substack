# Trust, but verify

"Trust, but verify" is a proverb popularised during the mid-1980s nuclear arms negotiations between President [[ronald-reagan|Ronald Reagan]] and Soviet President [[mikhail-gorbachev|Mikhail Gorbachev]].[1] A July 11, 2024 Badlands Media essay on the exploitation of trust treats it as one of three competing postures toward trust, alongside the implicit "trust, don't verify" of everyday institutional deference and the stricter "don't trust, verify" of modern cryptography and cybersecurity.[1]

## Origin and meaning

The saying essentially meant, in the essay's gloss of a published account, "Sure, we'll approach this in good faith, but we're going to double-check to see that you've done what you promised."[1] It thereby recognises that placing trust is inevitable while also acknowledging the dangers that attend it, and demands complete verification prior to trust being extended.[1]

## Criticism

The essay's objection is practical: in the maxim's real-world application, initial-only verification and infrequent re-verification are common vulnerabilities that get exploited.[1] Trust extended without ongoing evidence and investigation outsources thinking, decision-making and personal responsibility, substantially increasing the risk of exploitation.[1]

## "Never trust, always verify"

Three and a half decades after Reagan and Gorbachev, the essay argues, technology and business have become inextricably linked and the headline becomes "Never trust, always verify."[1] It quotes consulting commentary that the current cybersecurity and ransomware landscape makes a "guilty until proven innocent" posture the only reasonable approach to system access; that systems will be more secure with a default policy of denying access until trustworthiness is proved, rather than the other way around; and that constantly verifying everyone's access "has become the only safe way to continue."[1] This is the same logic the essay finds in [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]], which substitutes cryptographic proof for trusted third parties, and in the [[proof-of-work|proof-of-work]] argument of [[softwar|Softwar]].[1] Compare [[zero-trust|zero trust]].[1]
