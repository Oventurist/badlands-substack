# Deposit Insurance

Federal deposit insurance guarantees depositors' balances at insured banks and thrifts. In the account of Badlands Media contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]], it is also the mechanism that made the [[savings-and-loan-crisis|savings and loan crisis]] both possible and inevitable.[1]

Financial writer [[martin-mayer|Martin Mayer]], in *The Greatest-Ever Bank Robbery: The Collapse of the Savings and Loan Industry*, wrote that "deposit insurance has proved to be the crack cocaine of American finance" — a line Lange holds sheds more light on the disaster than any other book on the scandal.[1] Mayer's focus was the forest rather than the trees: not the crooks who took over the industry but Washington, where a combination of naiveté, ineptitude, bad faith and cravenness made crookedness likely and inevitable.[1] The government's [[deregulation|deregulation]] of a once closely regulated industry was bad enough, but by raising federal deposit insurance on individual accounts from $40,000 to $100,000 in 1980 it guaranteed that the new "entrepreneurial" owners of S&Ls were taking no risk when they gambled with depositors' money; the government became the ultimate safety net and the industry a giant Ponzi scheme.[1]

Lange extends the argument: most people believe deposit insurance is good because it protects the little guy, but in his reading it ultimately protects "the untouchables, who are above the law," by placing financial responsibility on taxpayers.[1] The American people, he writes, are the backstop for every major financial crisis, and that is no accident — the protection of the little guy is "just a narrative."[1] The pattern recurred throughout the crisis: when [[mario-renda|Mario Renda]]'s 130 thrifts failed, depositors were promptly repaid their federally insured deposits at public expense.[1]
