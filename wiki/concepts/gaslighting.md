# Gaslighting

**Gaslighting** is a term for psychological manipulation in which a person's perception of reality is repeatedly questioned or overridden by another party. In a May 22, 2023 essay for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] titled "Gaslighting From All Sides," contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] adopted the technical definition of gaslighting as "the subjective experience of having one's reality repeatedly questioned by another," while offering his own working description: a crazy person telling a sane person that the sane person is the one who is crazy.[1]

## Carlson's expanded definition

Carlson argued that gaslighting extends beyond one-on-one abuse into mass communication, describing it as an intentional showcasing of a subject matter, usually performed in unison by the media, in order to influence audiences and change their beliefs.[1] In his framing, the persuasive force comes from apparent consensus: the more people who appear to support an idea, the more believable and legitimate that idea seems, and the initial idea may not be the endgame but rather a stepping stone that conditions the audience to accept a larger claim later.[1] He used the contested assertion that men can menstruate as an example of a proposition that gains plausibility through sheer repetition rather than evidence.[1]

## Uses and directions

Carlson maintained that gaslighting is directionally neutral as a technique. It is generally deployed by the media to push fear and control and to reshape belief systems to fit a narrative and agenda, but it can also be turned back on the people pushing false narratives.[1] He listed its possible effects as keeping people asleep, keeping them in fear, altering their beliefs and morals, making them "blackpilled," or conversely waking them up and exposing lies.[1] The essay's subtitle, "Annoying, but Necessary," reflects his conclusion that gaslighting directed at an unaware public is an unpleasant but justified instrument of awakening.[1]

## Application to the 2023 border debate

The central case study in the essay was the sudden surge of attention to the southern border in the days surrounding the expiration of [[title-42|Title 42]] in May 2023.[1] Carlson observed that the issue had not been prominent two weeks or two months earlier, and asked why it abruptly became the country's biggest concern only two days before the policy lapsed.[1] He proposed a three-way answer: the [[deep-state|Deep State]] does not want the public to know how bad the border is; "black-pillers" want to use the situation to instill fear and hopelessness; and [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and "the Patriots" are using it to wake people up.[1]

In Carlson's account, Trump and the Patriots were themselves gaslighting the border situation, exaggerating it roughly tenfold — bad, in his view, but not as grave as portrayed — precisely in order to draw attention to it.[1] He argued the alternative would be to let the border deteriorate until it genuinely matched the exaggerated description, an outcome he considered worse.[1] He credited the exaggeration with pressuring Congress toward a serious border-protection policy that Democrats would be forced to support once their own constituents, also gaslit, began demanding it.[1]

## Countervailing narratives

Carlson treated the claim that white supremacy is the nation's greatest threat — voiced by [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] in a commencement address at [[howard-university|Howard University]] and echoed by Homeland Security Secretary [[alejandro-mayorkas|Alejandro Mayorkas]] — as a competing act of gaslighting intended to divert attention from the border.[1] He argued the narrative was internally illogical, since minorities were risking their lives to enter a country supposedly saturated with racism, and noted that Asian Americans, the highest-earning demographic group in the United States, are rarely discussed by the Left because they complicate the white supremacy framing.[1]

He extended the analysis to corporate marketing, contending that Bud Light, Sports Illustrated, Miller Lite and Adidas were being made to push revenue-losing narratives so that the public would perceive society as worse off than it is and be motivated to act.[1] His summary image was that people do not wake from a whisper across the room but from an annoying alarm clock.[1]

## Reading the message

Carlson closed with a practical heuristic: recognize when a message is aimed at you and when it is aimed at others.[1] Messages meant for the already-aware are straightforward and accurate, while messages meant to penetrate the mind control of the sleeping are exaggerated; mistaking the latter for the former leads to being overwhelmed and losing hope.[1] He predicted that the economy would likely worsen but not to the catastrophic degree being advertised, and that Trump and the Patriots would not permit a nuclear war even though the sleeping needed to believe their politicians might cause one.[1]
