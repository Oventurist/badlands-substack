# Justice40 Initiative

Justice40 is a [[joe-biden|Biden administration]] policy framework stipulating that at least 40 percent of the benefits derived from federal climate spending must flow to communities the administration designates as disadvantaged or marginalized.[1] The agenda governs how climate-related appropriations are distributed across agencies and is invoked as the rationale for targeted tribal, low-income, and environmental-justice awards.[1]

## Tribal climate funding

In March 2024 the [[department-of-the-interior|Department of the Interior]] announced $120 million for Native American tribes to fight climate change, split into 146 separate awards supporting "climate resilience" projects in tribal communities.[1] The package was described as consistent with the Justice40 agenda and premised on the administration's view that Native American populations are among the least able to prepare for or recover from climate impacts.[1] The $120 million formed part of a broader $440 million tribal climate spending push and was assembled from $26 million under the bipartisan infrastructure law, $71 million from the [[inflation-reduction-act|Inflation Reduction Act]], and $23 million in fiscal year 2023 appropriations.[1] Interior Secretary [[deb-haaland|Deb Haaland]] said indigenous communities face "unique and intensifying climate-related challenges that pose an existential threat to tribal economies, infrastructure, lives and livelihoods."[1]

## Criticism

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the framing inverts cause and effect, pointing to earlier reporting on how Colorado's environmental agenda harmed Navajo communities in the Four Corners region by closing a coal mine and stripping the reservation of a critical fuel source.[1] The resulting "double energy crisis" left residents dependent on firewood while lacking the transportation needed to gather and haul it, with locals warning that elderly residents would die as a result.[1] She contended that the crisis stemmed from government action taken in the name of climate change rather than from climate change itself, doubted that tribes could use the new funding to secure fuel, and characterized the award as likely "another money laundering play" in which intersectional climate narratives serve as cover.[1]
