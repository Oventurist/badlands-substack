# 123 Agreement (US–Saudi Civil Nuclear Pact)

A "123 Agreement" is a bilateral civil nuclear cooperation agreement authorized under Section 123 of the [[atomic-energy-act-of-1954|U.S. Atomic Energy Act of 1954]], which sets the legal preconditions for American firms to transfer nuclear technology, materials, and services to a partner state.[1]

In July 2026 the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration signaled it would submit a 123 Agreement with [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] to [[congress|Congress]] within days, authorizing U.S. cooperation on civil nuclear technology.[1] The document was expected to be signed by Energy Secretary [[chris-wright|Chris Wright]] and Saudi Energy Minister [[abdulaziz-bin-salman|Abdulaziz bin Salman]], following a preliminary agreement reached in Riyadh the previous year.[1]

## Terms and omissions

The pact does not incorporate the so-called [[gold-standard-nonproliferation|Gold Standard]], which would prohibit Saudi Arabia from enriching uranium or reprocessing spent fuel — both potential routes to weapons-grade material.[1] It also omits the Additional Protocol that would grant the [[international-atomic-energy-agency|International Atomic Energy Agency]] expanded rights to conduct snap inspections at undeclared sites.[1] While the agreement creates a legal pathway for cooperation across the nuclear fuel cycle, including enrichment, it does not obligate the United States to transfer the related technology or capabilities.[1] The [[united-arab-emirates|United Arab Emirates]] accepted the Gold Standard when it signed its own 123 Agreement in 2009.[1]

## Congressional review

Once submitted, Congress has roughly 90 days of continuous session to review the agreement; blocking it would require a joint resolution and, against a presidential veto, a two-thirds majority in both chambers.[1]

## Badlands analysis

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] noted that Crown Prince [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]] told Fox News's Bret Baier years earlier that Saudi Arabia would pursue a nuclear weapon if [[iran|Iran]] obtained one, and observed that the deal arrives after the death of [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]], who had vehemently opposed Saudi acquisition of nuclear technology.[1] He predicted congressional resistance driven by members aligned with [[israel|Israel]], which he says treats any Muslim nation's receipt of nuclear technology as a red line, while arguing Israel lacks the political capital to stop the pact.[1] His conclusion: if American contractors are blocked, Riyadh will simply hire South Korean or Japanese firms — the Saudis will get a nuclear energy program either way, and the only open question is whether the United States participates.[1]
