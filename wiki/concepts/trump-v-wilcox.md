# Trump v. Wilcox

**Trump v. Wilcox** is a United States Supreme Court emergency-docket matter arising from President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s attempt to remove a member of the [[national-labor-relations-board|National Labor Relations Board]] (NLRB) and a member of the [[merit-systems-protection-board|Merit Systems Protection Board]] (MSPB) from office.[1] Lower courts had blocked the removals: the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia imposed temporary blocks that were affirmed en banc by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit.[1] On a Thursday in late May 2025, the Justices lifted those temporary blocks, allowing the firings to take effect while litigation continued.[1]

## Significance for the removal power

Most analysts read the ruling as sending strong signals that a majority of the Court believes the President possesses constitutional authority to remove at least some high-level officials who exercise executive power, even where [[congress|Congress]] had sought to insulate them from presidential control by requiring legal "cause" for termination.[1] The action did not, however, suggest that all high-ranking members of the federal bureaucracy would necessarily be vulnerable to at-will presidential termination and replacement; commentators noted that the Court would, for historical and structural reasons, exempt the leadership of the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] from the same logic.[1]

## Kagan dissent

Justice [[elena-kagan|Elena Kagan]] dissented, joined by Justices [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]] and [[ketanji-brown-jackson|Ketanji Brown Jackson]].[1] Legal commentary published by Justia focused on passages in the dissent that ranged well beyond the NLRB, the MSPB, or unitary-executive theory, and instead carried broader implications for judicial review and the separation of powers — in particular assertions that would effectively treat Presidents like lower federal judges with respect to a duty to follow past Supreme Court precedent.[1]

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary from [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] characterized the Kagan dissent as "a 10-page panic over the [[humphreys-executor|Humphrey's Executor]] precedent," noting that Kagan again discussed how courts "make law" and suggested that catering to practical needs, including the markets, can be a sound basis for doing so — though not, in her view, in the manner the majority had done it.[1]
