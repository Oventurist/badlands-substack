# Moore v. United States

**Moore v. United States** is a case taken up by the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] on December 5, 2023, concerning the taxation of "unrealized" income and the constitutional limits on the federal taxing power.[1] The case was described in contemporaneous coverage as important but little-noticed, and it drew attention because the Court could use it to strike down the Mandatory Repatriation Tax.[1]

## Legal question

At issue is whether income must be realized before it can be taxed under the [[sixteenth-amendment|16th Amendment]] to the Constitution.[1] Conservative constitutionalists argued that if the Supreme Court found the [[mandatory-repatriation-tax|Mandatory Repatriation Tax]] to violate the 16th Amendment, the resulting precedent could prevent [[congress|Congress]] from enacting legislation to tax wealth.[1] Wealth tax proposals surface regularly in Congress; Sen. [[ron-wyden|Ron Wyden]] (D-Ore.) had recently introduced a plan to tax the unrealized capital gains of high earners.[1]

## Reaction

Liberal groups worried that invalidating the tax law could unleash chaos.[1] The left-leaning [[institute-on-taxation-and-economic-policy|Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy]] said the case "could become the most important tax case in a century," warning that "a broad ruling could destabilize our tax system, enrich many profitable corporations, and widen existing economic and racial inequalities."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] characterized the oral arguments as potentially "seismic" once a decision was handed down in June, framing the branded question — whether Congress can enact what he called a "communist tax" on unrealized capital gains, "profits that do not exist in corporeal reality" — as narrower than the case's true reach.[1] He argued that legal experts saw implications for the 16th Amendment itself, which he described as among the least popular amendments among Americans who understand that the income tax "WAS and SHOULD BE prohibited."[1] He tied the case to a broader thesis that "winning is all around us," positioning the litigation as part of a framework being laid for an America First second term for [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], and cautioned readers not to be shocked "if Taxation itself gets a shock in 2024."[1]
