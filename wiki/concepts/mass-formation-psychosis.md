# Mass Formation Psychosis

**Mass formation psychosis** is a term used in alternative-media commentary to describe a condition in which a large population is conditioned, through sustained and coordinated messaging, to accept a false narrative as self-evident truth.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[brian-cates|Brian Cates]] applied the concept to the American public's reception of the [[russiagate-hoax|RussiaGate]] narrative.[1]

## Application to RussiaGate

In Cates's account, the public was subjected to a years-long psychological operation conducted through five channels: federal law enforcement agencies, intelligence agencies, congressional hearings and investigations, the mainstream media and major social platforms, and popular culture in the form of books, films and music.[1] The purpose was to sell the claim that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and those around him were Russian agents and traitors.[1]

Cates listed eleven media organizations he considered participants in poisoning the prospective jury pool for years before a U.S. Attorney was even appointed: the New York Times, the Washington Post, CNN, ABC, NBC, CBS, Fox News, Time, Newsweek, NPR, and Twitter/Facebook.[1] The campaign, he wrote, continued at full intensity for a further three years while [[john-durham|Durham]] conducted his investigation.[1]

## Consequences for prosecution

The practical implication drawn from the concept is procedural: [[durham-special-counsel-investigation|Durham's Special Counsel investigation]] had to be conducted "backwards," using minor charges against low-level participants as vehicles for surfacing suppressed evidence, because an unbroken conditioning campaign made a fair trial on major charges unattainable.[1] Cates argued that people who insisted the big indictments should have come years earlier were refusing to acknowledge the power of the psyop, and that a broader public awakening — including about the press and the [[russiagate-hoax|RussiaGate hoax]] itself — had to occur before major indictments could succeed.[1] He acknowledged that a large segment of the public remained influenced by the campaign even as many others had come to reject it.[1]

## Foundation of Generational Dependence Syndrome (February 2025)

A February 11, 2025 Badlands Media essay by [[observing-consciousness|Observing Consciousness]] treated Mass Formation Psychosis (MFP) as the psychological foundation of what it called [[generational-dependence-syndrome|Generational Dependence Syndrome]], describing MFP as a deliberate psychological weapon designed to isolate, overwhelm and subjugate entire populations.[2] Citing [[mattias-desmet|Mattias Desmet]], the essay stated that MFP thrives on isolation, anxiety and a fixation on singular narratives, overwhelming individuals and creating a disconnection from community and identity that weakens critical thinking and heightens emotional vulnerability.[2]

By amplifying fear and sowing division, the author argued, those in power construct a reality in which the populace becomes dependent on centralized authority for perceived safety and order.[2] Over time this conditioned state was said to crystallize into a generational cycle in which fear-based compliance is inherited and reinforced, producing not merely passive submission but active loyalty to the system of control.[2] The essay characterized the "genius" of MFP as its ability to turn emotional disorientation into a tool of systemic dominance, laying the groundwork for GDS as it is enforced through [[fourth-generation-warfare|Fourth-Generation Warfare]].[2]

## Definition and origins of the term (January 2023)

A January 20, 2023 Badlands Media essay by [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]], with contributions from [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], noted that "mass psychosis" and "mass formation psychosis" are not standard terms in psychology, sociology or other areas of official research.[4] Psychosis proper is defined as a set of conditions affecting the mind in which there has been some loss of contact with reality; by extension, mass formation psychosis was defined in the essay as a group disconnected from reality, triggered by some event.[4] The term has historically been used informally to describe the collective insanity associated with World War II and the Cold War, and is usually applied to groups such as the Nazis who collectively believed something that led them to lose touch with reality and to justify crimes against humanity.[4]

## Malone, Rogan, and the media backlash

After Dr. [[robert-malone|Robert Malone]] used the phrase during his February 2022 appearance on the [[joe-rogan|Joe Rogan]] podcast, the essay argued, the mainstream media worked tirelessly to discredit the idea — for good reason, since the phrase accurately described the effect globalist propagandists had on masses recruited as pawns for the COVID policy machine.[4] Malone's usage implied both that the people had been weaponized and that those who caused it were as bad as the Nazis, and that the public was effectively hypnotized by propaganda, which highlighted the hypnotist as the perpetrator and made the framing a threat to the pandemic industrial complex.[4]

## Mechanics and COVID-era application

The concept implies that connection to reality acts as a deterrent to immoral behavior — an idea usually found in religion and, the essay argued, held by the American founding fathers — and that social reinforcement plays a role in justifying harm that would otherwise be taboo.[4] Participants in group hysteria are described as acting drunk or intoxicated, numb to the criminally harmful nature of their behavior.[4] Under COVID-19 policy, people were asked to isolate, put their lives on hold, cripple their businesses and accept untested medical decrees while considering themselves virtuous for doing so, while dissenters were cast as threats to public safety — a perfect storm in which "Karens" policed public spaces and a formerly peaceful population was pitted against itself.[4]
