# Congress of Vienna

The Congress of Vienna of 1815 was the European settlement convened after two decades of Napoleonic wars had decimated Europe.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-gateway-to-a-new-world-of-cooperation)

## Assessment

Badlands Media commentary characterises the Congress as "a weak attempt" at a peace conference, contrasting it with the more durable framework established by the [[peace-of-westphalia|1648 Peace of Westphalia]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-gateway-to-a-new-world-of-cooperation) It is placed within a lineage of postwar settlements that also includes the League of Nations and the [[united-nations|United Nations]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-gateway-to-a-new-world-of-cooperation) The author notes that such conferences were sometimes shaped by honest agendas and sometimes not, but that human memory is weak and the Byzantine web of power politics in which the Anglo-American deep state operates is not easily transformed by agreements alone.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-gateway-to-a-new-world-of-cooperation)

## The Restoration of the Oligarchies

In a separate Badlands article, [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] describes the 1815 Congress as "a dark moment in history which has been called 'The Restoration of the Oligarchies'," concluding the brutal years of the Napoleonic Wars.[2] Under grand strategists such as Lord Metternich and Lord Castlereagh of the British Foreign Office, together with the Spanish Habsburgs, the assembled imperial powers of the Holy Alliance resolved to ban all ideas deemed dangerous to state stability or inclined toward revolutionary ideals, producing what Ehret calls a total dictatorship in the arts.[2] Books were banned — Schiller, Lessing, Mendelssohn, Thomas Paine and the political essays of Shelley — with censors installed in every publishing house and monitors in every school, and professors who refused to obey expelled and forbidden to work, a pattern Ehret compares to the red-scare blacklists of the twentieth-century Cold War and which drove many republican thinkers and scientists from Germany to America.[2] The repercussions of the Congress and of the 1819 Carlsbad Decrees produced the backlash of the Three Glorious Days of riots in France, in which the [[marquis-de-lafayette|Marquis de Lafayette]] again had the opportunity to establish a republic.[2]
