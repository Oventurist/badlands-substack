# US Federal Debt Interest Crisis

The US federal debt interest crisis refers to the rapid escalation of interest payments on the national debt as the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] raised rates through 2022 and 2023, with annual interest costs approaching $1 trillion.[1]

## June 2023 budget figures

A monthly US budget deficit report covered by [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] in July 2023 showed government outlays soaring 15 percent to $646 billion in June, nearly $100 billion higher than a year earlier, while tax receipts slumped 9.2 percent from $461 billion to $418 billion.[1] The trailing-twelve-month drop in receipts of over 7.3 percent was the biggest since June 2020, and ZeroHedge noted that receipts had never before fallen that sharply without the US entering a recession.[1]

The June budget deficit nearly tripled from $89 billion a year earlier to $228 billion, far above the $175 billion consensus estimate.[1] Nine months into the fiscal year the cumulative deficit stood at $1.393 trillion, the third highest on record and surpassed only by the crisis years of 2020 and 2021.[1] ZeroHedge speculated sardonically about where the incremental $50 billion deficit hole had gone, remarking that the FBI could not even determine who left cocaine inside the White House.[1]

## Interest burden

Federal interest payments, which totaled $476 billion in 2022, were projected to potentially double to over $1 trillion, driven by a Fed funds rate of 5.25 percent — up 200 percent from the prior year's 1.75 percent.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] placed the debt trajectory inside a long-horizon globalist plan he dated back at least 150 years, encompassing [[15-minute-cities|15-minute cities]] and [[agenda-2030|Agenda 21/2030]].[1] He argued that the 18th-century revolutionary period featured unprecedented autonomy and self-governance, and that the industrial and technological age — fueled by what he called the new secular religion of science — eroded that autonomy in favor of dependence on globalist facilities.[1]

In his account, the Fed's 1990s campaign of low-cost credit expansion replaced saving and hard work with credit-card and business-line financing, stock speculation, and artificially inflated business valuations, leaving citizens and corporations nearly totally dependent on credit.[1] Raising interest rates then triggered the banking collapse, which he argued functioned as a psychological operation to destroy confidence in the venture capital sector and to prepare the ground for a [[central-bank-digital-currency|central bank digital currency]] and an "own nothing and be happy" system, with government bankruptcy — and by extension that of the people — as the next stage.[1]
