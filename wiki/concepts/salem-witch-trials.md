# Salem Witch Trials

The Salem witch trials of 1692–1693 in Massachusetts are treated in the Badlands Media essay "In Defense of Benjamin Franklin" as the second wave of a colonial confrontation with satanic cults, involving a total of 19 executions.[1]

The essay places the trials in a longer sequence beginning with the spread of witch covens into the English colonies — covens it says were often overseen by Druid priests typically left untouched by the witch trials of Europe.[1] From 1648 to 1663 an effort was made to end the spread of satanic cults, centring on Connecticut, where the trials involved 80 accused witches and 15 executions.[1]

[[cotton-mather|Cotton Mather]] and his father [[increase-mather|Increase Mather]] are credited with a dual role: exposing the actual satanic covens while resisting the more fanatical zealots among superstitious Puritans who threatened to turn a serious fight against evil into a paranoia-led terror.[1] In *Cases of Conscience* (1693) Increase Mather attacked the use of "spectral evidence," writing that "It were better that ten suspected witches should escape, then that one innocent person should be condemned."[1] The essay concludes that the relatively small number of executions and the quick end to the trials by 1693 settled the storm for a period, but that the threat did not disappear — resurfacing in the form of the [[hellfire-club|Hell-Fire Club]] networks that took control of colonial covens.[1]
