# Continuing Resolution

A continuing resolution, commonly abbreviated "CR," is a stopgap measure passed by [[congress|Congress]] to temporarily fund the federal government when the twelve regular appropriation bills required to fund the government through its fiscal year have not been enacted.[1]

## November 2023 shutdown standoff

In early November 2023, Republicans controlling the [[us-house-of-representatives|U.S. House of Representatives]] showed no sign of coalescing around a government funding plan just ten days before Congress faced its next shutdown deadline, with existing funding set to expire on November 17.[1] Holding a slim 221-212 majority, Republican lawmakers met behind closed doors to debate how to structure a continuing resolution.[1]

Representative Brian Babin told reporters afterward that "Everybody's got a little bit different position, different opinions. Right now, we're cogitating about it."[1] House Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]], the top Republican in Congress, described the closed-door debate as "positive," saying, "We certainly want to avoid a government shutdown. It's a dangerous time around the world right now. We recognize that," and promising to reveal a plan "in short order."[1]

Republican lawmakers, who said they feared being forced to back a rival funding measure from the Democratic-led [[us-senate|Senate]], told reporters they expected Johnson to unveil a stopgap within days, ensuring a House vote early the following week.[1] Representative Dusty Johnson predicted, "He is going to make a play call, and I would expect that the overwhelming majority of the conference would back his play."[1] The standoff was reported by [[reuters|Reuters]].[1]

## The "laddered CR" (November 2023)

On Saturday, November 11, 2023, House Republicans unveiled the stopgap funding bill intended to avert the shutdown, with just five legislative days remaining before the deadline.[2] Rather than a typical funding extension covering the entire federal government, Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] — two and a half weeks into the job — opted for a two-step continuing resolution, an untested approach aimed at appeasing far-right members of the GOP conference who despise CRs.[2] Johnson and others dubbed the structure a "laddered CR," while critics likened it to a step stool.[2]

Under the two-step strategy, several spending bills needed to keep the government open would be extended until January 19 while the remaining bills would run on a CR until February 2.[2] The plan included no budget cuts and no aid for [[israel|Israel]], and the House was expected to vote as early as Tuesday, giving members 72 hours to read the text of the bill.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that he expected another extension to be reached, "if only so we can watch the [[uniparty|Uniparty]] continue to kick the can down the road in increasingly-dramatic (and exposing) fashion," and described recurring shutdown warnings as narrative deployments whose threat was becoming, for an increasingly bipartisan public, more of a promise.[2]

## March 2025 stopgap and the House vote

In March 2025 [[us-congress|Congress]] again scrambled to pass a continuing resolution to avert a shutdown that could have begun at the end of that week — which would have been the first of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s second term.[3] A shutdown would furlough federal workers without pay and pause nonessential government functions; the CR under consideration would fund the government through September 30 without requiring new appropriations legislation, and had to pass by March 14.[3] Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] unveiled the stopgap over the preceding weekend; it maintained current spending levels until the September expiration while reducing [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] state and local law enforcement assistance.[3] The House had faced a similar position in December, when Republicans struggled to pass an initial CR amid criticism from [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] before a scaled-down version was submitted early on December 21, hours after the funding deadline expired.[3]

The House passed the measure on Tuesday, March 11, 2025 by a narrow 217-213 vote, sending it to the [[us-senate|Senate]], where bipartisan backing would be required for final approval.[3] Just one Republican, Rep. [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] of Kentucky, voted against, and just one Democrat, Rep. Jared Golden of Maine, voted for it.[3] "We did our job today," Johnson said moments after the vote.[3] The episode was reported by [[newsweek|Newsweek]].[3]

Badlands commentary was hostile to the reflexive defense of the CR: [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that Massie might be helping to shut down what he called a corrupt and illegitimate government, while Jon Herold said plainly that he wanted the shutdown.[3]

## September 2023

The House Republican continuing resolution offered during the [[september-2023-government-shutdown-standoff|September 2023 government shutdown standoff]] would have funded the government through October 31, held Defense and the [[department-of-veterans-affairs|Department of Veterans Affairs]] at current levels, cut all other discretionary spending by 8 percent, and incorporated the H.R. 2 border bill minus its E-Verify provision, while excluding Ukraine and disaster-relief money sought by the [[white-house|White House]].[4]

## March 14, 2025 funding deadline

In late February 2025, Senate Appropriations Vice Chair [[patty-murray|Patty Murray]] acknowledged that another short-term funding bill would likely be needed to extend the March 14 funding deadline and avoid a partial government shutdown, even as negotiations on a topline spending agreement for fiscal year 2025 had progressed.[5] [[congress|Congress]] was by then almost five months behind in passing a final FY25 spending agreement and had been relying on a series of continuing resolutions to keep the federal government funded.[5] At the March 14 deadline, lawmakers would need to pass a final spending bill, enact another CR, or accept a partial shutdown.[5] Badlands commentary marked the date with a joking reference to the Ides of March: "Wonder what happens on March 15th? MADNESS!"[5]
