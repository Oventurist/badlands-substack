# NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.

*NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.* was a federal civil-rights lawsuit filed in the [[us-district-court-of-colorado|United States District Court for the District of Colorado]] on March 9, 2022, in which three civil-rights organizations sought to hold Colorado election-integrity activists liable for voter suppression, intimidation, coercion, and threats.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The plaintiffs were the CO/MT/WY Area Conference of the [[naacp|NAACP]], the [[league-of-women-voters-colorado|League of Women Voters of Colorado]], and [[mi-familia-vota|Mi Familia Vota]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The defendants included [[useip|USEIP]] and individually named activists, at least one of whom represented herself pro se.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Claims

The complaint advanced two counts under Section 11(b) of the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]] and one count under the [[ku-klux-klan-act-of-1871|Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The core allegation was that the defendants were "planning to, threatening to, and actually deploying armed agents to knock on doors throughout the state of Colorado" in order to intimidate voters and political advocates.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Legal theory

The plaintiffs' theory conflated questioning an election outcome and door-to-door canvassing with inherently intimidating conduct, without establishing any objectively intimidating act.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Critics trace the theory to a 2015 article in the NYU Social Justice Law Review, [[voters-strike-back|"Voters Strike Back: Litigating against Modern Voter Intimidation"]], which the plaintiffs' expert witness cited in his official report.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) That article argued that Section 11(b) eliminated any legal requirement of racial targeting and that modern intimidation takes subtler forms such as aggressive poll-watching, anonymous threats, frivolous voter-registration challenges, and employer coercion; it also recommended KKK Act claims for "political messaging," to "brand" defendants with "deeply embarrassing associations" and thereby drive settlement, defining success as "stopping the defendant's conduct."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The lone plaintiff representative to testify at trial admitted that stopping the defendants' conduct was the overriding goal.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Trial and outcome

Trial commenced July 15, 2024. The court cut the planned five-day trial short after three days, entering a Rule 52(c) judgement based on partial findings for the defendants and awarding them costs.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Judge [[charlotte-sweeney|Charlotte Sweeney]] stated: "In sum, plaintiffs have failed to introduce any evidence that can remotely be perceived as intimidating or threatening on behalf of the three defendants."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

A pivotal moment came when the only witness claiming to have been approached and intimidated conceded under oath that she named "USEIP volunteers" at the suggestion of plaintiffs' counsel, a position she then incorporated into an affidavit filed in December 2022 to defeat summary judgement.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) Defendants characterize the resulting claims as frivolous, noting they were sustained for 208 days on that affidavit and that no evidence of intimidation ever materialized in discovery, motions practice, or trial.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## Aftermath

The prevailing defendant wrote that association with the Ku Klux Klan by prominent civil-rights organizations damaged her reputation, career, family, and mental health, and that an award of costs did not begin to compensate for it — but that complete vindication in federal court affirmed her faith in justice, which she argued must be actively fought for.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The case is cited in Badlands Media commentary as a case study in [[lawfare]].[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)
