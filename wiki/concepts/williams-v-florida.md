# Williams v. Florida

*Williams v. Florida* is the 1970 decision of the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court of the United States]] holding that the [[sixth-amendment|Sixth Amendment]] does not require a criminal jury of twelve members. The ruling permitted states to seat smaller panels, and as of June 2026 six states — Florida among them — still allow juries of fewer than twelve in certain criminal cases.

## The 2026 challenge

The Badlands Brief of June 16, 2026, citing SCOTUSblog, reports that as the Court entered the closing weeks of its term it agreed to hear three new cases, including a challenge from Florida to whether the Constitution requires twelve-person juries in criminal trials. The Brief notes that the case could prompt the Court to revisit *Williams*, and that several current justices — [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]] named specifically — have signaled interest in reexamining the precedent on the ground that the historical understanding of a jury at the Founding may have required twelve members. Arguments are expected in the term beginning in October.

## Interpretation in the Badlands corpus

The prospect of overruling *Williams* is received in the corpus as potentially transformative. [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] writes that reexamining modern jury practice against the Founding-era understanding could produce "rapid top down change to constitutional juries." Jonathan Drake, quoted in the same edition, locates the crux of the case in the Constitution's silence: because the document mentioned trial by jury without defining it, "it declined to define it, and therefore the objective external standard of historical precedent would determine what it meant. And that's not just for 12. It's for any of their perversions of trial by jury."
