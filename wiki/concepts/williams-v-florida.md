# Williams v. Florida

Williams v. Florida is the 1970 Supreme Court decision holding that the [[sixth-amendment|Sixth Amendment]] does not require twelve jurors in a criminal trial.[1] The ruling permitted states to seat smaller juries, and six states — including Florida — still allow juries of fewer than twelve in certain criminal cases.[1]

## Renewed scrutiny in 2026

In June 2026 the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] agreed to hear a Florida case challenging whether the Constitution requires twelve-person juries, a posture that could prompt the Court to revisit Williams directly.[1] Several sitting justices, including [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]], have signaled interest in reexamining the precedent on the ground that the historical understanding of a jury at the time of the Founding may have required twelve members.[1] Arguments were expected in the term beginning in October.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] welcomed the prospect of measuring modern jury practice against founding-era understanding, suggesting it could produce broad public education and rapid change toward "constitutional juries."[1] Quoting Jonathan Drake, the commentary argued that because the Constitution invoked trial by jury without defining it, it effectively deferred to the objective external standard of historical precedent — a logic that reaches beyond the number twelve to other departures from common-law jury practice.[1] The cited petition language observed that it is not for the court to distinguish between historic features of common-law jury trials that serve important enough functions to migrate silently into the Sixth Amendment and those that do not.[1]
