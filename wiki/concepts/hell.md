# Hell

In Badlands Media's [[the-werewolf-game|Werewolf Game]] series, "hell" is used less as a doctrinal religious term than as the best available word for what the author calls a *veil of evil* enshrouding mankind's collective psyche — one which, he argues, may literally hail from subterranean realms.[1] The author explicitly declines to convert readers to any particular belief or religion, presenting his case as reportage of evidence rather than theology.[1]

## The central thesis

The article's argument is that the dark occultists who run the [[deep-state|Deep State]] are literally attempting to create hell on Earth, and have in all likelihood been working toward this for millennia — with the author adding the assurance that they are going to lose.[1] Because the world contains, in his estimation, too much love, goodwill and beauty to be hell itself, his thesis is instead that hell is *invading* this realm, with the [[koryos|Koryos]] cult serving as a primary vector of that invasion since the beginning of recorded history.[1]

## Evidentiary strands

Four principal lines of evidence are marshalled. The first is the clinical work of [[jerry-marzinsky|Jerry Marzinsky]], who concluded that the voices heard by schizophrenic patients are parasitic demonic entities rather than hallucinations, feeding on negative emotion because they cannot generate their own energy.[1] The second is the near-universality of hell in world religion; the author quotes Frederick Dodson's *Levels of Heaven and Hell* asking whether the ancients of Japan, America, Greece, Scandinavia, India, China, Siberia, Polynesia and South America, along with Christians, Muslims, Jews, Roman scribes, Celts, Iberians, Maya and Aztec, were all liars.[1] The third is the physical anomaly of the [[black-sea|Black Sea]] and its resemblance to the Greek Tartarus by way of [[tartaria|Tartaria]].[1] The fourth is the behaviour of narcissists and psychopaths, whose isolation-and-feeding pattern the author reads as the corporeal analogue of the spirits' method.[1]

## Levels of hell

Many religions, the article notes, agree not only that hell exists but that it has levels.[1] In Tibetan Buddhism the first level is the realm of hungry ghosts — beings who have forgotten who they are and wander tormented by perpetual hunger, often depicted as ugly and misshapen with fire in their bellies.[1] Their only relief comes from sexual acts, and lacking any other outlet they become utterly obsessed with acquiring sex, a pattern the author invites readers to recognise in contemporary human affairs.[1]

## Invitation, magnetism and defence

The article argues that malevolent spirits, like the vampires of folklore, generally require some form of invitation, though it stresses that schizophrenics and others afflicted by such entities did not knowingly invite them.[1] Certain emotions, beliefs and actions are said to "magnetize" such beings, while others repel them, with genuine trust in God identified as the single most important factor.[1] Reciting Psalm 23 aloud is presented as effective precisely because it affirms that trust; one of Marzinsky's patients reported the voices reacting like "worms thrown into a frying pan."[1]

## Materialism as a spiritual weapon

The essay holds that the strategic aim of these entities is to sever people's connection to God, isolating them so that they can be used as food, since hopelessness increases the yield of negative energy.[1] If there is no God, the argument runs, there is no transcendent purpose to human life and morality becomes a purely human invention, so that theft and murder are not intrinsically wrong in any cosmic sense.[1] The author concludes that the enshrinement of materialism and atheism at the institutional level throughout Western civilization strongly implies the presence of evil within the halls of power.[1]

## Countervailing note

The article closes on the observation that parasites need their hosts while hosts do not need parasites, and that Marzinsky found uplifting spiritual ideas to act as "kryptonite" on the entities afflicting his patients — asking how powerful such spirits can be if the statement "I trust God" causes them to scatter like rats.[1] The author holds that men and women possess both the ability and the authority to refuse this intrusion, provided they first learn to accurately assess what has been hidden from them, since one cannot effectively protest what one cannot accurately describe.[1]
