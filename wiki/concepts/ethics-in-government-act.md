# Ethics in Government Act of 1978

The Ethics in Government Act of 1978 (EIGA) is a United States statute governing the financial conduct of federal officials.[1] It prohibits U.S. government officials from holding financial interests that could compromise their objectivity or independence, and establishes the disclosure regime through which compliance is demonstrated.[1] The [[office-of-government-ethics|Office of Government Ethics]] plays a central role in overseeing executive-branch ethics programs and in reviewing officials' financial disclosures under the act.[1]

## Application to Howard Lutnick

The statute drew renewed attention in May 2025 in connection with Commerce Secretary [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]]'s divestiture from [[cantor-fitzgerald|Cantor Fitzgerald]].[1] Under an ethics agreement with the U.S. government, Lutnick agreed to transfer his stake in the firm to his children and a group of investors and to divest from two of its Wall Street subsidiaries.[1] Reuters reported that he sold his Class A shares in the brokerage BGC Group for $151.5 million and in the real-estate firm Newmark Group for $127 million, with the shares sold to the subsidiaries themselves and Cantor Fitzgerald expected to purchase his Class B shares so as to maintain majority ownership of both companies.[1] Disclosure of those stock sales was framed as ensuring his compliance with EIGA.[1]

## Badlands framing

The May 21, 2025 [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] carried the divestiture story with commentary from [[gmoney|GMONEY]], who shifted attention from the ethics mechanics to Cantor Fitzgerald's balance sheet — noting the firm's large [[microstrategy|Strategy]] ($MSTR) position and asking why it was not buying the digital assets named in Trump's crypto executive order.[1] In that framing the statutory divestiture is treated as a formality surrounding a more interesting question about where institutional capital is actually being placed.[1]
