# Long-Term Military Aid for Ukraine

Long-term military aid for Ukraine refers to a 2023 initiative by the Biden administration and European allies to lay plans for multi-year military assistance to Kyiv, intended both to ensure Russia could not win on the battlefield and to persuade the Kremlin that Western support would not waver.[1]

## Structure of the initiative

The effort built on commitments made at a [[g7|Group of Seven]] leaders meeting held on the sidelines of the [[nato|NATO]] summit in Vilnius, Lithuania in July 2023, and initially took the form of bilateral negotiations between the United States and Ukraine and between the United Kingdom and Ukraine.[1] About 18 non-G7 countries signed up to the group's pledge to provide long-term assistance to Kyiv, including the Netherlands, Sweden and other European states.[1]

The stated goal was to ensure Ukraine would be strong enough in future to deter Russia from attacking again.[1] More immediately, Ukraine's Western allies hoped to discourage the Kremlin from believing it could wait out the Biden administration in expectation of a more sympathetic successor in the White House.[1]

## Binding future administrations

Western officials sought ways to lock in pledges of support and limit the ability of future governments to backtrack, amid fears in European capitals that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], should he recapture the White House, would seek to scale back aid.[1] The Wall Street Journal noted that Trump held a wide lead in early Republican primary polling but had lost the 2020 election to President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] and had been indicted in four criminal cases in state and federal courts.[1]

Officials acknowledged the practicalities were complicated even where the G7 shared broad support: the Biden administration is limited in its ability to bind future administrations to international agreements, and Trump had already proven willing to reverse his predecessors' deals with foreign capitals.[1] European states, moreover, lacked the financial and military capacity to pick up the slack if a future president reduced or terminated U.S. aid to Kyiv.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that the one thing that "observably transcends the kabuki theater of partisan politics in America" while binding establishment Democrats and Republicans is the needs and wants of the [[military-industrial-complex|Military Industrial Complex]], and that "the truest hallmark of a deep-state stooge is their unshakable devotion to the MIC."[1] He contended that since the Trump administration — which he credited with pulling the U.S. out of the perpetual Middle Eastern warfare that followed 9/11 — a primary goal of the establishment had been to reinvigorate the U.S. war economy and keep NATO relevant in the post-Cold War era.[1] Anticipating objections about the killing of Qassem Soleimani, DeLarme said that strike "ENDED a problem rather than perpetuating one," in contrast to the standard operating procedure of the U.S. security state.[1]

DeLarme further questioned who would actually fight a war projected years into the future, observing that [[volodymyr-zelensky|Zelensky]] relied on a conscript army and that young men were fleeing Ukraine in droves to avoid being sacrificed.[1] He called the attempt to bind a future president "nothing more unAmerican," arguing it makes a mockery of the American political tradition and of elections if a sitting president can prevent a successor from taking actions benefiting the American people, and concluded: "At least [[raytheon|Raytheon]] and [[lockheed-martin|Lockheed]] will be happy!"[1]
