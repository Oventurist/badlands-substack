# Palisades Fire

The **Palisades Fire** was a destructive January 2025 wildfire in the Pacific Palisades area of Los Angeles that became a focal point of criticism over municipal water infrastructure and emergency preparedness.[1]

## Water supply failures

Los Angeles Fire Department insiders told DailyMail.com that firefighters battling the Palisades Fire ran out of water, attributing the failure to a nearby reservoir that had been left disconnected and to fire hydrants that had been broken for months prior to the blaze.[1] Those insiders placed responsibility on [[janisse-quinones|Janisse Quiñones]], the chief executive of the [[los-angeles-department-of-water-and-power|Los Angeles Department of Water and Power]], who was reported to have known about the empty reservoir and the inoperative hydrants months before the fire began.[1]

Quiñones had been hired in May 2024 on a $750,000 salary — nearly double that of her predecessor — after Mayor [[karen-bass|Karen Bass]] directed the city to max out its budget in order to "attract private-sector talent."[1] Her previous employer, [[pacific-gas-and-electric|Pacific Gas & Electric]], where she served as senior vice president from 2021 to 2023, was itself linked to major California fire scandals, including the 2021 Dixie Fire and the 2018 Camp Fire.[1]

## Casualties and scale

The fire erupted on January 7, 2025 near a hiking trail overlooking the wealthy coastal neighborhood, ultimately killing 12 people and destroying more than 6,000 homes.[2] It scorched more than 23,000 acres (9,308 hectares), caused about $150 billion in damage, and raged for more than three weeks, also ravaging parts of Topanga and Malibu.[2] It ranks among the most destructive blazes in Los Angeles history.[2] Homes belonging to celebrities including Mel Gibson, Paris Hilton and Jeff Bridges were among the thousands of structures destroyed.[2] The separate [[eaton-fire|Eaton Fire]], ignited the same day in the Los Angeles area, killed another 19 people and razed 9,400 structures, and its cause remained unclear as of October 2025.[2]

## Arrest of Jonathan Rinderknecht

In October 2025, roughly nine months after the fire, a 29-year-old man named [[jonathan-rinderknecht|Jonathan Rinderknecht]] was arrested on suspicion of starting the blaze.[2] Justice Department officials said evidence collected from his digital devices included an image he had generated on [[chatgpt|ChatGPT]] depicting a burning city.[2] [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] contributors ridiculed the case, characterizing Rinderknecht as a "patsy" and mocking the reliance on an AI-generated picture as proof of guilt.[2] Contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] wrote, "We know they got the right patsy because the Machine made him a scary drawing."[2]

## Litigation against LADWP

In April 2025, two federal judges who lost their Pacific Palisades homes in the January firestorm joined hundreds of their neighbors in suing the [[los-angeles-department-of-water-and-power|Los Angeles Department of Water and Power]], claiming the utility failed to properly prepare for the wildfire and to respond once it broke out.[3] U.S. District Judge Dean Pregerson, who sits on the Central District of California's court, and Vijay "Jay" Gandhi, a former magistrate judge on the same court, filed the suit in Los Angeles Superior Court along with their families.[3] The complaint alleges that the Palisades conflagration "was caused by both LADWP's water and power assets, specifically empty reservoirs and energized powerlines."[3]

The lawsuit cites Los Angeles Times reporting that found LADWP's Santa Ynez Reservoir, located in the Palisades, sat empty during the firefight, having been closed months earlier for repairs.[3] "Despite dire warnings by the National Weather Service of a 'Particularly Dangerous Condition-Red Flag Warning,' of 'critical fire weather' which had the potential for rapid fire spread and extreme fire behavior, the LADWP was unprepared for the Palisades fire," the complaint said.[3]

## Commentary

Badlands Media contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the fire and the systemic failures behind it were simultaneously real events and a vehicle through which long-buried municipal corruption was being exposed, writing that "if questioning ANY narrative hurts your feelings, you're not doing it right."[1]

Contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that there was "a not insignificant chance that suing the utility leads to the government on this one," while expressing skepticism the case would reach discovery, partly because it was brought by judges whose filings carried "a baked in legal review."[3] She predicted the case would more likely settle, with "the secrets of public-private-partnership governing utilities" sealed in private action — "an unsatisfying but all too common outcome when disaster strikes."[3]

## Investigation into a possible human cause (January 2025)

By January 13, 2025, investigators had publicly focused on a possible human cause of the Palisades Fire, while cautioning that any such ignition might not have been intentional.[4] At the same press conference the Los Angeles district attorney announced that a total of ten people had been arrested for arson as well as looting and other crimes.[4] [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentator [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] mocked official reticence about arson and the reflexive attribution of the fires to climate change, arguing that authorities were losing control of the narrative and that their explanations were becoming more ridiculous as a result.[4]

The fire burned amid rare, extremely critical fire weather: the National Weather Service issued a new round of fire weather warnings on January 13, 2025, advising of a "particularly dangerous situation" for parts of the Los Angeles metro region including the San Fernando and Simi valleys, Oxnard, Ventura, and portions of the Interstate 5 corridor, with red flag warnings running from north of Santa Barbara to east of San Diego and winds forecast to gust strongly through at least Wednesday as regional drought expanded.[4]

## Radio traffic and the water failure

On the morning of January 7, 2025, a firefighter near the Pacific Palisades neighborhood of Los Angeles observed a 10-acre brush fire along a ridgeline and radioed a warning to other first responders: "Just to give you guys a heads up... this has the potential for 200 acres plus."[5] The Palisades fire and those that followed went on to burn more than 40,000 acres, an area three times the size of the island of Manhattan.[5] Roughly eight hours after that initial call, at around 6 p.m., a firefighter in Pacific Palisades reported a problem with the water supply, with radio traffic later reviewed by NPR capturing how crews lost hydrant pressure — one transmission reporting that "the hydrants up here are dead."[5]
