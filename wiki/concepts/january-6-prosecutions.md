# January 6 Prosecutions

The January 6 prosecutions are the federal criminal cases brought over the 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol, described by Justice Department leaders as the largest federal prosecution in the department's history.[1]

## Status in January 2025

Figures released by the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] on January 6, 2025 revealed that federal prosecutors were weighing charges against as many as 200 more people for their involvement in the attack, including 60 suspected of assaulting or impeding police officers during the riot that nearly derailed the transfer of power from [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]].[1]

About 1,583 people had by then faced federal charges for their role in the attack, including more than 600 facing felony charges for assaulting or resisting police. Although DOJ had released monthly statistics on the probe throughout, Monday's figures marked the first time prosecutors had estimated how many uncharged cases remained — described as a signal flare to Trump, who had indicated he expected to pardon many people involved in the attack but had repeatedly mischaracterized the status of the cases.[1]

## Context

Trump spent the four years after the riot seeking to recast it, calling January 6 a "day of love," referring to those arrested as "hostages," and vowing to pardon them immediately upon taking office. The pending caseload became public on the same day [[congress|Congress]] carried out the [[2024-election-certification|2024 election certification]] that sealed his return to power.[1]

The continued prosecutions were also invoked in commentary on [[inauguration-day-2025-protest-plans|leftist protest plans for Inauguration Day]], which asked whether the [[fbi|FBI]] and DOJ would pursue those demonstrators with equivalent vigor.[1]

## Statutory foundation challenged at the Supreme Court

A substantial share of the January 6 prosecutions rested on the obstruction-of-an-official-proceeding provision of the 2002 Corporate Fraud Accountability Act, a post-Enron statute used to charge more than 300 Capitol defendants with felonies.[2] On December 13, 2023, the Supreme Court agreed to review defendant Joseph Fischer's challenge to that theory after the D.C. Circuit split with district judge Carl J. Nichols, who had read the statute's "otherwise obstructs" clause to cover only document tampering.[2] Because the same provision underpinned two of the four counts across Jack Smith's indictments of Donald Trump, Badlands commentary argued that an adverse ruling could unravel convictions and pending indictments across the entire January 6 docket.[2]
