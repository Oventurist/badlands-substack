# A New American Renaissance

"A New American Renaissance: On Benjamin West, Incubator and Coordinator" is an essay published on Badlands Media on December 26, 2025 by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]]. Written as a chapter-length study, it argues that the Pennsylvania-born painter [[benjamin-west|Benjamin West]] — president of the [[royal-academy-of-arts|English Royal Academy of Fine Arts]] and court painter to [[king-george-iii|King George III]] — operated as a deliberate republican agent of cultural renaissance inside the heart of the British Empire, embedding subversive philosophical messages in his history paintings and training a generation of American artists. The article credits the Canadian-born historian [[pierre-beaudry|Pierre Beaudry]] with the pioneering research that made the chapter possible, stating that without Beaudry's studies "Benjamin West's story would still be hidden to this very day."

## The Royal Academicians and West's origins

The essay opens on West's 1795 group painting *The Royal Academicians*, in which West sits at the center surrounded by the artists of the British Royal Academy. The article stresses the anomaly of the arrangement: the president and co-founder of the English Royal Academy of Fine Arts was a Quaker born in Pennsylvania in 1737 — the same year as George III — who was entirely self-taught, having learned to paint with clay and bear grease supplied by Delaware natives friendly with his family.

West's father, a boarding-house keeper and close friend of [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]], is said to have made his home in 1751 or 1752 the first building in the world to be fitted with a lightning rod, and the young West was quickly drawn into the networks of Franklin's Pennsylvania Philosophical Society. The article recounts that the strict Quaker community, which regarded the arts as corrupting because they awakened dangerous passions, held an all-night emergency meeting over what to do with the talented boy, concluding that "if God gave him this skill... maybe he's the one who can bring painting to another level where it can make people better." Franklin's friends, impressed by West's teenage painting *The Last Days of Socrates*, sponsored his 1769 voyage to France and then Italy. A planned brief stop in London became permanent; West attracted the attention of an advisor to George III, became a founding member of the Royal Academy in 1769, and its president in 1791. He never returned to America, and the article notes he was denounced as a traitor by Americans and simultaneously accused by the British of being an American spy — the latter charge, the author writes, "may be a bit more true."

## The method of Platonic irony

Central to the essay is the claim that West worked in what it calls the classical Platonic tradition, using "the language of ironies": deliberately embedded anomalies designed to produce perplexity, then wonder, then discovery in the viewer. The author compares West's 1770 and 1776 self-portraits, noting that the eyes in the later work are darker, more worldly and less naive, that in both the sitter tugs at the suffocating English formal collar (read as a symbol of the tightness of English culture), and above all that the rag draped over the canvas frame changes: blank in 1770, but in 1776 bearing a faint human figure. The article identifies that figure as the priest and red-coated soldier from West's 1770 painting *The Death of General Wolfe* — a quotation inserted, significantly, in the year the Declaration of Independence reached George III.

## The Death of General Wolfe

The essay treats *The Death of General Wolfe* as West's key intervention. The painting depicts the 1759 Battle of Quebec at the Plains of Abraham, which the article calls the decisive moment when the French empire lost its stranglehold on North America and Franklin's networks gained room to organize what became the American Revolution. Citing the late historian [[graham-lowry|Graham Lowry]] and his *How the Nation Was Won: Volume 1 (1630–1757)*, the article argues these networks had been preparing the conditions for independence for decades, and that Quebec — whose French-Canadian population regarded the British as tyrants but got along well with the thirteen colonies — was expected to become the fourteenth signatory of the Declaration of Independence. Franklin's 1774 organizing trip to Quebec and his establishment of Canada's first postal service in 1759 are cited as parts of that design, which the article says was defeated by "certain games" and "certain bribes."

The essay dwells on the figure of [[william-johnson|William Johnson]], the Irish-born New York settler shown in native stockings and moccasins, pointing toward a soldier running up with a captured fleur-de-lis. Noting that in reality only two people were with Wolfe when he died behind a bush, the article argues West composed the scene for meaning rather than reportage. Johnson, who moved to New York in 1738, learned several native dialects, was made a Mohawk civil chief by 1742 and colonel of the Six Nation Mohawks, is presented as the man who won [[iroquois-confederacy|Mohawk and Iroquois]] allegiance away from the [[jesuits|Jesuit]]-run French and thereby tipped the battle. The prominence of the Native American warrior at the center of the canvas, set against the weak and dying aristocratic Wolfe, is read as a subtle image of the dying British Empire — an ambiguity that, the article notes, allowed an American republican and a British aristocrat to read opposite messages in the same picture.

The essay also emphasizes West's break with convention in using contemporary costume rather than Greco-Roman dress, for which he was reportedly threatened with never receiving another commission. It quotes his letter defending the choice: "The event intended to be commemorated took place on the 13th of September, 1759 in a region of the world unknown to the Greeks and Romans... but if, instead of the facts of the transaction, I represent classical fictions, how shall I be understood by posterity?" George III, impressed, purchased the painting and settled the debate.

## Penn's Treaty and the Westphalian principle

The article turns next to West's 1771 painting of the [[penn-treaty-of-1682|Penn Treaty of 1682]]. It recalls that West came from Swarthmore, Pennsylvania, and that his grandfather was a close friend of [[william-penn|William Penn]] (1644–1718), described as founder of the Pennsylvania colony and a key leader of the Leibniz/Jonathan Swift networks in America who developed his land along lines set out by John Winthrop Jr. and John Winthrop rather than for wealth extraction. Penn, holding dictatorial authority under the Crown Charter, was under no obligation to pay the Delaware anything, yet negotiated a treaty paying them generously and preserving their freedom to use the land. West's canvas shows textiles and goods — "not alcohol or guns" — being offered beneath the famous cedar tree, depicting the negotiations rather than the signing; an unexplained shadow across the central figures is read as a possible foreshadowing of later betrayals.

The essay quotes Articles 1 through 6, 8 and 9 of the treaty at length, singling out Article 4's injunction that neither side believe "false rumours or reports" of the other but "first come as brethren to inquire," burying such rumors "as in a bottomless pit." This clause is presented as a direct countermeasure to intelligence operations — specifically Jesuitical operations — that manipulated tensions between settlers and natives through slander and, the article claims, "very, very high level MK Ultra brainwashing operations" of the kind described by [[cynthia-chung|Cynthia Chung]] in *The Shaping of a World Religion: From Jesuits, Freemasons and Anthropologists to the Ghost Dance Religion*, and by Friedrich Schiller in his 1792 study *The Jesuit Government in Paraguay*. The treaty's ethic is linked to the [[peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]] of 1648 and its "Principle of the Benefit of the Other," under which each nation's self-interest is premised on its neighbor's welfare and the forgiveness of past transgressions.

## Virtue, sovereignty and the classical subjects

Asking how a painter conveys virtue rather than mere emotion or propaganda, the article examines *The Departure of Regulus for Carthage* (1769), bought by George III for a thousand pounds. It retells how the captured Roman consul Regulus, released to argue for Rome's surrender, instead urged the Senate to fight, then returned voluntarily to Carthage and execution rather than break his word. The author notes a single pensive woman in the composition who, amid general emotional collapse, mirrors the viewer wrestling with the philosophical question, and compares Regulus's choice to that of Socrates as recorded in Plato's *Apologia*, *Crito* and *Laws* — the subject of West's earliest painting.

A later work, *Cicero Discovering the Tomb of Archimedes*, is discussed alongside a long quotation from Cicero's *Tusculan Disputations* (45 BC) describing how Cicero, as quaestor, located the overgrown tomb by its sphere-and-cylinder emblem when the Syracusans denied it existed, and concluding that no one acquainted with liberal learning "would not choose to be this mathematician rather than that tyrant." The article draws a parallel between Cicero the foreigner rediscovering Syracuse's forgotten greatness and West the American reviving a healthier tradition among "a corrupt wayward people," observing that it often takes an outsider to see past a decaying civilization's "cloudy matrix of lies." The unifying theme across West's chosen subjects — Socrates, Regulus, Cicero, Penn — is identified as the sovereignty of the individual: only those free of the fear of death and of addiction to ego and comfort can defend the liberties of the Declaration of Independence or the Constitution.

## Messages to a troubled king

The article reports that beginning in 1769 West spent evenings at Buckingham Palace in philosophical dialogue with George III, who was his exact contemporary, making his pro-republican views known and finding the king "not so one dimensional" as popular culture suggests. It notes West's 1779 portrait of the king receiving news of the French-Spanish Armada threatening invasion.

Three paintings are then read as coded counsel to the monarch. *Saul and the Witch of Endor* retells the Old Testament episode in which Saul, terrified before battle with the Philistines, consults a witch to summon the dead prophet Samuel, receives a prophecy of defeat and death, and fulfills it the next day by suicide — the lesson being that superstitious belief in a forecast destroys free will, a principle the article compares to Hamlet's obedience to his father's ghost and to the three witches of *Macbeth*, and connects to the occultism of the British imperial elite and the Hellfire Club. A 1791 scene from *King Lear*, Act 3, depicts Lear in the storm refusing Tom Bedlam's cave because "the storm in his head is worse," and accepting his suffering as justice for having neglected the poor of his kingdom. The 1792 mural *Pharaoh and Host Lost at Sea* shows the Red Sea collapsing on the pursuing Egyptian army — read against Britain's obsession with reabsorbing its lost colonies. The essay stresses that George III's insanity did not appear suddenly in 1810 when he was removed from power, but in bouts from the early 1780s, and speculates that, like the Marquis de Lafayette, he carried two contradictory impulses: admiration for human genius set against the oligarchical principles of the empire he was born into.

## The death of Chatham and the unfinished peace

The article treats West's painting of the 1778 death of Lord Chatham, [[william-pitt-the-elder|William Pitt the Elder]], as a devastating republican critique that the British elite mistook for homage. It notes the apocryphal quality of the scene — Pitt actually died 34 days later in his bedroom — and recovers his purpose in Parliament: to defeat the Duke of Richmond's March 23 motion for withdrawal of all British troops from the United States, which had already been beaten by 56 votes in the House of Lords. Chatham's April 7, 1778 speech is quoted at length, including "while I have sense and memory, I will never consent to deprive the royal offspring of the House of Brunswick... of their fairest inheritance" and "if we must fall, let us fall like men!" He collapsed in a seizure immediately after; the author suggests that as he died, "so too did his sick cause." [[john-singleton-copley|John Singleton Copley]] — another American painter born the same year as West, who studied under him — produced a rival 1781 version juxtaposing the scene with the 1588 defeat of the Spanish Armada, the moment England displaced the Spanish Habsburg Empire as the world maritime power.

West's 1783 *The Peace of Paris* shows the American delegation — John Jay, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Henry Laurens ("Henry Lorenz" in the source) and Franklin's grandson William Temple Franklin, whom Franklin was training in statecraft. The painting is unfinished because the British delegation never appeared for the sitting, which the author calls fitting: "the revolution itself was never finished, even now, 250 years later." He argues the British oligarchic influence inside the United States was never extracted and remained as a powerful fifth column, citing post-1781 imperial policies to deny the colonists manufacturing and block settlement west of the Allegheny Mountains in order to crush their Promethean spirit.

## Franklin, Prometheus and the American school

The final work discussed is West's 1816 *Benjamin Franklin Drawing Electricity from the Sky*, painted after George III's final collapse had left West without royal patronage and, the author suggests, with nothing left to lose. Since his 1752 experiments with the "electric fire from the gods," Franklin had been known as "The Prometheus of America." Beaudry's hypothesis about the five cherubs — some innocently infantile, one bearing oddly adult attributes — is offered as another visual irony: that Promethean discovery requires adults to recover child-like wonder. The native headband on one cherub is read as the solution to the paradox of harmonizing European and First Nations cultures.

The essay closes by tracing a scientific lineage from Franklin's discoveries to [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]]'s electrical communications, developed with the circle around the Humboldt brothers including Carl F. Gauss, Wilhelm Weber and Ampère, and to Joseph Henry and Franklin's descendant Alexander Dallas Bache in what it calls the Göttingen-Philadelphia complex of genius. The author argues this tradition must be revived to overcome "absurd paradoxes" of modern physics such as black holes, dark matter and multiverses. A closing note lists American painters trained by West in England: Charles Willson Peale (1741–1827), Rembrandt Peale (1778–1860), Gilbert Charles Stuart (1755–1828), John Trumbull (1756–1843) and Copley. An endnote records that Morse later exposed Jesuit operations in America in his 1841 *Foreign Conspiracy Against the Liberties of the United States*, describing Prince Metternich's Holy Alliance deploying "hundreds of Jesuits and priests... who have a complete military organization through the United States." The article also invokes James Fenimore Cooper as a later novelist who pursued the same theme of European–Native reconciliation, holding that only when America treats Native peoples as "sacred partners in a common mission" will it "acquire the real moral fitness to survive."
