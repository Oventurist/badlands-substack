# Unemployment Statistics

Unemployment statistics are the official government measures of joblessness in the United States, routinely cited by government and media as evidence of economic health. Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argued in February 2023 that these figures are manipulated — sometimes through outright falsehood, and sometimes by changing definitions to produce a desired result.[1]

## Definitional manipulation

Carlson wrote that the definition of "unemployed" can be shifted from not having a job, to not actively looking for a job, to not having looked for a job within the past five minutes; and from not currently having a job, to not having had one for a month, to not having had one for a year.[1] Composition effects further distort the picture: a [[google|Google]] executive working the drive-through at a [[burger-king|Burger King]] is counted as employed, and a person taking a second job to survive can be reported as two people with two separate jobs.[1]

## Layoffs versus headline numbers

Carlson contrasted the reported record-low unemployment levels with visible mass layoffs of hundreds of thousands of workers from high-paying technology jobs.[1] He cited [[disney|Disney]]'s announcement of 7,000 layoffs, after which the company's stock rose 6% in after-hours trading — a move he attributed not to economic improvement but to cost-cutting in anticipation of a rougher economic period.[1] While isolated layoffs can reflect competitive failure, bad projections or unsuccessful advertising, Carlson argued that when the majority of big companies are laying people off it signals that the economy is weak or expected to worsen.[1]

## Labor-market anecdotes

Addressing the claim that long fast-food drive-through lines prove employers cannot find staff, Carlson suggested the more likely explanation is that owners cannot afford $15–$20 an hour, and that many fast-food restaurants learned during COVID that drive-through-only service allows them to staff fewer employees.[1] He questioned why teenagers willing to work for $6 an hour three years earlier would refuse the same job at $15–$20, while allowing that some employers genuinely cannot find good help.[1]

## Broader pattern

Carlson likened statistical manipulation in economics to the handling of the PCR test used for COVID, asserting that cycle counts were raised or lowered to make case rates appear higher or lower as desired, and that the same pattern of manipulation applies to economic tools.[1] His guiding maxim throughout was "believe half of what you see and none of what you hear," with "hear" standing for government and media claims.[1]
