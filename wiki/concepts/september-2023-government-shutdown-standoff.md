# September 2023 Government Shutdown Standoff

The September 2023 government shutdown standoff was a funding fight between the Republican-controlled House of Representatives and the Democratic-led Senate over legislation needed to keep federal agencies open past the September 30 end of the fiscal year. Both chambers faced that deadline to avoid what would have been the fourth potential government shutdown in a decade.[1]

## Background

Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] (R-CA) entered the standoff facing what was described as perhaps the biggest challenge of his eight months in the speakership: averting a shutdown with a fractured caucus.[1] McCarthy had cleared a major internal hurdle the previous week by announcing an impeachment inquiry into the Biden family, which helped set the stage for a Sunday-night proposal for a 30-day stopgap funding bill that would give House GOP leaders room to move separate bills funding the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] and the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]].[1]

## The stopgap proposal

The measure — a [[continuing-resolution|continuing resolution]] — would have funded the government through October 31, held the Department of Defense and the [[department-of-veterans-affairs|Department of Veterans Affairs]] at existing levels, and cut all other discretionary spending by 8 percent.[1] It also folded in the House GOP border bill known as H.R. 2, minus that bill's E-Verify provision.[1] The package pointedly excluded funding for the Ukraine war and for disaster relief, both of which the White House had sought in a supplemental request intended to ride along with a continuing resolution.[1]

The House Rules Committee was scheduled to mark up the continuing resolution on Monday, September 18, with Majority Leader [[steve-scalise|Steve Scalise]] telling members on a Sunday-night conference call to expect a Wednesday vote on the Defense appropriations bill and a Thursday vote on the CR.[1]

## Whip count problems

Doubt persisted over whether McCarthy had the votes. More than a dozen conservatives opposed the stopgap on the grounds that it continued to finance the Ukraine war and "woke" policies of the Biden administration.[1] Reporting by Punchbowl News put McCarthy's margin at just four votes, with some Republicans expected to miss votes because of health issues.[1]

## Democratic response

Senate Majority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]] (D-NY) dismissed the bill as a "[[house-freedom-caucus|Freedom Caucus]] wish list" and a "hard-right screed" with no chance of passing the Senate.[1] House Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] (D-NY) called it a "right wing wishlist that they want to jam down the throats of the American people," describing it as "unreasonable, unacceptable and laughable."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-media|Badlands]] News Brief, [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] framed the recurring autumn budget crisis as a fixture of American political life and argued that the Democratic reaction was wildly out of proportion to a bill whose main offense was cutting discretionary spending by up to 8 percent — spending he characterized as the source of government graft.[1] His closing question was whether the seasonal budget theater was itself a distraction from other developments.[1]

## The 'kitchen sink' impasse

By Tuesday, September 19, 2023 the standoff had deteriorated sharply for Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]].[2] House Republicans were forced to cancel a procedural vote on the 30-day stopgap measure, and hours later five GOP lawmakers crossed the aisle to vote with Democrats to bring down the rule for the [[pentagon|Pentagon]]'s spending bill.[2] Punchbowl News called it "a bad day for McCarthy and House Republicans," and [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] characterized a "kitchen sink" omnibus approach as the Speaker's only remaining path to avoid a shutdown — one that probably would not work.[2]

McCarthy needed votes from the [[house-freedom-caucus|Freedom Caucus]], whose members demanded the continuing resolution be spliced apart so that it would not fund what Rep. [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]] described as "the election interference of [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]]."[2] Gaetz additionally pressed McCarthy to sign a subpoena to investigate [[hunter-biden|Hunter Biden]]'s alleged "high crimes and misdemeanors," drafting the document himself.[2] McCarthy could alternatively pander to Democrats for the votes needed to pass the CR, but doing so would likely trigger the motion to vacate that Gaetz and others had threatened.[2]

## Uniparty framing

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that McCarthy was widely perceived by disillusioned voters in both parties as the face of the [[uniparty|Uniparty]], while Gaetz and the Freedom Caucus were seen as executing [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s will in the House.[2] Caving to Democratic demands to fund the war in Ukraine would deepen the divide between America First and the Uniparty; caving to the Freedom Caucus would signal a genuine shift in establishment deal-making toward sovereign nationalism and demonstrate the movement's power even with Trump out of office.[2] Because Trump had de facto installed McCarthy as Speaker, Burning Bright treated the whole confrontation as a narrative under construction.[2]

## Final days before the deadline

By Thursday, September 28, 2023 — with a little over two days remaining before funding lapsed — there was still no clear path to averting a shutdown, and divisions among House Republicans were on open display as tensions erupted during a closed-door conference meeting and the chamber convened for late-night votes.[3] The Senate worked to advance a bipartisan short-term funding extension, but House Republicans threw cold water on it as many conservatives balked at any stopgap bill.[3] The House passed several full-year spending bills late Thursday night in an effort by GOP leadership to show conservatives it was acting in good faith, though the measures could not stop a shutdown and had no prospect of passing the Senate.[3] At the end of the night a bill funding the Department of Agriculture failed on the floor when 27 Republicans voted against it, underscoring the conference's continued inability to coalesce around spending legislation.[3]

## Win-win framing

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the impasse as dark comedy, opening with "Don't threaten Americans with a good time" and describing a narrative convergence between the shutdown storyline and the economic troubles accumulating under the same government's watch — the most impactful labor strikes in decades, surging inflation and rising interest rates.[3] The government presiding over all of it, he wrote, was now seen by the public as panicking for the right to keep doing its job.[3] Setting aside deeper 5GW framings, he argued the public simply saw "a POTATO being threatened with impeachment and a corrupt Uniparty being threatened with a shutdown," making the prospect of a win-win outcome bipartisan — and, he suggested, exactly the sort of scenario a deal-maker would find advantageous.[3]
