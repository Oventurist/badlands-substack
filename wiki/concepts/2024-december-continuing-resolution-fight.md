# December 2024 Continuing Resolution Fight

The December 2024 continuing resolution fight was a rapid, chaotic budget confrontation in the U.S. House of Representatives that pushed the federal government to the brink of a shutdown in the final days before [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] took office for a second term. Over roughly twenty-four hours on December 18–19, 2024, a bipartisan short-term funding deal negotiated by Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] collapsed under public pressure from [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] and Trump, exposing what reporting described as a schism between the president-elect and the speaker.[1]

## Collapse of the Johnson deal

Trump had long supported Johnson, hosting him on election night, bringing him to the Army–Navy college football game, and backing him privately despite conservative complaints about the House's conduct. It therefore stunned many Republicans when Musk — with Trump's go-ahead — helped tank Johnson's short-term funding deal on Wednesday afternoon, unleashing a barrage of social media posts beginning early in the morning and calling the deal "criminal."[1]

Trump followed with threats to oppose in a 2026 primary any Republican who voted for the measure, and injected a further complication by calling for the [[debt-ceiling|debt ceiling]] — a tool Republicans had used for years to pressure Democrats into spending cuts — to be lifted or eliminated entirely before he took office. With funding expiring at the end of the night on Friday, Trump's last-minute demands pushed the government perilously close to a shutdown.[1]

The broadsides from Mar-a-Lago left Republican lawmakers wondering why, given how much the president-elect and the speaker communicate, it took until the final moment for the schism to burst into view. "It's all very strange," one GOP lawmaker told CNN. "This was completely avoidable."[1]

## The replacement bill and its failure

By Thursday evening Trump was again backing Johnson as the speaker tried to advance a different plan designed to appease the president-elect's demands. The revised bill would have extended government funding for three months, lifted the debt ceiling until 2027, extended the farm bill, and included $110 billion for disaster relief. It failed, with 38 Republicans voting against it. The twenty-four-hour whiplash underscored both Johnson's weakness and Musk's opening with Trump.[1]

The episode left House leadership scrambling and raised questions about how Republicans on Capitol Hill — holding a narrow majority with competing factions — would function once Trump took office. Democrats, who had helped Johnson save his job the previous spring, said they were through helping the Louisiana Republican manage his unruly conference.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the collapse as a demonstration of popular power: the disastrous continuing resolution died, but not before "the People rediscovered some of their power." She noted that on Thursday legislators returned with a 116-page bill that removed much of the objectionable spending, that Republicans and two Democrats in the House were on board, and that Minority Leader [[hakeem-jeffries|Hakeem Jeffries]] was leading entirely partisan opposition.[1]

Ashe expressed discomfort with raising the debt ceiling but said she was willing to extend Trump the benefit of the doubt if he needed room for negotiations in 2025 and beyond, arguing that a successful "transformational change program" returning the federal government to its constitutional limits should eventually allow the debt ceiling to be lowered as the country returned to sound monetary policy.[1]

## Withdrawal of the stopgap bill

On Wednesday, December 18, 2024, Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] withdrew the stopgap spending bill that would have kept the government funded into spring, after mounting opposition from fellow Republicans including President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and Vice President-elect [[jd-vance|JD Vance]].[2] The measure, unveiled Tuesday, drew criticism for its inclusion of billions in additional spending and prompted a GOP revolt that left the path forward uncertain.[2] House Majority Leader [[steve-scalise|Steve Scalise]] confirmed the bill's demise, saying "There's no new agreement right now," with lawmakers facing a Friday deadline to prevent a shutdown.[2]

The proposed bill would have extended funding through March 14.[2] Trump and Vance issued a joint statement urging Republicans to pass a streamlined measure without what they called "Democrat giveaways" and to simultaneously raise the [[debt-ceiling|debt ceiling]]: "Republicans want to support our farmers, pay for disaster relief, and set our country up for success in 2025 ... Anything else is a betrayal of our country."[2] The statement closed with the line, "THIS CHAOS WOULD NOT BE HAPPENING IF WE HAD A REAL PRESIDENT. WE WILL IN 32 DAYS!"[2] The debt ceiling, which limits the government's borrowing ability, had not been part of the negotiations at that point.[2]

## Contents of the bill and public backlash

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the purpose of the continuing resolution episode was to generate public support for what was coming, on the principle that "you can't tell the people, you have to show them."[2] Citing [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]], she wrote that the roughly 1,500-page bill would have given [[congress|Congress]] new protections to skirt sunshine laws and quash subpoenas — in her characterization, effectively congressional immunity.[2] The bill also continued funding for the [[global-engagement-center|Global Engagement Center]], which she described as the censorship apparatus responsible for deplatforming, debanking, and dehumanizing Americans with disfavored political views.[2]

[[vivek-ramaswamy|Vivek Ramaswamy]] noted that "the proposal adds at least 65 cents of new spending for every dollar of continued discretionary spending."[2] Critics also highlighted a 40% congressional pay raise contained in the package at a time when Congress's approval rating stood at roughly 20% per Ballotpedia's December 12, 2024 polling index, and when military compensation and cost-of-living deficits went unaddressed.[2] Ashe concluded that the outrage was deliberate: a public enraged by waste, fraud, and abuse would supply the "emotionally-charged, highly sustainable public support" needed to dismantle the deep state and back [[doge|DOGE]] after January 20, 2025.[2] The bill reportedly died Wednesday night.[2]

## Speaker succession speculation

The fight generated speculation about Johnson's replacement. Sen. [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]] posted on X that "the Speaker of the House need not be a member of Congress" and suggested Musk for the job, arguing that "nothing would disrupt the swamp more than electing Elon Musk." Rep. [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]] said later Thursday that she would be open to supporting Musk, tying the idea to [[doge|DOGE]]: "DOGE can only truly be accomplished by reigning in Congress to enact real government efficiency."[1]

Musk, co-leader with [[vivek-ramaswamy|Vivek Ramaswamy]] of Trump's Department of Government Efficiency advisory panel, had helped drive the wave of opposition to the bipartisan spending deal, with Trump and Vice President-elect [[jd-vance|JD Vance]] ultimately urging Republicans to oppose the measure. Several Democrats criticized Musk and claimed that he, not Trump, was driving the president-elect's position.[1]
