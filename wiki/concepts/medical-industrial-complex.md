# Medical Industrial Complex

The **Medical Industrial Complex** is the term Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] uses for the interlocking system of hospitals, insurers, pharmaceutical manufacturers and licensing bodies that he argues is bankrupting American businesses while "facilitating population control and modernized eugenics."[1]

## Cost

The article traces the system's economic weight: as far back as 2004 the United States was spending roughly $1.7 trillion a year on healthcare, about 14% of GDP, and that figure had grown to $4.3 trillion by 2021 — growth dramatically outpacing the economy as a whole.[1]

As an illustration DeLarme cites [[general-motors|General Motors]] and the Michigan saying "as General Motors goes, so goes America." Before Obama forced the company's CEO to resign in 2009, GM had received $17.4 billion in bailouts and still could not recover, in part because of the enormous cost of employee healthcare — the company was spending significantly more on healthcare than on the steel and other materials needed to build its cars.[1]

## Critique

DeLarme quotes an unnamed source describing the medical industry as a "Church of Biological Mysticism" with physicians as its "high priests," and argues that modern medicine — barely a century old, against the three-thousand-year records of Chinese and Ayurvedic systems — has been badly overtrusted. In his account the perception that these "emperors" are naked has only grown since COVID-19.[1]

The structural cause he identifies is the [[flexner-report|Flexner Report]] and the Rockefeller–Carnegie reorganization of medical education, which produced a profession trained in synthetic drug therapy and ignorant of nutrition, wellness and prevention, and a pharmaceutical industry largely built from petroleum.[1] He points to the mid-century cigarette advertising campaigns that used doctors — actors playing doctors — to reassure a public worried about "coffin nails" and "smoker's hack," with Camel still running such ads in the late 1940s and cigarette campaigns appearing in medical journals, as a precedent for professional consensus later proven false.[1]
