# Sarbanes-Oxley Act

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 is a United States federal statute that regulates financial record keeping and documentation, enacted in the aftermath of corporate accounting scandals in which large businesses defrauded the government, the markets and the public while their auditors covered up the fraud.[1] The law was intended to punish corruption and to reestablish public trust and confidence in the securities markets.[1]

## Purpose and effect on corporate America

Commentary published by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] argues that the impact of Sarbanes-Oxley on corporate America, on auditors and on financial reporting "cannot be understated," and that Section 404 of the Act — the internal-controls attestation requirement — profoundly reshaped the professional services industry.[1] Writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] has argued that the compliance burden created by Section 404 contributed to the growth of what she describes as the "unmoored consulting influence" that dominates modern corporate governance, a theme she covers on the Badlands program Culture of Change.[1]

The rationale underpinning the statute is that auditors attest to the validity of corporate books; if auditors attest to fraud, confidence in the markets rightly disappears. The entire system, in this framing, hinges on the perception of independent auditors whom the public can trust to ensure that corporations tell the truth about their businesses.[1]

## Use against January 6 defendants

A provision of Sarbanes-Oxley — the obstruction-of-an-official-proceeding statute originally aimed at the destruction or alteration of records in federal investigations — became one of federal prosecutors' favored charges against defendants who breached the Capitol on January 6, 2021.[1] Approximately 152 people were charged under the provision, more than 100 were sentenced, and the offense carries a maximum penalty of up to 20 years' imprisonment.[1] Badlands commentary characterized the discovery that a financial-fraud statute was being applied to protest defendants as "black pill material," noting that the charge only drew judicial restraint once the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] agreed to review it in [[fischer-v-united-states|Fischer v. United States]].[1]
