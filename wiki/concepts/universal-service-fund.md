# Universal Service Fund

The Universal Service Fund is an $8 billion federal program that subsidizes the availability and affordability of high-speed internet and telephone service for millions of Americans, including rural libraries and low-income communities.[1]

## 2025 Supreme Court challenge

On March 26, 2025, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] heard a conservative-backed challenge to the program that could jeopardize service for millions of beneficiaries.[1] Reporting on the case highlighted the Arkansas River Valley Regional Library System, whose seven libraries serve a rural population with a median family income below $46,000 and whose librarians mounted rooftop antennas during the pandemic to extend internet signal to residents.[1] Regional library director Misty Hawkins said that "lives are being changed through the distribution of a computer, through a connection, or a person being able to have résumé assistance, or being able to get their GED."[1]

## Nondelegation stakes

Beyond the telecommunications subsidies themselves, the case carried broad potential repercussions for how much power [[congress|Congress]] may delegate to federal agencies to make decisions about implementing legislation.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands commentary grouped the case with the congressional fight over public broadcasting funding as fronts in an information war, describing the administrative state as being dismantled narratively before being dismantled in fact.[1]

## Questions presented

Badlands coverage the day before argument set out the three questions the Court agreed to consider in the consolidated cases dealing with congressional funding of [[federal-communications-commission|FCC]] "universal service subsidy programs" and its "mandatory contributions from telecommunications carriers": whether Congress violated the [[nondelegation-doctrine|nondelegation doctrine]] by authorizing the Commission to determine, within the limits set forth in Section 254, the amount providers must contribute to the Fund; whether the Commission violated the doctrine by using the administrator's (USAC's) financial projections in computing universal service contribution rates; and whether the combination of Congress's conferral of authority on the Commission and the Commission's delegation of administrative responsibilities to USAC violates the doctrine.[2] The Court additionally directed the parties to be prepared to argue why the case was not moot "in light of the challengers' failure to seek preliminary relief before the Fifth Circuit."[2] [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] said it would carry the hearing live.[2]

## Oral argument and likely outcome

Following the Wednesday, March 26, 2025 oral arguments, reporting indicated the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] leaned toward upholding the subsidy program, described as a $9 billion fund supporting phone and internet service in rural areas and schools.[3] The dispute gave the justices an opportunity to examine the [[nondelegation-doctrine|nondelegation doctrine]], which prevents [[congress|Congress]] from delegating its legislative authority to the executive branch; the Court had not struck down a statute under the doctrine in 90 years, though anti-regulatory groups hoped the conservative supermajority would revitalize it by finding that Congress handed the [[federal-communications-commission|FCC]] too much power.[3]

Justice [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]] probed the bounds of the FCC's authority with a hypothetical in which the commission ordered that every American be provided access to [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s Starlink, suggesting the government's position would permit it; "It sounds like it. It's a pretty good deal. I'd like one," Gorsuch joked.[3] The case was described as the latest at the Court to take aim at federal agency power.[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who covered the session on Badlands Live, reported that two cases had been consolidated for argument and expressed surprise at how much of the hearing centered on taxes versus fees as a matter of law and precedent, and on administrative costs versus program costs.[3] The name of Chief Justice John Marshall was invoked and the maxim that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" — from *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) — was debated, prompting the commentary that fees are hidden taxes and that it is time to make this a matter of law.[3]
