# A Glaring Double Standard

"A Glaring Double Standard," subtitled "Should the 'Freedom of Speech' Apply to All Speech?", is an opinion essay published by Ryan DeLarme through Badlands Media on June 19, 2025. The article argues that prominent American conservatives who spent the years between roughly 2016 and 2023 denouncing campus "safe spaces" and cancel culture abandoned those commitments after the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack on Israel, and that this reversal is being exploited by what the author calls the "interagency blob" or deep state to advance government-sponsored censorship. The piece carries the standard Badlands Media disclaimer that contributor articles represent the opinions of their authors.

## The summer of 2020 and the rise of "safe spaces"

The essay opens with a recollection of the American political climate in 2020, which the author characterizes by "social and ethnic tensions" following the death of George Floyd, "gender hysteria... approaching its zenith," the COVID-19 pandemic, and unusually sharp partisan polarization ahead of what the article calls one of the most consequential elections in the nation's history. It describes a "highly reactionary time" of riots, lootings, shootings, and unrest in the streets and on college campuses.

Against that backdrop, the article traces the history of the term "safe spaces", which it says first gained prominence in the mid-1960s within the LGBTQ+ community and the feminist movement to describe venues such as gay and lesbian bars where people could gather free of harassment. The idea, the source argues, later migrated into educational institutions and was adopted "largely by white, middle-class social justice crusaders" to designate spaces where marginalized groups could feel supported.

According to the article, safe spaces reemerged forcefully between 2016 and 2020 as part of what it calls "some new, mutant strain of liberalism" working to undo prior civil rights victories. Libertarians, portions of the right and center, and some anti-establishment leftists criticized the concept — some viewing it as a regression toward segregation, others as evidence of "a mass form of arrested development."

## Campus deplatforming of conservative speakers

The source states that when conservative personalities such as Ben Shapiro or Charlie Kirk appeared on college campuses they were met with "howls of execration" and demands that universities cancel their events so that campuses could become safe spaces — not for ethnic or sexual minorities, but "for anyone who disagreed with the speaker's opinions." It reports that Shapiro had at least five major speaking engagements canceled by universities between 2017 and 2019, and that Kirk had several as well.

The article says this dynamic became a meme on the right, with "countless influencers and pundits" mocking liberals as cowardly and weak through "low-effort, boilerplate memes." It argues the ribbing turned to genuine outrage when demands for safe spaces "metastasized into calls for political censorship, both on campus and online," producing event cancellations, disruptions, and self-censorship among conservative students.

The essay cites the 2020 college free speech rankings published by the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (FIRE), which found that 62% of college students believed shouting down speakers was acceptable to some degree, and that conservative students reported higher rates of self-censorship — 73% for strong Republicans versus 52% for strong Democrats.

## Government-sponsored censorship as the underlying agenda

The article contends that what appeared to casual observers to be an organic culture war in fact concealed an agenda that "did not care about protecting people from 'hate speech'" but sought to build public support for government-sponsored censorship. The essay offers a working definition, describing government-sponsored censorship as actions taken by a government to suppress or restrict the dissemination of information, ideas, or opinions, including both direct censorship through laws and regulations and "indirect methods like pressuring private entities to censor content."

It argues that liberals of an earlier era regarded censorship as repugnant, but after years of partisan strife and psychological manipulation had "been convinced to beg for government censorship against their perceived enemies: MAGA conservatives." The author stresses that the "deep state" — described as "the unelected interagency blob that's been running the show in this country for decades" — is not exclusively pro-Democrat or liberal-sympathetic; it exists in both parties and "cares not which cause brings sufficient public support for its machinations." A recurring refrain in the essay states: "The people who want to erode your freedoms do not care if you acquiesce for a left-wing cause or a right-wing cause, as long as the needle is moved."

## Eyal Yakoby and the inversion of the safe-space argument

The article's central illustration of hypocrisy is Eyal Yakoby, described as a recent University of Pennsylvania graduate and one of Shapiro's "biggest amplifiers," who gained prominence as a vocal advocate against antisemitism on college campuses. The source notes that Yakoby delivered a speech at a House press conference preceding the congressional testimony of the presidents of the University of Pennsylvania, Harvard, and MIT, an event facilitated by House Speaker Mike Johnson, whom Yakoby praised for showing "moral clarity" on antisemitism.

The article quotes Yakoby's remarks at length:

> "… Since October 7th, American Jews have been under attack. Penn, once renowned for groundbreaking discoveries like the mRNA vaccine is now a chilling landscape of hatred and hostility… has devolved into and arena where Jewish students tiptoe through their days; uncertain and unsafe. During COVID, strict guidelines governed everything from class attendance to graduation walks, yet now, when students and faculty defy policies to intimidate Jewish students, where is the same resolute enforcement? As a student, despite what my university says, I do not feel safe, let me be clear; I do not feel safe."

The author argues that Yakoby "literally calls for COVID-grade guidelines and enforcement to protect Jewish students," and that the thing students are being protected from is "hearing a dissenting opinion about the conduct of the foreign nation of Israel."

Addressing Yakoby's reference to "attacks," the article cites reporting on [[anti-defamation-league|Anti-Defamation League]] figures counting 1,200 "antisemitic incidents" on U.S. college campuses between October 7, 2023, and September 24, 2024, including assaults, vandalism, harassment, and hate speech. The source asserts that the vast majority of these fell under "harassment" and "hate speech," and argues that on closer inspection many amount to differences of opinion — criticism of the government of Israel, or expressions of sympathy for "the starved in Gaza or those bombed in Raffa," being reported as harassment. The author writes that he is "just as unmoved and unwilling to scrap free expression" by Yakoby's assertions as by "young liberals squealing about fascism."

## Comedy, Dave Portnoy, and the limits of humor

The essay extends the argument to comedy, questioning whether "anti-cancel culture 'manosphere'-adjacent comedians" would still be permitted to make edgy jokes about Israel or Jews. It focuses on Dave Portnoy, founder of Barstool Sports, described as a leader in the effort to destigmatize humor in the age of political correctness. The article reports that on June 4 Portnoy appeared on a Barstool podcast and had what the author describes as "one of the most loony, unhinged meltdowns I've ever witnessed on a podcast," premised on the belief that jokes about American Jews, however light-hearted, would get them killed in the street.

The author writes that he did not come away persuaded that Jews are so uniquely endangered that all jokes at their expense should be self-censored, observing that jokes are permitted about "gays, trans people, Blacks, Hispanics, the homeless, drug addicts, the mentally challenged, amputees, and dead Gazans." The essay adds that within this framework Jews who do not support Israel are dismissed as "self-hating."

The article states it is not attempting to "rag on Jews or Israel," but to criticize what it calls the "overblown, amorphous and often recklessly employed term 'antisemitism'" — a term the author says has long been seen as actually causing antisemitism to proliferate, on the theory that "the harder you try to police the discourse around a topic, the more charged that topic will become." It draws the same parallel to COVID and conspiracy theories.

## "Hate speech" as the universal pretext

The essay argues that the ultimate goal is to convince the public that "hate speech" is dangerous enough to justify legal frameworks limiting speech. It describes the protection-from-hate-speech premise as "the foundation of every censorship framework that the interagency, international blob has advocated for over the course of the last decade," and identifies it as the same pretext used to justify the European Union's Digital Services Act, which the article characterizes as "highly repressive." The author's summary of the pattern: "The reasoning remains the same, but it seems that some snowflakes are more precious than others."

## The pre-October 7 free speech champions

The article names Bari Weiss, Dave Rubin, and outlets it labels "Con INC.," including the Daily Wire and the Babylon Bee, as self-proclaimed free speech champions who billed themselves as opposed to cancel culture and rallied against the policing of public discourse — "especially when conservatives were fired from their jobs for expressing their beliefs."

Three examples are given. First, Christopher Rufo tweeted on December 20, 2021, in response to the firing of Tony Kinnett, an Indianapolis teacher who posted a video about critical race theory in public schools: "Outrageous: this brave teacher stood against critical race theory indoctrination in his school district—and they fired him."

Second, Tim Pierce quoted Ben Shapiro in a February 2021 Daily Wire piece titled "Shapiro: Gina Carano Firing Part Of A Movement To 'Expel' Half Of America." The article recounts that Disney fired Gina Carano over an Instagram image depicting a Jewish woman running from Nazi guards, captioned with the observation that "Jews were beaten in the streets, not by Nazi soldiers but by their neighbors…even by children," and asking how hating someone for being Jewish differs from hating someone for their political views. Shapiro called the firing "indicative of where we are in the culture, and it is a terrible moment for American culture," adding that "we are now in the middle of a mass social movement to expel half of the American population from the body politic."

Third, Bari Weiss's 2021 Commentary Magazine op-ed "We Got Here Because of Cowardice. We Get Out With Courage" is quoted:

> "…So the tools themselves are not just replaced, but repudiated. And in so doing, persuasion—the purpose of argument—is replaced with public shaming. Moral complexity is replaced with moral certainty. Facts are replaced with feelings. Ideas are replaced with identity. Forgiveness is replaced with punishment. Debate is replaced with de-platforming. Diversity is replaced with homogeneity of thought. Inclusion, with exclusion… As Douglas Murray has put it: 'The problem is not that the sacrificial victim is selected. The problem is that the people who destroy his reputation are permitted to do so by the complicity, silence and slinking away of everybody else.'"

The author states agreement with Rufo, Shapiro, and Weiss in these instances — that people should be allowed to hold unpalatable opinions, that the First Amendment is the most important right in the Bill of Rights, and that it is dangerous for employers to enforce beliefs through threat of firing. "The big difference, however," he writes, "is that I didn't suddenly stop believing this after October 7th, 2023."

## Firings of Israel critics

The article lists individuals terminated for dissenting views on Israel's military actions in Gaza. Maura Finkelstein, a tenured Jewish professor at Pennsylvania's Muhlenberg College, lost her job over remarks about "Zionists"; The Intercept reported that she "was the subject of a campaign of thousands of anonymous, bot-generated emails sent every minute for over 24 hours to the school's administrators—as well as local news outlets and politicians." Hesen Jabr, a nurse at NYU Langone Health, was fired for comparing the ethnic cleansing of Gaza by Israel to the ethnic cleansing of European Jews by the Nazi party. Nikki Noor Aytoglu, a dental hygienist, was terminated after social media posts labeling Jews "conniving" and suggesting Israeli involvement in 9/11.

The article asserts that after these firings, none of the right-wing free speech advocates warned of the dangers of cancel culture as they had previously. It identifies the advocacy group StopAntisemitism as "the primary moving factor in pressuring the employers to fire the individuals mentioned above, often doxxing them via StopAntisemitism's X account."

The essay singles out Chaya Raichik, operator of the Libs of TikTok account, as "perhaps one of the most blatant of right-wing hypocrites," for amplifying StopAntisemitism's posts about the firings with comments such as "Consequences for hate are necessary" — despite having been doxxed herself by Taylor Lorenz and then touring media outlets warning of the dangers of doxxing, saying, "Every time I have to give my name for something, my heart skips a beat. It's unnerving."

The author clarifies that he is not arguing the speech in question was defensible, nor that employers lack the right to fire people; his concern is "how easily people will abandon their ethics and stance on an issue when the tables are turned." By way of contrast, he notes commentary about Palestinian civilians, citing Florida State Senator Randy Fine, who posted a series of tweets claiming there were no innocent Palestinian civilians, calling them "demons that live on Earth" who "deserve death," and demanding that "Gaza must be destroyed," with no comparable doxxing campaign.

## True colors shown brightly

The article's second major section argues that the aftermath of the October 7 attacks followed a familiar pattern: a surge of traumatic social media posts followed by "a deluge of atrocity-stoked revenge porn" designed to build support for another bombing campaign in Gaza. It lists prior Israeli operations in the enclave — Operation Summer Rains (2006), Operation Autumn Clouds (2006), Operation Cast Lead (2008–2009), Operation Pillar of Defense (2012), Operation Protective Edge (2014), Operation Guardian of the Walls (2021), and Operation Breaking Dawn (2022).

The essay observes that after major tragedies, and especially acts of terrorism such as September 11, politicians "scuttle like cockroaches to capitalize on the fear and anger of the people." Following October 7, the article says, many conservative politicians began advocating censorship using the same formulations liberals had used during "Trump 1.0" — that speech must be curtailed because it could incite violence or endanger minority groups. The author calls this "the EXACT same reasoning, urging the public to accept the exact same solution: the trampling of free speech."

The first politician identified as adopting such measures is Florida Governor [[ron-desantis|Ron DeSantis]], then still competing against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] for the Republican nomination. Within days of the attack, DeSantis forced the deactivation of the National Students for Justice in Palestine chapter at the University of South Florida. Reuters is quoted: "Florida's university system, working with Governor Ron DeSantis, ordered colleges on Tuesday to shut down a pro-Palestinian student organization, marking the first U.S. state to outlaw the group whose national leadership backed Hamas' attack on Israel." The article notes that the local chapter had not made the statements at issue; the national chapter had said it supported Hamas's actions as justified by decades of Israeli brutality. The author calls that stance reprehensible but insists it remains protected speech, adding that "Repellent speech is still free speech" and that one has just as much right to say "I hope that the IDF murders every last man, woman and child in Gaza," as figures including Nikki Haley and Lindsey Graham have intimated.

The article criticizes Haley for the rhetorical structure of prefacing censorship proposals with concessions — "College campuses are allowed free speech, BUT…" — with the true intention following the "but," in her case taking an existing law limiting the parameters of free expression and giving it "teeth." It adds that "one can be both a Jew or 'Semite' and an anti-Zionist at the same time," again highlighting what it calls the amorphous nature of the term antisemitism, and asks whether Haley and DeSantis had not recently been railing against placing specific minorities on a pedestal when liberals did so with BIPOC and LGBTQ+ groups.

## The Antisemitism Awareness Act and Executive Order 14188

The article identifies the Antisemitism Awareness Act (AAA), which it says passed the U.S. House in May 2024 by a vote of 320–91, as the point at which the trend became dangerous. The act would codify the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA) definition of antisemitism into federal civil rights law, directing the Department of Education to apply it when investigating antisemitic discrimination on college campuses.

The IHRA working definition is quoted: "Antisemitism is a certain perception of Jews, which may be expressed as hatred toward Jews. Rhetorical and physical manifestations of antisemitism are directed toward Jewish or non-Jewish individuals and/or their property, toward Jewish community institutions and religious facilities."

The article argues that what constitutes "hatred" is a gray area typically determined by whichever political party holds power, citing the multi-year effort to classify political speech as hate speech — including labeling the claim that COVID originated in a lab as anti-Asian hate speech "despite it being a U.S.-funded lab," and treating opposition to pediatric gender-affirming care as hate speech. It contends the IHRA definition's examples are overly broad and vaguely worded, that the ambiguity will lead to inconsistent, politically biased enforcement, and that this could stoke antisemitic sentiment and undermine the bill's stated intent.

The source notes that some of the bill's biggest supporters came from the interagency blob, naming Elissa Slotkin (described as a CIA analyst turned Democratic politician), Seth Moulton (described as both a CIA figure and a Young Global Leader at the World Economic Forum), and Robert Mendez (described as a regular fixture in Brussels and at the Munich Security Conference, which the article calls an annual gathering of military-industrial complex functionaries).

Turning to the executive branch, the article describes Trump's Executive Order 14188 of January 29, 2025, as expanding measures against antisemitism, including monitoring and potentially deporting non-citizens such as international students who engage in speech deemed antisemitic — often on the basis of the IHRA definition, which the author says encompasses many legitimate criticisms of Israel.

## Conclusion

The essay concludes by comparing the present moment to the aftermath of 9/11, when, it argues, politicians convinced Americans to relinquish longstanding rights in the name of safety, producing "such gross government excesses as the Patriot Act and the mass, warrantless spying on Americans by the intelligence community."

It sets out a historical sequence of accusations: opposition to the war in Afghanistan marked one as pro-terrorist; opposition to "the massacre in Libya" as pro-Gaddafi; opposition to sending billions to Ukraine as pro-Putin; and criticism of Israel today as both pro-Hamas and antisemitic. The article calls this "the same pseudo-moral psychological manipulation framework," with "antisemitism" replacing "terrorism" as "the cudgel to justify censorship."

The hypocrisy of figures such as Ben Shapiro, Bari Weiss, and Dave Rubin, the article argues, "reveals a troubling truth: their commitment to free speech was always conditional, tethered to tribal loyalties rather than principle," and the interagency blob exploits that weakness to advance frameworks like the Antisemitism Awareness Act and Executive Order 14188 that "threaten to criminalize dissent under vague definitions of 'hate speech.'"

The closing argument is that the erosion of free speech, whether driven by left-wing or right-wing causes, "serves the same end: control," and that true defenders of the First Amendment "must reject this selective outrage and defend all speech, no matter how repugnant, because once the precedent is set, the blob will not hesitate to turn it against us all."

The article ends with a note directing readers to more of DeLarme's work at the Post-Liberal, and Badlands Media's standard appeal for paid subscriptions to support its "collective of citizen journalists."
