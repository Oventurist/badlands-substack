# Toronto Desistance Study

The Toronto desistance study is a decades-long follow-up study of boys referred to a clinic for [[gender-dysphoria|gender dysphoria]], begun in Toronto, Ontario in 1975 and published in the journal *Frontiers in Psychiatry* in 2021.[1] Badlands Media contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] described it as research that, once completed, should have had a devastating impact on the push to normalise [[gender-affirming-care|gender affirming care]] in North America.[1]

## Design and researchers

The study was conducted by three researchers — Devita Singh, [[susan-bradley|Susan J. Bradley]] and [[kenneth-zucker|Kenneth J. Zucker]] — working out of the [[centre-for-addiction-and-mental-health|Centre for Addiction and Mental Health]] (CAMH).[1] It followed 139 birth-assigned males who had been clinic-referred for gender dysphoria between the ages of three and twelve, and reported, in the authors' words, "follow-up data on the largest sample to date of boys clinic-referred for gender dysphoria (n = 139) with regard to gender identity and sexual orientation."[1]

## Findings

Thirty-four years later, the researchers compiled the follow-up data. Only 12% of the gender-confused youths had continued to struggle with gender dysphoria; almost 90 percent had completely desisted.[1] The paper stated that "of the 139 participants, only 17 (12.2%) were classified as persisters and the remaining 122 (87.8%) were classified as desisters."[1] Esler further noted that the majority of the desisters turned out to be gay, and argued on that basis that the mass transitioning of gender-nonconforming youth underway in Canada and the United States is a deeply homophobic movement.[1]

## Significance in Badlands coverage

Esler cited the study as one of three international sources — alongside the closure of the [[tavistock-institute|Tavistock]] gender clinic in the United Kingdom and Sweden's policy reversal — that he said should have decimated the narrative driving the medical transition of children, yet failed to slow its momentum.[1] He asked how a study with so many participants over so long a period could fail to send shockwaves through the gender-affirmative medical and academic community in Canada and America, answering with the essay's recurring refrain: "Information warfare."[1] He also framed it as especially striking that Canada maintained momentum behind the [[gender-affirming-care|gender affirmative model]] despite being the source of one of the most definitive studies on gender dysphoric youth in the world.[1]
