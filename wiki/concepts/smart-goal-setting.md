# SMART Goal Setting

SMART Goal Setting is a management framework created by [[george-doran|George Doran]] in 1981 which holds that effective goals should be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.[1]

In a February 13, 2025 guest essay for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], election analyst [[seth-keshel|Seth Keshel]] applied the model to the problem of reforming American elections during the second Trump administration, arguing that the compressed calendar before the [[2026-midterm-elections|2026 midterms]] made a disciplined goal-setting method necessary rather than optional.[1]

## The five criteria as applied to election reform

**Specific.** Keshel argued that specificity requires the public to understand a goal in both definition and scope, and that over-complicated problem sets cannot be solved within time or legal constraints.[1] His stated goal was to evaluate each state's system of elections for laws and conduct that disenfranchise its own voters — and by extension voters of other states in national elections — to enact laws and procedures providing transparency and fairness, and to challenge disenfranchising laws with the full strength of the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]].[1]

**Measurable.** Progress would be quantified against his [[eight-cardinal-sins|Eight Cardinal Sins]] of election administration, practices that are not universally shared among the fifty states, unlike electronic voting systems which are used everywhere.[1] Identifying where these demerits are most concentrated and working backward from there makes the mission measurable.[1]

**Achievable.** Restraint, he wrote, is critical: while his ultimate end state is the more complex [[ten-points-to-true-election-integrity|Ten Points to True Election Integrity]], the near-term achievable target is exposing [[voter-registration|voter registration]] corruption in blue-wall states rather than persuading skeptics that machines flip votes — an argument he considered harder to make to general audiences, whose only "normie-proof" objection to machines is the absence of transparency and auditability.[1]

**Relevant.** The goal aligns with the Trump 47 administration's broader commitment to gutting corruption and increasing transparency, which Keshel noted had been opposed by those who enriched themselves at taxpayers' expense.[1] He described the electoral process as the most critical means of self-government, standing between peaceful orderliness and chaotic upheaval.[1]

**Time-bound.** Working backward from Election Day 2026, Keshel estimated a 90-day [[election-reform-committee|Election Reform Committee]] cycle, 21 days for presidential deliberation and executive action, 90 days of injunctions and hearings, and 90 days for a Supreme Court appeal — roughly 300 days before replacement of bad systems could even begin.[1]
