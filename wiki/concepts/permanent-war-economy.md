# Permanent War Economy

The permanent war economy is an alternative name for the [[military-industrial-complex|military-industrial-congressional complex]] (MICC): a standing political-economic arrangement in which defence production, congressional appropriation and campaign finance are mutually reinforcing, so that continuous conflict becomes structurally necessary rather than incidental.[1]

## Origin of the term

Badlands Media traces the vocabulary to the farewell address of President [[dwight-eisenhower|Dwight Eisenhower]], delivered from the White House on 17 January 1961 and written by Malcolm Moos and Ralph E. Williams.[1] The draft phrase was "military-industrial-congressional complex," but the reference to Congress was edited out before Eisenhower delivered the speech.[1] The same underlying concept is what the article calls the permanent war economy.[1]

## Why the warning failed

The article argues Eisenhower's warning received little attention at the time, presumably because the CIA's [[operation-mockingbird|Operation Mockingbird]] had already given the Deep State a high degree of control over the corporate media — the primary tool for drumming up support for war.[1] Even as public awareness of the MICC's exploits has risen, the speech still falls on deaf ears whenever a new war arrives with a good marketing campaign, requiring only a few rounds of [[atrocity-propaganda|atrocity propaganda]] to produce popular support.[1]

## Money flows

Badlands Media contends that when large appropriations bills move taxpayer dollars in any direction, there are "six ways from Sunday" that money can be skimmed, diverted or appropriated — a fact the publication says applies to humanitarian aid as much as to military support packages.[1] The congressional component of the MICC receives its cut from the donor class, and the donor class in turn "get what they pay for" from the politicians they back.[1] This, the article argues, explains why officials can profitably cultivate support for both sides of a conflict even when the United States funds only one.[1]

## Representation gap

The article frames the permanent war economy as a crisis of representation: a majority of American voters opposed US support for Israel in 2023, many fearing a broader conflict, yet the permanent political class was in near-total agreement that Washington should back Israel.[1] If officials blatantly ignore constituent concerns, the article asks, who do they really work for — answering that the answer is all too often the donor class, particularly the military-industrial complex.[1]
