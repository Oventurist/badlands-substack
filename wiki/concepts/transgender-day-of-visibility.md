# Transgender Day of Visibility

The **Transgender Day of Visibility** is an annual observance held on March 31 each year, created by advocates fifteen years before 2024 as a way to celebrate transgender people and to raise awareness of the discrimination they face.[1] It was established in 2009 by [[rachel-crandall|Rachel Crandall]], the head of [[transgender-michigan|Transgender Michigan]], a Michigan-based transgender advocacy organization, and was first held on March 31, 2009; it has been observed on that same date ever since.[1]

## 2024 proclamation controversy

On Friday, March 29, 2024, President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] issued a presidential proclamation recognizing March 31 as Transgender Day of Visibility, as he had done every year since taking office.[1] Because Easter Sunday fell on March 31 in 2024, conservatives responded furiously, accusing the president — who is Catholic — of attempting to stifle Christian values by highlighting the observance during an important religious holiday.[1]

House leadership voiced its outrage, as did the [[donald-trump|Trump]] campaign, which demanded an apology from the [[white-house|White House]].[1] The White House pushed back, stating that "as a Christian who celebrates Easter with family, President Biden stands for bringing people together and upholding the dignity and freedoms of every American."[1] Transgender advocates characterized the controversy as manufactured, while [[politico|Politico]] framed the episode as the latest in a series of social conflicts enveloping the political process and the electoral cycle.[1]

Defenders of the proclamation noted that the date of Easter changes each year according to a calculation tied to lunar phases, making the 2024 overlap coincidental, and that multiple proclamations for the same date are common — Easter Sunday 2024 was also proclaimed Cesar Chavez Day.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] argued that the episode was best understood as a demonstration of the "online outrage machine" and its use for emotional manipulation.[1] In his reading, the controversy first enticed conservatives into divisive social-media eddies where they reinforced a shared sense of being attacked, with the goal of producing right-wing reactionary behavior and fear of the erasure of Christian identity, and second provided propaganda fodder to be fed to left-wing audiences.[1] Esler noted that because the observance has been fixed to March 31 since 2009, the two days would not overlap again for another eleven years, and concluded that the proclamation was intended to inflame and fuel the [[culture-war|culture war]].[1] He urged readers to consider how much conservative cognition and emotional energy the outrage machine consumes within the digital domain — energy he argued would be better spent in the physical domain or on building a parallel society — describing curated outrage as one of "the enemy's go-to advantages," especially when the audience is morally and objectively right about the nature of the attack.[1]

## Biden's denial of the proclamation (April 2024)

Days after the 2024 proclamation, President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] denied having issued it.[2] Asked about proclaiming Easter Sunday "trans day of visibility," Biden said flatly, "I didn't do that," as reported by RealClearPolitics' Philip Wegmann.[2] Asked about Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]]'s claim that he had, Biden responded, "he's thoroughly uninformed."[2] ZeroHedge, publishing the account on April Fool's Day, contrasted the denial with months of Democratic insistence that the president was sharp in private and asked "who's running the show."[2]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the observance was marked not only by the [[white-house|White House]] but by a large part of the Executive Branch and by all but two Colorado Democratic state legislators, arguing that such institutional coordination would, in a normal world, prompt journalistic questions about the true motives behind the proclamations.[2]
