# The Truman Show

*The Truman Show* is the 1998 film whose premise — a man whose entire life, including every relationship in it, is a staged production performed by paid actors — is used within [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary as the governing metaphor for mass media conditioning in the United States.[1]

## Use as a metaphor in Badlands commentary

In his October 2024 essay "The Aftermath of a Trump Victory," [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] opens with an unattributed retelling of the film's premise: a man born on an island who never leaves it, kept ashore by a childhood boating trauma and by a desire for new experience weaker than his fear, and unaware that his family, spouse, friends, employer and customers are all placed actors.[1] Nothing tragic ever happens to him; he is never bullied, mugged, burgled or laid off, and because this is the only world he knows, none of it appears contrived.[1] Carlson only reveals at the end of the parable that it is the plot of *The Truman Show*, with references to *The Matrix* and *The Wizard of Oz* mixed in.[1]

Carlson notes that Hollywood delivered this account of reality a quarter of a century ago, that *The Wizard of Oz* was released 85 years ago and its source book published 124 years ago, and says he does not claim to fully understand whether such works were warnings or boasts about what has been done.[1]

## The strangers on the street

A recurring element of the metaphor is the figure of the stranger who approaches Truman to tell him his life is fake and is then seized and dragged away by unseen handlers.[1] Carlson identifies members of the [[truth-community|truth community]] with those strangers, and identifies the handlers with Facebook and Twitter deplatforming, media outlets labelling dissenters conspiracy theorists, Snopes fact-checking, and government threats to jail people for spreading misinformation.[1] The "glitch in the matrix," the curtain in the Emerald City and the man behind it are used in the same passage as parallel images of the moment a conditioned person begins to notice that apparently random patterns are not random.[1]

Carlson uses the metaphor to pose the essay's central question — how a person deals with the discovery that no relationship, accomplishment or experience in their life was real — and to introduce the possibility that some will spiral downward or retreat further into the false reality rather than accept it.[1]

## Invoked as a model for manipulated reality (Carlson, 2023)

In "The Sting" (May 30, 2023), [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] proposed *The Truman Show* as a more accurate metaphor than "[[we-are-watching-a-movie|we are watching a movie]]" for the political manipulation he described.[2] Summarising the 1998 film, he wrote that Truman lives a life he thinks is real while everything in it is contrived and controlled and everyone around him — his parents, friends, wife, neighbours and coworkers — is an actor.[2] Truman's reactions might be real, Carlson noted, but they are all based on everything else being fake.[2] He paired the film with reality television such as MTV's *The Real World*, where scenes are not fully scripted but are entirely manipulated.[2]

## Cited as early predictive programming (2023)

In "The World is a Vampire" (January 24, 2023), [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] listed *The Truman Show* among the films that demonstrate [[predictive-programming|predictive programming]], writing that in 1998 it showed audiences how their lives are completely controlled and orchestrated without their knowing.[3] He summarised the premise as following a boy who grows into a man while living a life entirely orchestrated by television producers for the world to watch.[3] Carlson placed the film within a broader argument that entertainment manipulates thought, that people care about or fear things because the entertainment media tells them to, and that no one is exempt from the manipulation — with some finding it too hard to accept that their lives have been a lie and so choosing to accept the lies instead.[3]
