# Obstruction of an Official Proceeding (18 U.S.C. 1512(c)(2))

Obstruction of an official proceeding is a federal criminal offense codified at 18 U.S.C. 1512(c)(2), which makes it a crime to "corruptly" obstruct, impede or interfere with any official government proceeding and carries a maximum penalty of twenty years in prison.[1]

## Use in January 6 cases

Federal prosecutors applied the statute in more than three hundred cases arising from the storming of the Capitol on January 6, 2021, using it to describe the central event of that day: the disruption of the Electoral College vote certification then taking place inside the Capitol during a joint session of Congress.[1] As a general matter, defendants were charged under the provision when prosecutors believed they had evidence that a defendant's conduct played some role in halting the certification process or in driving lawmakers away from their duties.[1]

Almost as soon as the charge began appearing in Capitol riot cases, defense lawyers argued that the government was stretching the statute well beyond its intended scope.[1] Those challenges produced a pair of federal appeals court cases and were seen as capable of reaching the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]].[1]

## Relevance to the Trump prosecution

The statute became significant beyond the rank-and-file riot cases because it was among the three laws that prosecutors investigating [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s efforts to overturn the 2020 election identified to him as possible bases for an indictment.[1] Although the pending appellate challenges made inclusion of the count appear risky for special counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]], the way the law is written was described as making it almost uniquely suited to charging Trump, whose alleged conduct was aimed directly at the certification proceeding.[1]

Some legal analysts suggested Trump could attack the charge by arguing that he genuinely believed he had been robbed of victory through fraud and therefore could not be said to have acted "corruptly," the mental state the statute requires.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

Commentary by [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] in the Badlands News Brief of July 26, 2023 read the coverage as a signal of coming moves by both prosecutors and the president's defense, relaying a theory advanced by [[just-human|Just Human]] that Trump's strongest defense in a January 6 prosecution would be disclosure — placing evidence of election irregularities before a court and, through it, before a mainstream audience.[1] In that reading, the corrupt-intent element of 1512(c)(2) creates a legal opening through which claims the community had long circulated might be formally aired and codified in the public mind.[1]
