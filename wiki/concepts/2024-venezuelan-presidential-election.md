# 2024 Venezuelan Presidential Election

The July 2024 presidential election in [[venezuela]] produced a contested result in which opposition leaders and their foreign backers alleged fraud, while the government pressed the dispute through the country's courts.[1] Observers noted that the United States took a keen interest in the outcome and that Venezuela possesses the world's largest proven crude oil reserves.[1]

## Judicial review

Venezuela's Supreme Court of Justice (TSJ), the highest court in the country, examined the results, and the National Electoral Council (CNE) — the government body responsible for managing elections — submitted its records to the court in an attempt to resolve the dispute.[1] The TSJ asserted that its decision would be final and binding.[1] The opposition refused to take part in the process and was criticized for failing to present the evidence the court requested.[1]

Venezuelan constitutional expert Dr. Olga Alvarez argued that "no Venezuelan party that alleges to have 40 percent more votes than President Maduro would hesitate to present the evidence to the National Electoral Council," adding that "whoever cries election fraud must irrefutably prove it; the onus is on them, not on the CNE to prove there wasn't."[1]

## CIA allegations

Commentator [[niko-house|Niko House]] alleged that opposition leader [[maria-corina-machado|Maria Corina Machado]] "has been operating as a foreign agent on behalf of the United States" and that "the [[cia|CIA]] literally writes her a paycheck."[1] House described Venezuela's electoral system as containing multiple checks and balances that permit opposition monitoring of precinct-level counting, and said "the most simplistic explanation is the oil" for U.S. involvement.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] called the report a fascinating but non-definitive datapoint on which side the CIA was backing in what he termed a Venezuelan coup, and connected it to his research on [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]]'s pursuit of oil oligarchs at [[glencore]].[1] He noted that Maduro's civil action against some 40 "Big Oil" executives appeared to have contributed to executive actions by the Trump administration and to the indictment of former Glencore executive Alex Beard.[1] Observing that Maduro was calling for an audit while [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] had never sought one of the 2020 U.S. election, he wrote that "sunlight is the best disinfectant" and suggested a successful Venezuelan audit could serve as a template for the United States.[1]

## US recognition of González

Venezuela's [[national-electoral-council|National Electoral Council]] declared [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]] the winner of the July 28, 2024 vote on the following Monday, but hours later the main opposition coalition said it held contrary evidence in the form of more than two-thirds of the tally sheets printed by each electronic voting machine after polls closed.[2] On Thursday, August 1, 2024, the United States recognized opposition candidate [[edmundo-gonzalez|Edmundo González]] as the victor, with Secretary of State [[antony-blinken|Antony Blinken]] stating that "given the overwhelming evidence, it is clear to the United States and, most importantly, to the Venezuelan people that Edmundo González Urrutia won the most votes."[2] Multiple governments, including close Maduro allies, had called on the electoral body to release detailed vote counts as it had in previous elections.[2] Maduro answered the U.S. move with "The United States needs to keep its nose out of Venezuela!"[2]

Badlands commentary treated the unified Western position as a "reverse indicator," placing the episode in a lineage of contested foreign elections and regime-change operations running from the "show elections" of Saddam Hussein through the 2011 Libya intervention and the 2014 Maidan Revolution, and questioning whether the crisis amounted to an attempted coup against Maduro in retaliation for his multi-year purge of corrupt oil officials and his lawsuit against [[glencore|Glencore]], Trafigura and Vitol.[2]

## Candidate registration blocked

In the early hours of March 26, 2024, the Maduro regime blocked the Democratic Unitary Platform (PUD) opposition coalition from registering a candidate before the midnight deadline.[3] Opposition primary winner [[maria-corina-machado|María Corina Machado]], 56, who had been favored in polls to defeat [[nicolas-maduro|Maduro]] in the July elections, had already been barred, and her chosen successor, Dr. [[corina-yoris|Corina Yoris]], was blocked as well.[3] The [[white-house|White House]], through Press Secretary [[karine-jean-pierre|Karine Jean-Pierre]], said it was deeply concerned by the National Electoral Council's decision, was working with the international community toward inclusive and competitive elections, and suggested Venezuela could lose sanctions relief absent free and fair balloting.[3]

## Election night and rival victory claims

On the morning of Monday, July 29, 2024, both President [[nicolas-maduro|Nicolás Maduro]] and opposition rival [[edmundo-gonzalez|Edmundo González]] claimed victory, after a vote marked by accusations of underhand tactics and isolated incidents of violence.[4] Just after midnight, the country's electoral authority declared that Maduro had won a third term with 51% of the vote against 44% for González, despite multiple exit polls pointing to an opposition win.[4] The opposition had earlier said it had "reasons to celebrate" and asked supporters to keep monitoring the count.[4]

Maduro appeared at the presidential palace before cheering supporters, calling his reelection a triumph of peace and stability and reiterating his campaign assertion that Venezuela's electoral system is transparent; he said he would sign a decree that day to hold a "great national dialogue."[4] Fireworks sounded over Caracas as lighted drones formed a brightly-colored image of Maduro above the palace.[4] Opposition leader [[maria-corina-machado|María Corina Machado]] countered that González had won 70% of the vote and that multiple independent exit polls and quick counts decisively showed his victory, declaring in a joint statement with him: "Venezuela has a new president-elect and it is Edmundo Gonzalez. We won and the whole world knows it."[4] González said he was not calling on supporters to take to the streets or commit acts of violence.[4]

## Badlands commentary on American parallels

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], argued that the contested Venezuelan election was less a preview of America's future than "a marathon of reruns" of America's past.[4] She listed several parallels drawn from the coverage itself: the reinforcement of a "50/50 nation" framing as a predicate for narrow victory margins; the elevation of [[edison-research|Edison Research]] as an authoritative election-night source, noting that Edison did not go down in Venezuela as she said it had in the United States in 2020; Venezuela's status as the birthplace of [[dominion-voting-systems|Dominion Voting Systems]]; the role of the media in calling elections; the weaponization of government to jail political opposition; and the use of the judiciary to keep opponents off the ballot, a point she connected to Colorado Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] and the [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]] Fourteenth Amendment case.[4]

She also noted that the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]] had forecast the outcome two weeks in advance, quoting its observation that the opposition had been virtually absent from elections since 2015, with the government banning serious candidates, intimidating others, and rigging votes, and that Maduro's October 2023 agreement to hold a competitive, internationally monitored race was exchanged for a six-month U.S. lifting of sanctions on Venezuela's oil and gas sector, with provisions to reimpose them if he faltered on his democratic commitments.[4]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] offered a second take grounded in Info War analysis, arguing that when Western leaders, globalists, and the media complex uniformly call something a "threat to our Democracy" while Russia and China say the opposite, it is worth examining one's preexisting biases.[4] He said he did not know whether Maduro had won a real election or whether that would be a good thing, but that he did know "exactly what the establishment WANTS me to believe about it," concluding that narrative seeding was underway on both sides.[4]
