# Chatham House Rule

The Chatham House Rule is a protocol governing the confidentiality of discussions at private meetings.[1] It gives participants the discretion to use information gleaned from the talks but forbids the identification of speakers or participants, thereby ensuring anonymity.[1]

## Use at the Bilderberg Meeting

The rule is the mechanism by which the details of the annual [[bilderberg|Bilderberg Meeting]] remain obscured from the public.[1] A Bilderberg Meetings press release stated that "thanks to the private nature of the Meeting, the participants take part as individuals rather than in any official capacity, and hence are not bound by the conventions of their office or by pre-agreed positions."[1]

Badlands Media contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] criticized this framing, writing that the Bilderbergs "arrive as individuals, hence they need not make their discussions public, but they act as one," and that permitting elected officials and other custodians of public rights to engage in such behavior amounts to open conspiracy tolerated by supposedly free countries.[1]

## Conflict with US open-meetings law

In "Poison Fruit of the Decision Tree," Deschamps quoted the rule in full — "When a meeting, or part thereof, is held under the Chatham House Rule, participants are free to use the information received, but neither the identity nor the affiliation of the speaker(s), nor that of any other participant, may be revealed" — and named it as the answer to how officials can attend secret meetings where global policy and national interests are discussed without provoking a scandal.[2] He attributed the rule to [[chatham-house|Chatham House]], the Royal Institute of International Affairs, which he called the older brother of the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]].[2]

Deschamps argued that the rule allows government officeholders to conspire with people they should not, and that it does not conform to the spirit of the [[sunshine-act|Government in the Sunshine Act]].[2] In his account the rule incorrectly assumes that information may be received and exchanged so long as identities are withheld when the information is later used or passed on, whereas the Sunshine Act is not about privacy for officeholders, nor about using shared information to indirectly influence other officeholders, but about letting voters keep an eye on their elected officials.[2] Meetings conducted under the rule, including the annual [[bilderberg|Bilderberg]] gatherings held every year since 1954, therefore constitute in his view a violation of the sacred trust between citizens and their trustees and of the spirit of officials' oaths of office.[2]
