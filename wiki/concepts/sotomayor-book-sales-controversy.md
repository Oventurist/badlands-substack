# Sotomayor Book Sales Controversy

The **Sotomayor book sales controversy** concerns reporting in July 2023 that Supreme Court Justice [[sonia-sotomayor|Sonia Sotomayor]]'s taxpayer-funded staff repeatedly prodded public institutions hosting her to purchase her memoir and children's books.[1]

## Associated Press reporting

Amid a media campaign against conservative Supreme Court justices in the wake of *Dobbs v. Jackson*, the [[associated-press|Associated Press]] examined the other justices and reported on Sotomayor.[1] The AP noted that few names are bigger than Sotomayor for colleges and libraries seeking a guest lecturer, and that she had benefited from schools' purchases of hundreds — sometimes thousands — of her books.[1] Her writings had earned her at least $3.7 million since she joined the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Court]] in 2009.[1]

Details of the events, largely out of public view, were obtained through more than 100 open records requests to public institutions, producing tens of thousands of pages of documents that offered a rare look at the justices beyond their official duties.[1] In Sotomayor's case the documents revealed repeated examples of taxpayer-funded court staff performing tasks for the justice's book ventures — work that employees in other branches of government are barred from doing.[1] American Thinker characterized the arrangement as a pay-to-play operation run by Sotomayor and her aide Anh Le, muscling universities into hosting her in exchange for large book purchases.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] framed the episode as a question of whether officeholders should leverage the social status of their positions for personal gain, comparing it to a president using White House staff to arrange interviews, contact publishers, or edit drafts of a personal book.[1] He argued that appropriated resources of personnel, time, and energy exist to carry out the functions of the government positions their holders occupy, and that those resources come from the people through taxation and government income-generating schemes the people permit.[1]

Deschamps grounded the argument in contract principles, writing that all laws are ultimately agreements and that using entrusted resources outside the agreed purpose constitutes a violation of trust, bad faith, and dishonor — illustrated by the analogy of borrowing a friend's car for the grocery store and instead using it for another purpose.[1] He contended that if anyone in government should know the lawful boundaries of appropriated resources, it should be a justice of the Supreme Court, comparing the misuse to a firefighter dousing a home in gasoline or a doctor putting arsenic in the candies at the front desk.[1] He concluded that such conduct indicates either incompetence in duty and honor or corrupt intent, and that corruption across American institutions is the norm rather than the exception, closing with the question of whether a president can fire a judge — "let's ask [[donald-trump|Trump]] what he thinks after 2024."[1]
