# Sanctuary Cities and States

"Sanctuary" jurisdictions are U.S. cities and states whose policies limit local cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. In January 2026 these policies were at the center of a national confrontation over federal-state relations following the fatal shooting of [[alex-jeffrey-pretti|Alex Jeffrey Pretti]] by federal immigration agents in Minneapolis.[1]

## The 2026 controversy

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the dispute around the slogan "no one is above the law," which Democrats invoked repeatedly during the prosecutions of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] under the Biden administration.[1] She argued that in 2026 state sanctuary policies openly violate federal law, posing the question of whether state governments are themselves above the law.[1] The commentary noted that cities have created "newcomer playbooks" to provide aid, comfort and access to entitlement programs to foreign nationals in the country illegally, and that brick-and-mortar businesses have closed temporarily when [[immigration-and-customs-enforcement|ICE]] activity is reported in their areas, ostensibly because they employ illegal workers.[1]

The Badlands piece also drew a historical parallel to Prohibition, describing that era — during which Al Capone was public enemy number one — as perhaps the greatest season of federal civil overreach before COVID, and noting that the anniversary of Capone's death fell the day before publication.[1] Trump had frequently compared his four indictments to Capone's single indictment during the 2024 campaign.[1]

## Public opinion

Despite what the article described as a whole-of-legacy-press effort to claim Americans oppose the administration's immigration enforcement, the piece cited polling showing Americans remain divided along party lines, with public sentiment essentially unchanged since the summer of 2025.[1] The commentary asserted that Trump won the popular vote on a mandate to remove criminal invaders and that federal immigration enforcement in sanctuary cities and states was expected to continue for the foreseeable future.[1]

## Busing and the 2023 hypocrisy debate

In October 2023, comedian [[bill-maher|Bill Maher]] argued on "Real Time" that border-state Republicans who bused migrants to sanctuary metro areas — mainly in the Northeast — had "called the Democrats' bluff about sanctuary cities," leaving the Democrats looking like "sanctuary city hypocrites."[2] He noted that the receiving cities did not like the arrangement, citing New York Mayor [[eric-adams|Eric Adams]]'s warning that the influx was "going to destroy our city," and Adams's travel to Mexico, Ecuador and Colombia to tell prospective migrants directly that New York had no room.[2] Maher framed the episode against President [[joe-biden|Biden]]'s broader policy U-turn on the southern border that same week.[2]

## Bracing for mass deportations (January 2025)

As President-elect [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] prepared to take office, Democrat-run cities and states faced growing uncertainty over how effective sanctuary policies would be under the incoming administration, party insiders told Newsweek.[3] One Democratic Party donor who supports those vulnerable to federal immigration policies described unease within Democratic circles that sanctuary cities — where local governments limit cooperation with federal immigration authorities — could be targeted.[3] Rep. Zoe Lofgren (D-Calif.) suggested that local law enforcement's lack of desire to search for undocumented immigrants could matter more in stemming deportations than sanctuary rules themselves, while Sen. [[bill-cassidy|Bill Cassidy]] (R-La.), who proposed legislation barring sanctuary states from offering Medicaid coverage to undocumented immigrants, vowed: "We will close the border."[3]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that by 2025 the citizens of sanctuary jurisdictions were "done," arguing blue-city leaders had created a separate class of residents exempted from criminal liability and subsidized at public expense, and that fear of being called racist no longer kept the public quiet.[3] Drawing on her experience in Denver, she described a progression from the "empathy play" of "migrants are our strength" to home invasions, jewelry-store and gun-shop thefts, stolen cars, apartment-complex takeovers, kidnappings and torture, citing an Aurora resident whose fingernails were pulled out by Tren de Aragua kidnappers.[3] She noted that Democratic politicians had begun agreeing that criminals should be deported — a marked improvement over three months earlier — and said she hoped [[tom-homan|Tom Homan]] was serious about imprisoning officials who obstruct removals.[3]

## The 2023 busing standoff and the "visualization of the crisis"

In late December 2023 the mayors of Chicago, New York City and Denver renewed pleas for federal help and coordination with Texas as asylum-seekers arrived in their cities by bus and plane; Governor [[greg-abbott|Greg Abbott]]'s busing operation had moved more than 80,000 migrants to Democratic-led cities since 2022, recently supplemented by chartered flights.[4] New York Mayor [[eric-adams|Eric Adams]] complained that buses arrived without warning at any hour and outside designated drop-off zones, preventing orderly assistance.[4]

Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] fixed on the mayors' statement that "for many months, we were able to keep the visualization of this crisis from hitting our streets, but we have reached a breaking point," arguing that it revealed their true concern to be optics — "we're losing the optics of the Great Migration" — rather than the underlying crisis.[4] She argued the sanctuary designation originated as a political rebuke of border communities sounding the alarm about conditions at the border, and that those communities had, to their credit, forced liberal cities to put their money where their rhetoric was by sending migrants to the sanctuaries.[4] In her view the problem would only worsen until the border was addressed and non-asylum seekers deported, and she noted that the same officials who invoke compassion would waste no time removing unwanted persons from their own property.[4]

## March 2025 House Oversight hearing

On Wednesday, March 5, 2025, the mayors of four so-called "sanctuary cities" testified before the Republican-led [[house-oversight-committee|House Oversight Committee]], where they defended their jurisdictions' immigration policies and their treatment of undocumented immigrants.[5] The witnesses were [[eric-adams|Eric Adams]] of New York, [[mike-johnston|Mike Johnston]] of Denver, [[brandon-johnson|Brandon Johnson]] of Chicago and [[michelle-wu|Michelle Wu]] of Boston, all Democrats.[5] CBS News described the "sanctuary city" label as covering a broad range of policies that limit local cooperation with federal immigration agents; the mayors argued the policies are crucial to ensuring undocumented immigrants report crimes and access city services without fear of deportation, while Republicans accused them of defying President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s immigration agenda and releasing dangerous criminals onto the streets.[5]

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the hearing "surreal," noting the four mayors shared identical talking points: that their jurisdictions are no longer "sanctuary cities" but "welcoming cities," that they follow all local, state and federal laws, and that violent criminals should be deported.[5] She argued this was mind-boggling given that the jurisdictions are in fact sanctuary cities, that the "sanctuary" is from federal immigration law enforcement, and that they release violent criminals into American communities daily — concluding that the mayors "lied — brazenly and under oath."[5] She also reported that Representative [[anna-paulina-luna|Anna Paulina Luna]] told Johnston, who had spent $2 million in taxpayer funds preparing for the hearing, that she was referring him to the Department of Justice for harboring violent criminal illegal aliens.[5]

## April 2025 executive order and sanctuary list

Ahead of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 100th day in office, his administration devoted Monday, 28 April 2025 to its immigration record.[6] White House press secretary [[karoline-leavitt|Karoline Leavitt]] and "border czar" [[tom-homan|Tom Homan]] held a morning news briefing in advance of Trump signing executive orders later that day, including one directing administration officials to publish a list of so-called "sanctuary cities."[6] During the briefing, Leavitt said such jurisdictions "obstruct the enforcement of federal immigration laws."[6] Washington Post–ABC News–Ipsos polling released around the same time found Trump's job approval sagging, with 39 percent of adult Americans approving of his handling of the job against 55 percent who disapproved.[6]

Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] linked the sanctuary-list order to the arrests of judges for obstruction of justice, writing that "from judges being arrested for obstruction of justice to sanctuary cities being cleansed of sanctuary status, it looks like we're at the beginning stages of the sort of mass psychological whiplash we've been projecting for years," and concluding that "total inversion is in the offing."[6]

## Minnesota litigation, September 2025

On 29 September 2025 the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] filed a [[doj-lawsuit-against-minnesota-sanctuary-policies|34-page lawsuit]] against [[minnesota|Minnesota]], [[minneapolis|Minneapolis]] and [[saint-paul|St. Paul]] over their sanctuary policies, asking a judge to invalidate sections of the state constitution, both cities' codes, and Hennepin County administrative orders.[7] Federal prosecutors argued that policies preventing local cooperation with Immigration and Customs Enforcement are unconstitutional, and Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] said Minnesota officials were jeopardizing the safety of their own citizens.[7]

## Los Angeles litigation (2025)

On June 30, 2025, the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] sued the City of Los Angeles and Mayor [[karen-bass|Karen Bass]] over the sanctuary ordinance the City Council passed in the weeks after [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 2024 election victory, which prevents city resources from going toward immigration enforcement.[8] The DOJ argued the city's laws "interfere with and discriminate against the Federal Government's enforcement of federal immigration law," and asserted that the city's refusal to cooperate had produced "lawlessness, rioting, looting, and vandalism" since June 6, 2025 — conditions severe enough to prompt deployment of the California National Guard and United States Marines.[8]

The ordinance, as described by the Los Angeles City Council in November 2024, "permanently enshrines sanctuary policies into municipal law" and prohibits the use of city property and personnel for immigration enforcement or cooperation with federal immigration agents.[8] Badlands contributor Ashe in America framed the suit as the fulfilment of [[tom-homan|Tom Homan]]'s December 2024 pledge to target sanctuary jurisdictions, and asked whether other sanctuary cities would learn from the Los Angeles example.[8]
