# Attack on Pearl Harbor

The Japanese attack on the US Pacific Fleet anchorage at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, on December 7, 1941, brought the United States into [[world-war-ii|World War II]]. In Badlands Media commentary the episode is treated less as a surprise assault than as a case study in engineered entry into war: writer [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argues that the US government was aware the attack was coming and chose not to warn the commanders at Pearl Harbor, effectively allowing 2,469 people to die in order to bring the United States into what he characterizes as Britain's war.[1]

## Casualty and cost framing

Carlson places the Pearl Harbor deaths inside a much larger accounting: a war that ultimately cost 418,000 American lives and upwards of 85 million lives worldwide.[1] He pairs this with his figure of roughly 17 million deaths in [[world-war-i|World War I]], presenting both wars as instruments of an elite project rather than as accidents of history.[1]

## The Roosevelt administration's response

According to Carlson, the Roosevelt-led government blamed the disaster on the incompetence of the military leaders stationed in Hawaii, and specifically on Admiral [[husband-kimmel|Husband Kimmel]], the fleet commander.[1] This allocation of blame is presented as a cover story that survived until a wartime naval inquiry quietly undercut it.[1]

## The 1944 Naval Court of Inquiry

In April 1944, Secretary of the Navy [[james-forrestal|James V. Forrestal]] ordered that a Naval Court of Inquiry be convened to investigate the facts surrounding the Japanese attack and to assess any culpability borne by members of the Navy.[1] The court convened on July 24, 1944, and held daily sessions in Washington, D.C., San Francisco, and Pearl Harbor, interviewing numerous witnesses before completing its work on October 19, 1944.[1]

Its report to the Navy Department largely exonerated Kimmel, finding that his decisions had been correct given the limited information available to him.[1] The court concluded that, "based upon the facts established, the Court is of the opinion that no offenses have been committed nor serious blame incurred on the part of any person or persons in the naval service."[1] The findings implicitly revealed that American cryptographers had broken the Japanese codes — a critical wartime secret — and for that reason the report was not made public until after the end of the war.[1]

Carlson draws the inference plainly: the codes were broken, and therefore the attack was foreseen.[1]

## Place in the false-flag argument

Pearl Harbor functions in Carlson's argument as the first of a pair of American precedents, the second being the [[september-11-attacks|September 11 attacks]], for the proposition that governments will accept or arrange mass casualties among their own citizens to unlock a desired war.[1] He extends the same reasoning to the October 2023 [[hamas|Hamas]] assault on [[israel|Israel]], asking why it should be inconceivable that a comparable foreknowledge scenario applies there.[1] The related pattern of [[false-flag-operation|false flag operations]] — including the [[sinking-of-the-lusitania|sinking of the Lusitania]] in the previous war — is treated as continuous across the century.[1]

## Forrestal's later death

Carlson notes that Forrestal, who ordered the inquiry and later served as Secretary of Defense, was forcibly admitted to Bethesda Naval Hospital for psychiatric treatment in 1949 and was reported to have jumped to his death from a sixteenth-floor window, a death Carlson suggests — as with that of [[george-s-patton|General Patton]] — may have been an assassination by his own government.[1]
