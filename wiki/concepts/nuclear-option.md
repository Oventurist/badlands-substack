# Nuclear Option (Senate Rules Change)

The "nuclear option" is the procedural device by which a simple majority of the [[united-states-senate|U.S. Senate]] changes the effect of its own rules — typically by having the presiding officer issue a procedural ruling that the majority then upholds — rather than by formal amendment requiring a supermajority.[1]

## Mechanics

Formal rule changes routed through the Rules Committee require 67 votes, but with 51 votes rules can effectively be changed by majority vote after a procedural ruling by the presiding officer.[1] Other routes exist: quick changes by unanimous consent, which any single objection blocks, and challenges to how existing rules are interpreted that set new precedents and thereby change practice without amendment.[1] Both of those are rare, leaving the nuclear option as the most common modern approach because of its simple-majority threshold.[1]

## Recent precedents

- 2013: Democrats under Harry Reid eliminated the [[filibuster|filibuster]] for judicial and other executive nominees, excluding the Supreme Court, setting the precedent.[1]
- 2017: Republicans under Mitch McConnell extended that precedent to Supreme Court nominees, allowing Neil Gorsuch's confirmation by simple majority.[1]
- 2019: McConnell cut post-cloture debate for district court nominees from 30 hours to two, speeding confirmations.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Writing amid the slow pace of [[donald-trump|Trump]] Cabinet confirmations in February 2025, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the 30-hour post-cloture debate rule amounts to fabricated obstruction, that the Senate imposes such rules on itself and that their value to the American people is "little more than ceremony."[1] She urged Majority Leader [[john-thune|John Thune]] to cut the 30 hours to two so senators could advise, consent and enter remarks in the record without wasting time and resources.[1] The commentary also noted that the nuclear option can backfire when the majority changes hands, and that a tight-margin Senate made the tactic more consequential.[1]

## September 2025 en bloc confirmations

The Senate's revised confirmation rules received their first major test on Thursday, 18 September 2025, when Senate Republicans confirmed 48 of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s nominees along party lines — many of them undersecretary or assistant secretary posts across federal agencies, along with ambassadorships.[2] Republicans had gone "nuclear" the previous week after a last-minute deal with Democrats collapsed, adopting a change that allows an unlimited number of nominees to be confirmed in batches, or en bloc, by simple majority.[2] The process nonetheless remained time-consuming, requiring procedural steps and 30 hours of debate.[2]

Senate Majority Whip [[john-barrasso|John Barrasso]] defended the maneuver on the floor, attributing the absence of voice-vote or unanimous-consent confirmations to "Democrat obstruction."[2] Badlands contributor Ashe in America mocked the terminology as sensational and self-important, observing that Trump had been in office exactly eight months and asking why the Senate had spent that time pretending it needed to negotiate with Democrats to confirm his appointees.[2]
