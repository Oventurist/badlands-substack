# Zionism

Zionism is described by [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] as "a secular political movement with religious foundations" that "would finally do what could not be done centuries earlier" — namely, establish Jewish sovereignty in the Holy Land.[1]

## In the Badlands framing

Deschamps places Zionism at the end of a long lineage of Holy Land restoration projects that begins with [[neoplatonism|Neoplatonism]], passes through the Crusades, [[sabbateanism|Sabbateanism]] and the Frankism of [[jacob-frank|Jacob Frank]], and touches the [[bavarian-illuminati|Bavarian Illuminati]].[1] He argues that the zeal for holy war did not end with Zionism's success: factions within the Christian world continue to fan the flames of crusade and conquest for Israel into modern times, with surface motivations changed and the rhetoric given new branding.[1]

On this reading, there is "seemingly a lot more going on with Israel than just war hawks like [[nikki-haley|Nikki Haley]] trying to gobble up some of the government graft," and the idea of the holy land and God's chosen people has long been used to realize "not so benevolent ends."[1]

## Discourse around the term

In commentary on the [[october-7-attacks|October 7 attacks]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described being called an antisemite for criticizing the Israeli government's security failure and, in the same weekend, being called a Zionist for refusing to endorse the destruction of the Jewish people — illustrating how the label functions as a rhetorical weapon on both sides of the debate.[1] Supreme Leader [[ali-khamenei|Ali Khamenei]] of [[iran|Iran]] used the term pejoratively in a statement posted on X declaring that "the cancer of the usurper Zionist regime will be eradicated at the hands of the Palestinian people and the Resistance forces throughout the region."[1]

## Contested meanings debate (December 2025)

Following the December 14, 2025 [[bondi-beach-attack|Bondi Beach attack]] targeting Jews at a Hanukkah celebration in Australia, the New South Wales government announced measures to further curb hate speech and symbols along with more controversial new protest powers, reopening national debate over the relationship between Jews, Israel, Zionism and anti-Zionism.[2]

Commentary in The Guardian argued that Zionism is a Jewish national movement that sought to create a Jewish state and then to secure and sustain it, but that the label is contested: for many Jews it signifies safety, continuity and belonging, while for Palestinians and many others it denotes dispossession and ongoing domination — a divergence that leads people to talk past one another with real-world consequences.[2] The piece traced the movement's origin to the late 19th century as a response both to a modern form of Jew-hatred grounded in pseudo-science and conspiracy theories and to the rise of European national independence movements, with early Zionists arguing that a Jewish state was the only solution to the precarious status of Jews as a persecuted European minority; in the nearly 130 years since, the term's meaning has continually evolved.[2]

Badlands commentator [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] responded to the quoted admission that, "short of having a Chinese-style system which you shut down and cut off access to the internet, it's not at all clear how you deal w/ access to [anti-Semitic] material," arguing that the implications are self-defeating: conceding that "refuting the claims" is not a plausible response, and that losing the ability to censor a view means it will spread everywhere, amounts to an admission that the view is "true enough to be compelling to virtually everyone."[2]

## Badlands critique of political Zionism (2025)

In commentary published alongside coverage of [[benjamin-netanyahu|Benjamin Netanyahu]]'s July 2025 visit to Washington, Badlands contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] characterised Zionism as an imperial movement seeking to re-establish the Babylonian Empire, predicated in his account on the Babylonian Talmud rather than the Torah, which he said states that the Nation of Israel cannot be re-established — nor the Third Temple rebuilt — until after the arrival of the Jewish messiah.[3]

He noted that Ultra-Orthodox authorities initially opposed 19th-century Zionism, which he attributed to the [[rothschild-family|Rothschild family]] acting through agents such as Moses Hess, on the grounds that it violated the three oaths sworn by the Jewish people: not to force their way into the homeland, not to hasten the end times, and not to rebel against other nations.[3] On this view Zionism constituted rebellion against divine will and a dangerous heresy, though the writer observed that Ultra-Orthodox leadership in Israel appears to have shifted over the intervening two centuries.[3]

The commentary described the movement as driven largely by political ambition to forge empire while also propelled by religious fervour in both Jewish and Evangelical communities, and judged its theological justifications flimsy in both Judaism and Christianity.[3] It further argued that Theodor Herzl, credited as the "Father of Zionism," did not adopt the cause until 1895, when it was already a powerful international coalition, and that he was distrusted by many factions as a secular opportunist who had assimilated with the Gentiles — tolerated because of his political talent and connections to networks in London and Germany, namely the Rothschilds.[3]
