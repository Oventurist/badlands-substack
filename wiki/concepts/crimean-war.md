# Crimean War

The Crimean War ran from October 1853 to February 1856, pitting Russia against an alliance of France, England and the Ottoman Empire.[1]

Matthew Ehret presents the war as a trap. Advisors dominating the government of Czar Nicholas I promoted the belief that Russia could take territory from the weakening Ottomans, pursuing what Ehret calls a foolish policy of expansionism without realizing they were falling into a British-set trap that would consume Russia in a three-year wasting war.[1] The outcome left Russia "humiliated, indebted and crippled morally and physically."[1]

The failure produced a new breed of Russian statecraft. [[alexander-ii|Alexander II]] took the reins and ended the war, and statesmen including [[grand-duke-constantine|Grand Duke Constantine]], [[nikolai-muraviev|General Nikolai Muraviev]], Foreign Minister [[alexander-gorchakov|Gorchakov]] and Ambassador [[eduard-de-stoeckl|Eduard de Stoeckl]] gained new influence.[1] Their doctrine combined an enhanced appreciation of the destructive role of British global strategy with recognition of America's importance as a collaborator and partner — the orientation that led to Russia's intervention in the [[american-civil-war|American Civil War]] and the [[alaska-purchase|sale of Alaska]].[1]
