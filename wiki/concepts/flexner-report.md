# Flexner Report

The **Flexner Report**, formally titled *Medical Education in the United States and Canada*, was a survey of North American medical schools submitted to the Carnegie Foundation and published in 1910. In the account given by Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]], the report was the decisive instrument by which a small group of industrial philanthropists reorganized American medicine around a single approved model of practice.[1]

## Origins

The project grew out of the [[american-medical-association|American Medical Association]]'s Council on Medical Education, created in 1904 under AMA head [[george-h-simmons|George H. Simmons]] with the stated mission of "upgrading medical education." The council devised a scheme to rank the nation's medical schools according to guidelines the article characterizes as dubious. By 1910 the AMA had exhausted its funds and could not complete the survey, at which point the Rockefeller and Carnegie foundations stepped in with an education fund to finish the work.[1]

[[simon-flexner|Simon Flexner]], a member of the board of directors of the Rockefeller Institute, proposed that his brother [[abraham-flexner|Abraham Flexner]] — who had no medical training — be hired to conduct the evaluation. Because the Flexners already worked for Rockefeller interests, the article treats the appointment as a foregone conclusion rather than an independent selection. The AMA's head of the Council on Medical Education travelled with Flexner as the schools were inspected.[1]

## Findings and mechanism

The report's central conclusions were that it was far too easy to found a medical school and that most existing schools were not teaching "sound medicine." Certification followed Flexner's recommendations alone, and schools that did not conform were denied both funding and prestige and were typically forced out of business. The practical result was that a physician who had not graduated from a Flexner-approved school and earned an M.D. degree could not find employment.[1]

## Consequences

DeLarme catalogues the collapse of competing healing traditions that followed. By 1925 more than 10,000 herbalists were out of business; by 1940 over 1,500 chiropractors had been prosecuted for practicing "quackery"; the 22 homeopathic medical schools flourishing in 1900 had fallen to two by 1923, and by 1950 every school teaching homeopathy had closed.[1]

The article argues that the report created the modern pharmaceutical industry, an industry whose products were largely developed from petroleum — the source of [[john-d-rockefeller|John D. Rockefeller]]'s fortune. It further contends that the resulting curriculum left M.D.s heavily biased toward synthetic drug therapy while teaching them "virtually nothing about nutrition, wellness, or disease prevention," so that doctors study disease rather than health.[1] A second motive attributed to the takeover was the advancement of [[eugenics]] and population control.[1]
