# Eco-Colonialism

Eco-colonialism is the term used in the Badlands Media corpus for a form of modern colonial control exercised through conservation policy, ecosystem management and the deliberate withholding of infrastructure development, rather than through direct territorial conquest.

## Origins in reservation policy

In Avoiding WWIII, [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] argues that the racist ghettoization of natives in tribal reservations has segregated First Nation tribes from the rest of society for generations, locking them into cycles of dependence, poverty, substance abuse, and rates of infant mortality and suicide magnitudes higher than the national average. He cites [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]]'s description of reservations as "like blocks of stone in the wall of a jail against the frontier line."

## Human ecosystems management

Since the late 1960s, the article states, it has become increasingly fashionable to treat native populations as extensions of their local ecosystems, both presumed by computer models to exist in stationary equilibrium; such models have been used for decades to calculate conservation regions and optimal population growth. Ehret contends that the vast growth of conservation parks and federal lands kept off-limits to infrastructure investment was not the work of "warm-hearted nature lovers" but a cold, calculated policy by geopolitical gamemasters intent on confining society to a controlled world of "limited resources." He presents this as the mechanism by which the large-scale economic growth policies of Franklin Roosevelt and [[john-f-kennedy|John F. Kennedy]] were derailed in the late 1960s with the onset of the Vietnam War.

## Sterilization, dependency and Africa

The article accuses liberal imperialists of shedding crocodile tears for abused natives while supporting the mass sterilization of native women throughout the 1970s and leaving reservations without clean drinking water, reliable electricity, healthcare or quality jobs. Money, it says, was often infused into the coffers of corrupt tribal leaders who allowed oil cartels to exploit their resources while their people remained in dependence with zero technological growth. Ehret draws a direct parallel between this arrangement and neo-colonial policy applied to Africa, contrasting it with what he describes as the development-based approach of China's [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]] against the debt programs of the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]], [[world-bank|World Bank]] and [[usaid|USAID]].
