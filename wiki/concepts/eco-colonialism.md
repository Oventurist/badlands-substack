# Eco-Colonialism

**Eco-colonialism** is the term [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] uses in "Avoiding WWIII" for a twentieth-century policy that treats native populations as extensions of their ecosystems in order to block large-scale continental development.[1]

## Reservations as instruments

Ehret argues that the racist program of ghettoizing natives on tribal reservations segregated First Nation tribes from the rest of society for generations, locking them into cycles of dependence, poverty, substance abuse, and rates of infant mortality and suicide magnitudes higher than the national average.[1] [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]] had already identified the reservations as "like blocks of stone in the wall of a jail against the frontier line."[1]

## Human ecosystems management

Since the late 1960s, Ehret writes, it has become increasingly fashionable to treat native populations as extensions of their local ecosystems, both presumed to exist in stationary equilibrium according to computer models used to calculate conservation regions and optimal population growth.[1] He argues that anyone struggling to understand why the large-scale economic growth policies of Franklin Roosevelt and [[john-f-kennedy|JFK]] were derailed in the late 1960s with the onset of the Vietnam War must understand this use of native reserves and ecosystem management.[1] The vast growth of conservation parks and federal lands kept off limits to infrastructure investment was not the work of warm-hearted nature lovers but a cold, calculated policy by geopolitical gamemasters intent on keeping society locked into a small, controlled world of "limited resources."[1]

## Abuses

While liberal imperialists shed "crocodile tears" for natives abused by colonizers, Ehret writes, they supported the mass sterilization of native women throughout the 1970s and left native communities without clean drinking water, reliable electricity, healthcare or access to quality jobs.[1] Under this modern colonialism, money was often infused into the coffers of corrupt tribal leaders who let oil cartels exploit their resources while keeping their people in dependence and zero technological growth.[1] Ehret sees a clear parallel in the neo-colonial policy applied to Africa.[1]
