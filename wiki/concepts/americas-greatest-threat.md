# America's Greatest Threat

**America's Greatest Threat**, subtitled "It Doesn't Come from a Foreign Government …", is an opinion essay by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] published by Badlands Media on April 24, 2024. The piece argues that the principal danger to the United States has always been its own government rather than any foreign adversary, and that the constitutional order was designed specifically to guard against domestic usurpation. It attracted 182 likes on the Badlands Media Substack and carries the standard disclaimer that Badlands Media articles represent the opinions of contributing authors.

## The founders' fear of domestic power

The essay opens by asserting that the patriots who founded the United States understood that the biggest threat to America "didn't come from overseas," not from invading armies, naval cannon, or pirates, but from America itself — specifically from the American government. On this reading the [[united-states-constitution|Constitution]] does not protect Americans from foreign adversaries directly; it protects them from an American government "that is infiltrated by said enemies." Carlson suggests the founders anticipated that foreigners would influence the American political system by infiltrating it from within, and that because "two great oceans protect the United States," the more urgent safeguard had to be internal.

Two cinematic analogies frame the argument. Stanley Kubrick's *The Shining* is invoked as an image of a family isolated by impassable snow-covered roads whose real threat comes from within the family, and the 1972 Burt Reynolds film *Deliverance* is used, less charitably, to characterise modern Americans who believe that access to unlimited information makes them better informed than earlier generations. The article contends that discernment and self-awareness have reached unprecedented lows while "a sense of self-importance and entitlement has never been higher."

## Stockholm Syndrome and the compliant citizenry

A central motif of the essay is that Americans collectively suffer from [[stockholm-syndrome|Stockholm Syndrome]] — empathising with and defending those who hold them captive. Carlson illustrates this with the extended image of an abused wife who fights the police attempting to arrest her husband, throwing punches with her unbroken arm, then makes excuses for the abuse and scrapes together bail money. Americans, the article argues, vote for people who want more control over them and treat that control as necessary and good, while dissenters are "ostracized and ridiculed, fined, jailed, killed or censored — removed from society, from all positions of power and influence."

As evidence of the impossibility of holding such controllers accountable, the essay states that the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] "has never been audited … not once," despite existing for more than a century.

## 1913, the Sixteenth Amendment, and the income tax

The article dates the decisive break to 1913. It states that the Constitution was amended and ratified on February 3, 1913 — "very close to the time the Federal Reserve was formed," which Carlson insists "is not a coincidence." The [[sixteenth-amendment|Sixteenth Amendment]] granted [[congress|Congress]] the authority to levy an income tax without apportionment by population, which the essay glosses as meaning "the people wouldn't be allowed to vote on it."

The reasoning offered is that American politicians understood their constituents would never approve either a [[federal-income-tax|federal income tax]] or a Federal Reserve System "run by the corrupt bankers themselves," and therefore simply removed the question from popular control. Politicians who voted these measures in may have forfeited re-election, the article allows, but "they were paid handsomely for their treasonous actions, and America would never be the same again."

Carlson maintains that the founders "specifically stated in the Constitution that a federal income tax was unconstitutional," and that the absence of such a tax kept government small, as intended. An income tax, by contrast, allowed government to grow and permitted taxpayers' money to be "siphoned off to the government officials and the people who control them."

## Career politicians

Related to this is the essay's claim that American government positions were never meant to be full-time jobs, and that the concept of career politicians was "appalling to America's founding fathers." The federal government, in this account, was supposed to resemble local government today: a handful of full-time officials, with most serving part-time before returning to work as farmers, lawyers, or small business owners. Carlson's satirical inversion is that federal politicians "still work part time for the government," spending the remainder of their time "lobbying and cutting deals with whoever will pay them the most."

## Debt slavery arithmetic

The article devotes an extended passage to the cumulative burden of taxation. It notes that income is taxed federally and, in many states, again by the state; that goods and services generate sales tax; and that property, investments, and savings are all taxed, so that "a dollar is taxed when you earn it, when you spend it and when you save it."

The largest levy, in Carlson's view, is [[inflation|inflation]] — "the tax that comes from the Federal Reserve printing money out of nothing, backed by nothing, diluting down the purchasing power of existing money." Anticipating the objection that a government able to print money need not tax at all, he answers that it does not need to: "Any time the government needs money, they can print it and pass on the inflation to the American people. The truth is taxes are a key component of creating and maintaining debt slaves."

An explicitly rough arithmetic follows. Assuming real inflation since Joe Biden took office has been far higher than reported — perhaps 30% cumulative, or roughly 10% a year — Carlson subtracts from a dollar earned: about 30% for income and payroll taxes, 10% inflation, 10% property tax, and 10% sales tax, leaving forty cents; a further 5% for "miscellaneous taxes" such as car and gas taxes leaves thirty-five cents; saving 10% for retirement leaves twenty-five cents to cover food, shelter, health insurance, car insurance, clothes, gasoline, electricity, and internet. He concedes the numbers are not run "perfectly" but insists the point stands: "Americans are debt slaves," and this [[debt-slavery|debt servitude]] "keeps the population in line; it keeps them from pushing back."

Carlson applies the argument to himself, writing that although he believes "taxes are theft," he files and pays before the deadline rather than risk jail and leave his family destitute. He can refuse the vaccine, disbelieve the media, and speak against the system, "but at the end of the day, I'm afraid to leave the plantation." The rhetorical question that follows — "If not for the threat of the [[internal-revenue-service|IRS]], who would continue to stay in line?" — is paired with the observation that "if not for the funding of the system, there would be no system."

## COVID-19 as demonstration

The essay's most concrete indictment concerns the [[covid-19-pandemic|COVID-19 pandemic]]. Carlson asks who forced Americans to stay home from work and school in 2020, who compelled them to take "an experimental drug or risk losing their jobs," who barred the unvaccinated from public life, and who censored dissent on social media. His answer is a series of negations: "It wasn't Putin. It wasn't Xi. It wasn't Iran. China didn't lock Americans down. Putin didn't force them to get jabbed. ISIS wasn't checking vaccine cards at the local restaurants."

Worse, in his account, the government "brainwashed Americans into doing their dirty work for them": business owners fired loyal and productive employees over vaccination status, some believing it was the right thing to do; Americans administered the injections; and many insisted the unvaccinated should be excluded from society. "Americans decided that other Americans didn't deserve their Constitutional Rights."

## The deception thesis

Adapting the saying that "the greatest lie the Devil ever told was that he didn't exist," Carlson argues that the American government "has Satan beat hands down," having convinced its citizens to surrender the majority of their income for protection from foreign enemies when the government is itself their greatest enemy — and when, he adds, "many of these foreign enemies have been created by the American Government and its agencies." The same persuasion, he writes, has convinced Americans that the Constitution should be abolished, that free speech is bad, and that the right to bear arms makes them less safe. Americans fear the government and therefore abide by "their unconstitutionality," while simultaneously defending "their debt slave masters."

## Prescription and Trump

The closing section sets out conditions for change: nothing will change until enough Americans see through "the fog of lies," until the political system undergoes a complete overhaul, until criminals are removed from power and failsafes installed, until the Constitution is upheld and holds authority over the government, and until government is reduced in size and works for the people. Carlson notes that one can change the majority in Congress and change the Speaker of the House, but that nothing will change "until the members of Congress actually work for the people and not the lobbyists and our foreign enemies."

He nonetheless expresses faith that the system can be repaired. Quoting the Joker's line from the 1989 *Batman* — "this town needs an enema" — he names [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] as the man who can administer it, "to drain the swamp, to exterminate the rats that live in the sewer system and the cockroaches that live between the walls," while warning that unless Trump "removes all the cancer, no permanent change will occur." Writing before the 2024 election, Carlson frames Trump taking office in 2025 as the point at which "the real work begins" and "the hoarder's trash will begin to be thrown out" — explicitly "not the finish line," because the hoarder must be cured of the underlying disorder "otherwise the house will just be filled up again."
