# One-Child Policy

The one-child policy was China's population-control programme, dated in this account to 1979 and characterized as disastrous for the country's long-term demographics.[1]

The policy is presented not as an autonomous Chinese initiative but as a condition imposed from outside: China was permitted to acquire the western technology needed to climb out of abject poverty only on the condition that it obeyed Rockefeller–World Bank demands that one-child programmes be adopted to curb population growth.[1] This framing situates the policy inside the wider Malthusian turn in postwar policy associated with [[henry-kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] and the [[nssm-200|NSSM-200]] report, which conditioned food and development assistance on recipient states' population-control measures.[1]

China's population is described as not yet healed from the policy and far from the 2.1 children per couple required for replacement fertility.[1] The limit was raised from one child to two in 2015, and economists at the Bank of China have called for the immediate elimination of all birth limits.[1] It is also noted that minority groups such as the Uyghurs and Tibetans were never subject to the one-child policy.[1]

Alongside the [[belt-and-road-initiative|Belt and Road Initiative]] and long-term energy and technology investment, this pro-natal reversal is cited by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] as evidence that China is now a leading force for population growth — and therefore an unlikely author of a depopulation-aligned pandemic.[1]
