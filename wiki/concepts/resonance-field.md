# Resonance Field

The **resonance field** is the organising idea of "Hijacked Consciousness - Resonance Field," the fifth installment of the [[hijacked-consciousness-series|Hijacked Consciousness]] series, published by [[observing-consciousness|Observing Consciousness]] through [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] on August 9, 2025 and subtitled "How Education Was Weaponized to Sever the Soul, Fracture Memory, and Re-write Reality."[1] Where earlier installments traced what the author calls the fracture "from sacred spiral to mechanized silence" — broken rhythm, flattened language, exiled myth, and a curriculum that shifted "from tuning to taming, from initiation to indoctrination" — this essay turns to what the author describes as the return of the signal.[1]

## Definition

In the article's framing, resonance is not merely a matching of tone but "a vibrational coherence" that reactivates memory and tells the nervous system that the reader is neither crazy nor broken but "tuned to something real."[1] The author distinguishes this explicitly from trauma bonding, calling it instead "memory reactivation," on the grounds that one keeps a person trapped while the other sets them free.[1] The biologist [[rupert-sheldrake|Rupert Sheldrake]] is quoted for the claim that "Memory is inherent in nature. Everything has a kind of memory," and the author identifies the mechanism at work as [[morphic-field|the morphic field]], which is said not to ask for belief but for listening.[1] The field is said not to spread in straight lines but to spiral and echo, remembering in patterns, and to deepen through recognition and communion rather than through argument or belief.[1]

## The hum

The author repeatedly uses the word "hum" to name the felt sense of energy in the body, describing it as the closest available description of a bodily experience of coherence.[1] The hum is presented as something that survived the conditioning described in earlier parts of the series: examples given include a child who flinches at bells, an adult who avoids eye contact but weeps at music, and an elder who remembers the forest better than the curriculum — treated not as flukes but as "fragments of coherence that survived the spell."[1] A room in which a dozen people exhale at once, or a crack in the tone of a voice that opens a silence deeper than words, are offered as everyday evidence that the field is operating.[1]

## Collective remembering

A central claim is that private memory becomes collective when it is spoken. The author recounts speaking in middle school about the ache of the classroom and the weight of fluorescent buzz, and being met afterward with quiet private acknowledgements — "That was my life," "I thought I was the only one," "I didn't have the words, but I always knew something was off" — and notes similar statements appearing in the comment sections of the series itself.[1] The conclusion drawn is that isolation was manufactured: "we were not alone - they just wanted us to think we were."[1] The author summarises the principle in a phrase repeated to his granddaughter: "You feel it, I feel it, we all feel it.. to some degree… so it has to be real!"[1]

## Integration and psychedelic experience

The essay describes an integration circle held after an ayahuasca ceremony in the desert near Tucson, Arizona, in which participants who had "seen through the veil" spoke of visions, forgotten memories, and light and shadow moving through their bones.[1] What struck the author most was not the content of the visions but the shame that followed — the external "shoulds" that returned as the medicine faded, which he characterises as "the old static of conditioning trying to retune them back into a dissonant world."[1] He told participants that integration was not about assigning meaning but about "preserving frequency," and that if they felt it in the body, it was real.[1] One woman described her childhood pain lifting from her chest like smoke before later doubting she had invented it; a man reconnected with his inner child and felt ridiculous the following morning.[1] The author's counsel was to follow resonance, allow life to reorganise around what felt coherent rather than what merely made sense, and expect resistance because "systems fear what they can't measure."[1]

## Tuning back in

The return of the signal is described as arriving through sensation rather than belief: breath deepening, shoulders dropping, jaw releasing, heart softening, though never all at once.[1] Rhythm, when it returns, is said to arrive "as presence" rather than as productivity, replacing the clock with "the current" — a movement described as non-linear, spiralling and contracting like breath, nature, or the quantum field.[1] The author invokes [[max-planck|Max Planck]]'s statement that consciousness is fundamental and matter derivative from it to argue that the material world is downstream of awareness, and therefore that "whoever controls the narrative, controls the field" — the reason, in this account, that perception had to be hijacked first.[1] [[bessel-van-der-kolk|Bessel van der Kolk]] is cited for the proposition that trauma is a current imprint living inside people rather than a story about the past, an imprint the author says cannot be argued away but can be "re-patterned" through rhythm, presence, and a field safe enough that the body no longer braces.[1]

## Becoming the resonance

The final movement of the essay argues that coherence spreads by contact rather than conversion, and that those who hold coherence amid static become "tuning forks" for others.[1] [[amit-goswami|Amit Goswami]]'s line that "consciousness is the ground of all being" is used to argue that every moment of coherence is not only healing but creation, while [[walter-russell|Walter Russell]]'s "Mediocrity is self-inflicted. Genius is self-bestowed" is reinterpreted so that genius means coherence rather than intellect.[1] The author describes a "new curriculum" encoded in presence — in how people speak, slow down, and hold the ache of others without trying to fix it.[1]

## Resonance as threat

The closing section, "The Hum Beneath the System," argues that the conflict was "a war on rhythm" before it was a war on thought, and that the system replaced "song with slogan, stillness with stimulation, and truth with noise."[1] Because rhythm carries memory, and embodied memory is described as dangerous to those who profit from forgetting, the author contends that environments were engineered to confuse the body under the labels of progress, efficiency, and safety.[1] Ordinary instances of the counter-effect are offered: a man humming in an elevator quieting a floor full of tension, a girl doodling spirals making a teacher pause, a store clerk whispering a prayer.[1] [[joseph-chilton-pearce|Joseph Chilton Pearce]]'s remark that creative life requires losing the fear of being wrong is glossed as a definition of resonance — "the courage to feel truth, even when it contradicts the curriculum."[1] The article concludes that resonance "can't be legislated," that in a world engineered for noise "silence becomes sacred defiance," and that the next phase is not to fight the system but to "out-resonate it," before directing readers onward to Part 6 of the series.[1]
