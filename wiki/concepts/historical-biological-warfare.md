# Historical Biological Warfare

Historical biological warfare refers to the documented and alleged use of disease agents as weapons across recorded history, a body of precedent invoked by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] commentary as evidence that the deliberate manufacture and release of pathogens is neither novel nor implausible.[1]

## Cited precedents

Writing in response to the discovery of the [[reedley-illegal-biolab|Reedley illegal biolab]] in California, [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] listed five historical examples to establish that biowarfare "is nothing new."[1]

During the Mongol siege of the Crimean city of Caffa (1346–1347), present-day Feodosia in Ukraine, the besieging forces were struck by a plague outbreak and reportedly catapulted the bodies of plague victims into the city; the tactic is believed to have contributed to the spread of the Black Death that eventually reached Europe.[1]

In 1763, during the French and Indian War in North America, British forces are reported to have used smallpox-infected blankets against Native American tribes allied with the French, an early instance of intentional germ warfare.[1] In the same conflict, during Pontiac's War (1763–1766), a smallpox outbreak occurred among tribes besieging Fort Pitt — present-day Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania — after British military personnel reportedly gave the besiegers blankets taken from a smallpox hospital with the intent of spreading the disease.[1]

During the First Crusade, at the Siege of Antioch (1097–1098) in what is now Antakya, Turkey, the Crusaders are alleged to have catapulted plague-infected corpses into the city; the resulting outbreak proved disastrous for both sides.[1] Ancient records from the Hittite Empire, dated to roughly 1320 BCE, describe a plague attack conducted against the city of Hatti in which the Hittites used infected rams to spread disease among their enemies.[1]

## Argument advanced

Deschamps held that these examples "prove the concept," and asked readers to weigh them against the pervasiveness of contemporary social manipulation.[1] He suggested it is not implausible that cells exist in which pathogens are manufactured and quietly dispersed into the general population so that medicines, vaccines and policy changes can take effect, or so that populations can be kept fearful of heavily publicised epidemics.[1] On that reading, he argued, the past two hundred years of epidemiological history could be rendered highly suspect, raising the question of which epidemics were real and which were not.[1]
