# Piltdown Man

Piltdown Man was the name given to a skull and jawbone unearthed in Piltdown, England in 1912, and hailed for decades as the long-awaited "missing link" between ape and man whose absence had frustrated gradualist Darwinians.[1]

## Discovery

According to Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]], the young Jesuit [[pierre-teilhard-de-chardin|Pierre Teilhard de Chardin]] — fresh from three years teaching paleontology in Cairo (1905–1908) — was called back for a holiday in Piltdown, where during a short walk he discovered a skull and bone fragments in a field.[1] The international press trumpeted the find as the great proof that Darwin was right, and a team of British archaeologists was deployed by the Royal Academy to finish the excavation work.[1] Chardin quickly became a celebrity.[1]

## Doubts and exposure

Rumblings of doubt persisted within the scientific community, especially among dentists, who asked why no teeth were found with the specimen and why the jawbone so closely resembled that of an orangutan.[1] Despite the excavation site being remarkably unprotected — with bystanders freely poking around for weeks — Chardin returned on holiday once more and conveniently stumbled upon a tooth that no one else had seen, a second discovery that further boosted his fame.[1]

It was not until 1953 that scientists studying the remains conclusively proved that Piltdown Man consisted of a monkey jaw, dyed and shaved, joined to a human skull; the tooth Chardin had found belonged to a dog, painted and shaved carefully to fit the jaw.[1] Ehret treats the affair as the template for what he calls "[[peking-man|Piltdown Hoax 2.0]]" — the later Peking Man discovery in China.[1]
