# FedAccounts

**FedAccounts** is the name given to a policy proposal under which the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] would offer bank accounts directly to individuals and businesses, rather than only to banks. In [[a-seismic-shift|A Seismic Shift]], [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] presents the proposal as one of the central mechanisms by which a public banking option would be established across the United States.

## Origin

According to the Berkeley Economic Review as quoted in the article, the proposal was posed in 2018 by three researchers, two of whom worked in the [[treasury-department|Treasury Department]]. Under their proposal, the Federal Reserve would offer all individuals and businesses in the United States the option of opening an account with the Fed itself, providing an alternative to private banks and credit unions, with potentially significant effects across monetary and economic policy. Lange notes that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] was president in 2018 and questions whether the timing is coincidental.

## Design and claimed advantages

The Berkeley Economic Review passage argues that FedAccounts would lessen, if not eliminate, the need for widespread deposit insurance, since deposits at a Federal Reserve Bank cannot be defaulted on. A private bank's debit card carries the liability of that bank and is backed by its ability to meet obligations to all depositors; a debit card issued by the Federal Reserve would instead hold **"pure sovereign money"** — the liability of the Federal Reserve itself, which as the issuer of sovereign money can always meet withdrawals.

Work by [[morgan-ricks|Morgan Ricks]], summarised by JustMoney.org, describes FedAccounts as offering all the functionality of ordinary transaction accounts — debit cards, ATM access, direct deposit, online bill payments, and online and mobile access — without fees or minimum-balance requirements. Ricks and co-authors suggest the Fed could partner with the [[united-states-postal-service|U.S. Postal Service]] to serve as a ubiquitous physical branch network servicing the accounts, merging FedAccounts with [[postal-savings-system|postal banking]] proposals to create a public system for money and payments that would function as "fully public infrastructure akin to roads, sidewalks, public libraries and the judicial system."

[[mark-dimondstein|Mark Dimondstein]], president of the [[american-postal-workers-union|American Postal Workers Union]], made a similar argument in The Hill after the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank, writing that the Federal Reserve currently serves as a bank for bankers and that the same services could be extended to individuals and small businesses, "creating a 'FedAccount' for everyone."

## The author's reading

Lange instructs readers to re-read the Berkeley description substituting "Treasury" for "Federal Reserve," asserting that the Federal Reserve is unconstitutional and that the Treasury is properly in charge of the people's money. He connects the proposal to a return to a redeemable [[gold-standard|gold standard]], arguing that accounts holding sovereign money rather than fiat Federal Reserve Notes would remove the need for [[federal-deposit-insurance-corporation|federal deposit insurance]] and therefore the need for taxpayer-funded bailouts of failed private banks.

## Opposition

The [[american-bankers-association|American Bankers Association]] has lobbied [[congress|Congress]] against the proposal, which it says would require banks with more than $10 billion in assets to offer basic "pass through" accounts whose deposits rest at the Federal Reserve rather than on the bank's balance sheet. The ABA calls the plan impractical and "highly disruptive," warns that FedAccounts would draw deposits from banks of all sizes to the Fed's balance sheet, and states that banks would be "unable to compete with a government agency offering deposit accounts with an unlimited balance sheet." Lange reads these objections as confirmation of the proposal's power, noting that it would abolish the $250,000 insured-deposit ceiling and end what he describes as the private banking industry's control over the federal government.
