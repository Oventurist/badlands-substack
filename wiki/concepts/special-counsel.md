# Special Counsel

A **Special Counsel** is an attorney appointed to investigate and potentially prosecute cases in which there is a conflict of interest or other special circumstances within the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]].[1] The role is established under the regulations codified at 28 CFR § 600.1 et seq., which allow the Attorney General to appoint a Special Counsel when the Attorney General determines that a criminal investigation or prosecution is warranted but that a normal Justice Department investigation would present a conflict of interest or other extraordinary circumstances.[1]

## Appointment and Senate confirmation

A recurring dispute concerns whether a Special Counsel must be confirmed by the [[united-states-senate|Senate]].[1] One view holds that no confirmation is required: the appointment is made directly by the Attorney General, who has authority to select an individual with a reputation for integrity and impartial decision-making to serve in the capacity.[1] The opposing view points to the case of [[jack-smith|Special Counsel Jack Smith]] and the ruling by Judge [[aileen-cannon|Eileen Cannon]], which dismissed the "classified documents" prosecution of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] on the grounds that Smith, as special counsel, had never been confirmed by the Senate.[1]

## Principal versus inferior officers

The question turns on the distinction between principal and inferior officers under the Appointments Clause.[1] **Principal officers** must be nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate; the category includes ambassadors, justices of the Supreme Court, and other significant executive officers, who typically wield broad authority and operate with a degree of independence from oversight within their domains.[1] If a Special Counsel were treated as a principal officer, the appointment would require Senate confirmation — which has not been the established practice — and the Special Counsel would enjoy significant autonomy and could not easily be removed without Senate involvement.[1]

Congress may instead vest the appointment of **inferior officers** in the President alone, in the courts of law, or in the heads of departments.[1] Treating Special Counsels as inferior officers means the Attorney General may appoint them without Senate confirmation.[1] Inferior officers are generally subject to supervision by principal officers; in the Special Counsel context, they are appointed by and removable by the Attorney General, typically for "good cause," which supplies a mechanism of control, and their powers, duties, and tenure are limited and defined by the Attorney General or by the governing regulations.[1] Cases such as [[morrison-v-olson|Morrison v. Olson]] (1988) classified the independent counsel — a predecessor to today's Special Counsel — as an inferior officer, citing limited jurisdiction, performance of specific duties, and the temporary nature of the office as hallmarks of inferior-officer status.[1]

## 2024 Gaetz speculation

Following the collapse of his nomination for Attorney General, social media speculation held that [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]] was all but certain to be named Special Counsel.[1] Ryan DeLarme, writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], assessed that [[pam-bondi|Pamela Bondi]], if confirmed as Attorney General, could at least attempt to appoint Gaetz as a Special Counsel under the inferior-officer designation, though the actual decision would weigh heavily on the political and ethical landscape at the time.[1] He further cautioned that the procedural latitude extended to Jack Smith would likely not be extended to Gaetz, as entrenched interests in the House and Senate would attempt to bend the rules in their own favor.[1]
