# Secret War in Laos

The "Secret War" was the covert campaign waged in Laos by the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] during the era of the [[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]]. Badlands Media presents it as the pivotal precedent in the agency's transformation into what the article calls the world's largest drug trafficker.[1]

## Why it was secret

Laos was supposed to be protected from war by the Geneva Peace Agreement, which the United States had signed and which barred military intervention there.[1] Because open American involvement would have violated that agreement, the campaign was conducted covertly — hence the name "secret war."[1]

## Opium and the Golden Triangle

Badlands writer [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argued that the true object of the war was control of the opium supply rather than the containment of communism.[1] After France lost the Indochina war, the [[french-connection|French Connection]] network lost control of the opium supply in the [[golden-triangle|Golden Triangle]], and the CIA required the U.S. military to step in and protect it.[1] In this framing the [[gulf-of-tonkin-incident|Gulf of Tonkin]] incident was itself a CIA operation designed to trigger the wider war, and money — not ideology — was "the keystone."[1]

As evidence of direct agency participation in the trade, the article cites the case of [[edgar-buell|Edgar "Pop" Buell]], an Indiana farmer recruited through International Voluntary Services who was assigned to the Plain of Jars, where the CIA was building its secret Meo army, and who used his agricultural skills to improve Meo opium cultivation.[1]

## Consequences

Badlands links the Laos campaign to the broader growth of a [[shadow-government|shadow government]], arguing that the agency became willing to fight undeclared covert wars to protect drug revenue, and that drug money went on to fund terrorist groups and proxy wars from Vietnam onward.[1] The wider Vietnam conflict, meanwhile, is described as having put the United States into massive debt, enlarged the military industrial complex and forced the country off the gold standard, while the Laos operation attracted almost no attention at all.[1]
