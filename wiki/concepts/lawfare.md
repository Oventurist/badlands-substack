# Lawfare

Lawfare is the use of litigation as a political weapon — filing or sustaining legal actions less to obtain a lawful remedy than to stigmatize, financially exhaust, or silence an opponent. Badlands Media commentary describes [[naacp-v-useip|*NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.*]] as an unprecedented attack on the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]] by "the leftist lawfare machine," conducted under the guise of fighting voter suppression.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

The case illustrates several hallmarks of the practice. Three civil-rights organizations, two law firms, and at least six attorneys pursued claims for which no evidence of intimidation ever materialized — not in discovery, not across two and a half years of motions, replies and responses, and not at trial — which defendants argue makes the claims frivolous by definition.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The legal theory was drawn from [[voters-strike-back|a 2015 law-review article]] that openly recommended [[ku-klux-klan-act-of-1871|KKK Act]] claims as a branding device to attach "deeply embarrassing associations" to defendants and drive settlement, and defined success as "stopping the defendant's conduct."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) The plaintiffs' sole intimidation witness admitted she named [[useip|USEIP]] volunteers only at counsel's suggestion, after which the claim was carried in an affidavit sworn under penalty of perjury.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

Defendants report reputational, professional, family, and psychological harm that an award of costs could not offset, but conclude that complete vindication in federal court affirmed their faith in justice — and that justice requires showing up and fighting for it.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

## SPLC probe as counter-lawfare (2026)

Commenting on Florida's civil investigation into the Southern Poverty Law Center, Ashe in America described the leftist lawfare playbook as forwarding claims of racism to generate headlines and funding, and argued that the subpoena appeared to be investigating that playbook directly.[2] She raised the prospect of the SPLC matter converging with the ActBlue and WinRed fundraising investigations.[2]

## Michigan salon case and the Jack Phillips precedent (2023)

Commenting on Michigan's civil-rights charge against salon owner Christine Geiger, Ashe in America argued that the point of such actions was not client safety but ensuring that a proprietor is "not allowed to not play the pronoun game."[3] She pointed to Colorado baker Jack Phillips, who had by then spent more than eleven years in court over refusing to bake a same-sex wedding cake, won at the Supreme Court, and then faced a fresh case over a gender-transition cake, as the instructive test kitchen for the practice.[3] She described lawfare as having two aims — punishing opponents, and chiseling away at the contours of the law so that each case becomes a building block toward a new precedent — with the process itself serving as the punishment while the regime fundraises and saturates the narrative and the defendant struggles to find counsel.[3] She called for the practice to be abolished and for harsher penalties for weaponizing the law out of frivolity, warning that otherwise it becomes normal.[3]

Burning Bright, writing on the X Corp suit against Media Matters and the parallel Texas attorney general investigation, framed the same period as a "lawsuit cascade" in the information war and as the beginning of a template-setting campaign against those who had assaulted constitutional free-speech rights, fought simultaneously in court and in the court of public opinion ahead of the 2024 election.[3]

## Canadian lawfare after the Freedom Convoy (2024)

Badlands writer [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] argued in February 2024 that "the front lines in the war for liberty in Canada seems to be in the domain of lawfare," describing a period of major movement in cases pitting government power against citizens.[4] The central development was Justice [[richard-mosley|Richard Mosley]]'s ruling that [[justin-trudeau|Justin Trudeau]]'s invocation of the [[emergencies-act|Emergencies Act]] against the [[freedom-convoy|Freedom Convoy]] was unlawful, which Esler said opened litigation pathways many had thought foreclosed after [[paul-rouleau|Paul Rouleau]]'s commission validated the invocation.[4]

Esler catalogued the surrounding cases: the release of two of the [[coutts-four|Coutts 4]] and the withdrawal of charges that they plotted to kill an RCMP officer; the ruling that [[deena-hinshaw|Deena Hinshaw]]'s Alberta COVID measures were illegal, opening the way to what looked to be the province's largest class action; the firing of podcaster BertaProudDad for hosting a conservative show; and the more than $100,000 in fines levied on nurse [[kristen-nagle|Kristen Nagle]] after four years of litigation.[4]

From these he drew a general pattern: rulings against the people are quickly exposed as corrupt government operations, while rulings in favour of citizens reveal which legal institutions and individuals remain uncompromised.[4] He concluded that "the bad guys have to cheat when they win any of these cases," that they publicly melt down when the public logs a win, and that both wins and losses were redpilling ordinary Canadians about how politically compromised the legal system had become.[4]

## The SCIF episode as lawfare

Badlands treated the August 2023 fight over [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s request for a [[sensitive-compartmented-information-facility|SCIF]] as characteristic of the lawfare dynamic, in which legal maneuvers double as narrative traps.[5] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that special counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] "may be wise to Trump's SCIF bait, but as is usually the way with Trump, once you've recognized one Narrative or legal trap, you've already fallen headfirst into three more."[5]


## Deschamps definition

Commenting in August 2023 on the secret subpoena of Twitter in the [[trump-twitter-search-warrant|Trump Twitter search warrant]] episode, Badlands contributor [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] offered a working definition of lawfare as "the use of the legal process to retard, hinder, or otherwise prevent justice, in principle; or in a practical sense, to starve out and waste the resources of your opponent so you can win outside of merit."[6] He argued that an outcome produced by manipulation and coercion cannot be fair or reflect truth and justice, because cheating was required to obtain it.[6] Applying the frame to the prosecutions of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], he contended that with every public action the globalists take they "might legally win but then lawfully lose" — that even a successful effort to rig Trump out of the 2024 race would be widely seen as bogus and would consume what little social capital the [[cabal|Cabal]] retained.[6]
