# Euromaidan

Euromaidan refers to the turbulent events in Kiev in early 2014, when initially peaceful protests spiralled into riots and clashes with police and culminated in the ouster of democratically elected President [[viktor-yanukovych|Viktor Yanukovych]].[1] Russian officials and much of the Badlands commentariat describe the episode as a Western-backed coup rather than a popular revolution.[1]

## Composition and aftermath

The anti-Yanukovych forces included ultranationalist groups such as Right Sector and Svoboda.[1] The change of government sparked counterprotests and further rioting, including a deadly clash in Odessa in May 2014 in which 48 people were killed.[1] Following Yanukovych's removal, Oleksandr Turchynov served as interim president pending new elections.[1]

## Putin's framing

Speaking to a group of businessmen in the Kremlin in May 2025, President [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] argued that Russia was left with no choice but to launch its military operation in Ukraine because of the continued persecution of the people of Donbass by Kiev following the 2014 Western-backed Euromaidan coup.[1] "You need to understand that we weren't the ones who orchestrated the coup in Ukraine," Putin said, adding that the West "has always told us that there should be democracy and elections… but they carried out a coup – a bloody one, in fact – as if it were normal. They later went on to suppress the Donbass, killing people with helicopters and jets."[1] He concluded that the West "practically forced us into doing what we're doing today, and now they're trying to blame us for it."[1]

## Badlands take

Contributor [[ghostofbasedpatrickhenry|GhostofBasedPatrickHenry]] endorsed Putin's account, writing that the Western Hegemony started the war by overthrowing the Ukrainian government in 2014, and linking the Ukrainian and Israeli theatres of conflict with Iran as the interlinking node between them.[1]

## Sequel to the Orange Revolution

Badlands Media's "How to Rent a Riot" series treats the 2013 Maidan uprising as the sequel to the [[orange-revolution|Orange Revolution]] of 2004–05, describing both as U.S.-sponsored regime-change efforts that used the power of [[nonviolent-action|nonviolent action]] to undermine the democratic process, and as precursors to the ongoing Russian invasion of Ukraine.[2] Contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argues the Orange Revolution's success was pyrrhic — [[viktor-yushchenko|Viktor Yushchenko]]'s government fractured through infighting, paving the way for [[viktor-yanukovych|Viktor Yanukovych]]'s 2010 return and the 2014 Maidan events, which he calls "yet another U.S. orchestrated coup using the exact same tactics and funding mechanisms."[2] The article situates both episodes within the Western foreign policy establishment's undeclared second Cold War against Russia and cites [[gene-sharp|Gene Sharp]]'s 1985 advocacy of civilian-based methods for [[nato|NATO]] as their doctrinal origin.[2]
