# Electronic Voting Equipment

Electronic voting equipment refers to the machines and accompanying technology used to mark, scan, tabulate, and report votes in U.S. elections. Its nationwide adoption was mandated by the [[help-america-vote-act|Help America Vote Act]], with the [[election-assistance-commission|U.S. Election Assistance Commission]] created to distribute federal funding and to test and approve the equipment and its vendors.[1]

## Growth into an industry

Badlands Media contributor [[holly-at-altitude|Holly at Altitude]] argued that the HAVA mandate turned U.S. elections into a multi-billion-dollar tech-driven industry almost overnight, expanding over subsequent decades into an organically developed, resilient, self-reinforcing, and overly complex system.[1] In her assessment that complex system has "grown malignant, corrupt, and untrustworthy," with its winners — companies, agencies, politicians, corporate media, NGOs, and power brokers — desperate to keep the status quo while American citizens lose.[1]

## Budget and contract challenges

Two of the five local remedies proposed in her March 23, 2023 article target electronic voting equipment directly.[1] The first is to challenge local and state election equipment budgets: identify who controls them (most likely county commissioners), obtain contracts and financial records for equipment, maintenance, and operation, and build a comparison budget against the cost of [[hand-counted-paper-ballots|hand-counted paper ballots]] for public presentation to elected officials.[1]

The second is to challenge election equipment contracts themselves.[1] The article noted that localities generally have rules, statutes, or laws governing how contracts are evaluated, how many competitive bids are required, and how awards are made — provisions that matter given the small number of election equipment vendors — and urged citizens to study those laws, examine the electronic voting contracts in detail for violations, and publicly prove any infraction that would render the contracts null and void.[1]
