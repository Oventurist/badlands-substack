# Administrative State

The administrative state is the network of federal regulatory agencies that write, interpret and enforce rules with the force of law, often adjudicating disputes internally through administrative law judges rather than in Article III courts.[1] Critics on the right, and in the [[truth-community|Truth Community]], regard it as an unelected fourth branch of government and treat it as functionally continuous with the [[deep-state|Deep State]].[1]

## SEC v. Jarkesy

On June 27, 2024, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruled in [[sec-v-jarkesy|SEC v. Jarkesy]] that defendants accused of fraud by the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|Securities and Exchange Commission]] have a Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial when the agency seeks civil penalties.[1] Contemporary coverage called it a serious blow to the administrative state and part of a years-long project by the financial sector and conservatives to weaken federal power.[1]

The decision reached beyond the SEC because agencies including the [[environmental-protection-agency|Environmental Protection Agency]], the Labor Department and OSHA also rely on administrative law judges — roughly 2,000 of them across the federal government — who report to agencies in the executive branch rather than sitting within the judiciary.[1] Analysts noted it was one of several cases that term chipping away at agency power, with the most consequential, concerning the deference courts owe agency interpretations, still undecided.[1] The case arose after [[george-jarkesy|George Jarkesy]], who ran two hedge funds the SEC alleged were fraudulent, was brought before an in-house agency judge.[1]

## Badlands framing

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the ruling — and the dismay of Slate's Mark Joseph Stern — as evidence that "the promised neutering of the (Deep) Administrative State is WELL underway."[1] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], surveying the same day's four decisions, added that the term's docket was "a stark reminder that our rights are under assault every day" and predicted the Court itself would remain a major campaign issue.[1] Among those rulings, Ohio v. EPA stayed the agency's air-quality actions pending a challenge in the D.C. Circuit, another marker of judicial pushback against federal regulatory reach.[1]

## Invoked over the Bongino FBI exit (December 2025)

Following [[dan-bongino|Dan Bongino]]'s December 2025 announcement that he would leave the [[fbi|FBI]], Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] recalled that President [[donald-trump|Trump]]'s stated core theme for 2025 was "dismantling the Administrative State" and returning power to the people, and asked whether Trump was in practice attempting to preserve federal power or rein it in.[2]

## Anticipated Supreme Court retrenchment (2023 term)

Lawyers who regularly argue before the Supreme Court predicted at a September 20, 2023 event hosted by The Heritage Foundation that the Court might take steps in its approaching term to roll back the administrative state.[3] Participants included Paul Clement of Clement and Murphy, who served as U.S. solicitor general under George W. Bush from July 2004 to June 2008, and Lisa Blatt, a partner at Williams and Connolly.[3] The central vehicle discussed was Securities and Exchange Commission v. Jarkesy, in which the Fifth Circuit held that removal protections for administrative law judges unconstitutionally shielded them from presidential oversight, that in-house SEC proceedings violated the Seventh Amendment jury-trial right, and that Congress had unconstitutionally delegated lawmaking power to the commission.[3]
