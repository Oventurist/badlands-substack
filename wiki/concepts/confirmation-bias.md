# Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias — described by [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] in his January 26, 2024 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay as the "confirmation bias fallacy" — is the tendency to select information supporting one's existing views, ignore contrary information, and interpret ambiguous evidence as supportive of attitudes already held.[1] Carlson noted that the effect is strongest for desired outcomes, for emotionally charged issues, and for deeply entrenched beliefs.[1]

## Role in narrative control

Carlson treated the bias as cutting in both directions. It is, in his account, the mechanism by which large numbers of people remain "asleep" despite the volume of information he regards as disproving [[deep-state|Deep State]] narratives — because humans reject data that conflicts with what they already hold.[1] But the same mechanism operates on truth-seekers, whose confirmation bias "can cause us to be led astray" when disinformation is tailored to validate what they already believe.[1] This makes the bias the operative lever in both [[controlled-opposition|controlled opposition]] and [[poisoning-the-well|poisoning the well]]: material that flatters a pre-existing conviction is accepted without vetting.[1]

## Diagnostic use

Rather than treating bias-confirmation as automatically disqualifying, Carlson made it the fifth of five questions to apply to any claim, alongside who is speaking, on what platform, why one is hearing it, and who benefits or is harmed by belief in it.[1] Information that supports one's bias "doesn't necessarily make it wrong, it just means you need to factor it in when you process the information" — and for a person diligently seeking truth from sources they consider reliable, most information encountered will naturally align with their existing view.[1]

## Self-validation and the Ramaswamy episode

Carlson illustrated the failure mode with the reaction to the brief public dispute between [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and [[vivek-ramaswamy|Vivek Ramaswamy]] before the [[2024-iowa-caucus|Iowa caucus]].[1] Commentators who had long called Ramaswamy a "snake" treated Trump's criticism as vindication; when Ramaswamy withdrew the following day, endorsed Trump fully, and drew warm remarks from Trump about future collaboration, the same commentators pivoted to reading a "weird look" from Trump as continued distrust.[1] Carlson characterised this as pure confirmation bias — people "only looking to validate themselves" — while declining to call them bad or necessarily wrong, and contrasted it with his own willingness to admit he might have been mistaken when the narrative appeared to change.[1]

He extended the point to the treatment of [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]], whose red-string bracelet was cited by a "truth influencer" as evidence of Satanism; five minutes of checking established that Carlson had said his daughter made it for him.[1] Erik Carlson described this as the habit of coming "in guns blazing on everything — shoot first and ask questions later, or don't ask questions at all."[1]
