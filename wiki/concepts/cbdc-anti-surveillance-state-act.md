# CBDC Anti-Surveillance State Act

The **CBDC Anti-Surveillance State Act** is United States legislation that would bar the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] from issuing a central bank digital currency directly to individuals.[1]

## Legislative history

The bill was introduced in September 2023 by House Majority Whip [[tom-emmer|Tom Emmer]] (R-Minn.) and advanced out of the [[house-financial-services-committee|House Financial Services Committee]] that month over heavy Democratic criticism.[1] The committee's ranking Democrat, [[maxine-waters|Maxine Waters]] (D-Calif.), called the measure "anti-innovation."[1]

On May 23, 2024, the [[us-house-of-representatives|House of Representatives]] passed the bill by a vote of 216 to 192 along largely partisan lines, with 213 Republicans in favor, three Democrats joining them, and 192 Democrats opposed.[1]

## Rationale

Emmer's stated motivation was concern about broad government monitoring of financial transactions. He had previously argued that [[central-bank-digital-currency|CBDCs]] are "government-controlled programmable money that, if not designed to emulate cash, could give the federal government the ability to surveil Americans' transactions and choke out politically unpopular activity."[1] A CBDC is a cryptocurrency issued and regulated by a country's central bank.[1]

## Origins and rationale (2023)

House Republicans introduced the bill amid warnings that a [[central-bank-digital-currency|CBDC]] could amount to an "authoritarian-style" and "surveillance-style" digital dollar, framing the measure as a restraint on "unelected bureaucrats" establishing and issuing a currency that would threaten the financial privacy of the American people.[2] [[tom-emmer|Tom Emmer]] said in a statement that "any digital version of the dollar must uphold our American values of privacy, individual sovereignty, and free-market competitiveness," adding that "anything less opens the door to the development of a dangerous surveillance tool."[2] Rep. [[warren-davidson|Warren Davidson]] (R-Ohio) argued that the [[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] must concentrate on its dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment instead of "eradicating financial autonomy," warning that "a retail CBDC would essentially allow the government to mediate all transactions, which would mirror what we see in China."[2]
