# United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA)

The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA) is the trilateral North American trade pact governing commerce between the United States, Canada and Mexico. Among its provisions is a restriction on member states negotiating free trade treaties with non-market economies — such as China — without notifying their treaty partners.[1]

## The 2026 Canada–China dispute

The agreement's non-market-economy clause became central to a January 2026 confrontation between Ottawa and Washington. Canadian Prime Minister [[mark-carney|Mark Carney]] said that Canada had no plans to pursue a free trade agreement with China, responding to President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s threat to impose a 100 percent tariff on Canadian imports if Ottawa were to finalize such a deal.[1] Carney characterized Canada's recent arrangement with China as a limited measure reducing tariffs on a narrow set of products rather than a comprehensive free trade pact.[1]

Carney explicitly reiterated that Canada would honor its commitments under the USMCA and stressed that Ottawa had no intention of moving toward a broader trade deal with Beijing.[1] The preliminary Canada–China arrangement announced earlier in January 2026 included tariff reductions on Chinese electric vehicles and lower duties on Canadian agricultural exports such as canola, following a period of elevated tariffs between the two countries.[1] China's foreign ministry said the arrangements were not aimed at third parties.[1]

The episode reflected broader geopolitical and economic friction among the three countries, with Ottawa attempting to manage its dominant trade relationship with the United States while diversifying foreign partnerships under Carney's leadership.[1]

## 2025 tariffs and the free-trade bet

After President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s 25% tariffs hit Mexico and Canada in March 2025, both economies faced a stress test rivalling the global financial crisis and the pandemic, according to Wall Street Journal reporting.[2] Each country has separately signed more than a dozen trade treaties giving them open access to the markets of more than 50 countries, but the most important remains the US–Mexico–Canada Agreement, an updated version of the 1994 North American Free Trade Agreement that eliminated most barriers between the three countries; more than 80% of both countries' exports go to the United States.[2] The arrangement turned Mexico into an export powerhouse in vehicles, beer and flat-screen televisions, while Canada used free trade deals with the US dating to the 1960s to vault its $2 trillion economy into the G-7.[2] Badlands contributor Burning Bright argued that the brilliance of Trump's trade strategy was that his threats baited "tariff tantrums" functioning as de facto defenses of sovereignty, since Trump wants nationalist rather than communist neighbors, and that most of the deployments were feints.[2]


## Badlands assessment

A 2023 Badlands Media retrospective described the signing of the USMCA as a major victory for [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]], arguing that the agreement opened the job market and brought manufacturing jobs back to the United States rather than allowing them to be sent overseas.[3] The author characterized the deal as a replacement for what he called the disastrous NAFTA agreement put in place under President Bill Clinton.[3]
