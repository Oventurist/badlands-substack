# Operation Cyclone

Operation Cyclone was the covert [[cia|CIA]] program to arm and train Afghan insurgents fighting Soviet forces and the Soviet-aligned government in Afghanistan during the Cold War.[1]

## Badlands account

In Badlands Media's chronology of the late 1970s, Operation Cyclone is presented as one of several concurrent American covert efforts running while [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]] served as National Security Advisor — the CIA "arming and training Osama bin Laden in Afghanistan to fight the Soviets via Operation Cyclone" at the same time the agency was preparing the overthrow of the Shah of Iran and Iran-Contra was in motion.[1]

The program is used in that commentary as evidence of a continuous institutional pattern: the same apparatus that manufactures and equips proxy forces abroad is held to be responsible for later constructions such as [[hamas]], described in the same article as "basically a street gang with CIA resources."[1]

## Badlands account

Badlands' July 2024 commentary described Operation Cyclone as the 1979 [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] plan devised by [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]] to arm the mujahideen in Afghanistan to fight the Russians.[2] Under the program, the account held, Brzezinski recruited the Saudi national [[osama-bin-laden|Osama bin Laden]], provided him with the best military training and weapons available, and gave him the resources to build a vast network of fighters drawn from across the Middle East — the network that became [[al-qaeda|Al Qaeda]] when bin Laden formalized it in 1988.[2]

## Narcotics dimension

Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] treats Operation Cyclone as a central episode in the pattern of American agencies tolerating narcotics trafficking by useful allies.[3] During the Soviet occupation of [[afghanistan|Afghanistan]] in the 1980s the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] backed [[mujahideen|Mujahedeen]] fighters including [[gulbuddin-hekmatyar|Gulbuddin Hekmatyar]], a known drug lord whose heroin empire supplied much of the world's market.[3] Afghan opium production surged in this period, with CIA-allied warlords controlling poppy-growing regions such as Helmand and Nangarhar, and heroin processed in laboratories along the Afghanistan-Pakistan border, often under [[inter-services-intelligence|ISI]] oversight, before being trafficked globally.[3]

Declassified records show that U.S. officials and the CIA were aware of the trade but prioritised the anti-Soviet fight over drug enforcement.[3] The declassified CIA assessment *Afghanistan's Expanded Opium Trade: Byproduct of War* notes that wartime disruption made heroin refining "an attractive enterprise" and states that "Afghanistan's role as a heroin supplier to international markets probably will expand as long as the instability and strife continue."[3] A second document declassified in 2013, *USSR: Domestic Fallout from the Afghan War*, discusses Soviet concerns about drug abuse among troops and notes Afghan opiates aboard Soviet vessels, indirectly indicating the scale of a trade the CIA monitored but did not act against.[3] The 1989 [[kerry-committee-report|Kerry Committee report]] concluded more generally that U.S. officials overlooked drug activities by allies when they served strategic interests.[3] Many CIA records from Operation Cyclone remain classified, limiting direct evidence of internal decision-making.[3]

## Origins and scale

Cyclone began as a program of the [[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]] under the administration of [[jimmy-carter|Jimmy Carter]], continued through the [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]] era into the Bush administration, and ultimately funneled a massive $3 billion in American taxpayer dollars into the coffers of the [[mujahideen]] between 1979 and 1992, making it one of the agency's most expensive covert operations.[4] The idea originated in the late 1970s as Pakistani intelligence officials privately lobbied the United States and its allies to send material and financial assistance to the Afghan resistance, and it was Carter's National Security Advisor [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]] who convinced the president of the importance of funding it.[4]

Contrary to the prevailing narrative of the 1980s and 1990s, Brzezinski conceded in a 1998 interview with *Le Nouvel Observateur* that Carter signed the first directive for secret aid to opponents of the pro-Soviet regime in Kabul on July 3, 1979 — almost six months before the Soviet invasion.[4] [[robert-gates|Robert Gates]], deputy CIA Director during the [[soviet-afghan-war|Soviet-Afghan War]], likewise indicates in his memoirs that U.S. intelligence was channeling aid to the Islamic brigades before Soviet troops arrived.[4]

## Mechanics and misappropriation

Arms and money reached the mujahideen indirectly, passing through Pakistan's [[inter-services-intelligence|ISI]] — an agency the economist Michel Chossudovsky describes as essentially CIA-controlled since the Soviet-Afghan War.[4] The Carter administration also secured a pledge from [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] to match U.S. funding through the same Pakistani channels.[4] Routing aid through [[pakistan]] led to massive misappropriation: many weapons were sold in markets in Karachi rather than delivered to Afghan rebels, and Karachi soon "became one of the most violent cities in the world."[4] Of the seven mujahideen groups supported by the Pakistani government, four espoused Islamic fundamentalist beliefs, and it was these fundamentalists who received most of the funding, weapons and training — the factions that would one day form the [[taliban]].[4]

The program expanded tremendously under Reagan, who met with mujahideen representatives in the Oval Office on multiple occasions; among the weapons supplied was the [[fim-92-stinger|FIM-92 Stinger]] MANPADS, with at least 2,300 shipped into Afghanistan.[4] Massive refugee camps along the Pakistani border, supported by millions in U.S. aid, became the seedbed of radicalization when Saudi Arabia — with the unofficial blessing of the U.S. security state — sent hardline fundamentalist clerics, among them [[osama-bin-laden|Osama bin Laden]], to indoctrinate tens of thousands of young rebels.[4]

## Radicalization pipeline

Bin Laden's precursor organization [[maktab-al-khidamat|Maktab al-Khidamat]] received CIA money for recruiting young Muslim men into the Afghan jihad, according to reporting cited in the article.[4] American funds also paid for Afghan schoolbooks filled with violent imagery and militant Islamic teachings, textbooks that later served as the Afghan school system's core curriculum and were used even by the Taliban.[4] The Egyptian double agent [[ali-mohamed|Ali Abdul Saoud Mohamed]] was sent to recruit American Muslims out of the [[al-kifah-refugee-center|Al Kifah Refugee Center]] in Brooklyn and later trained fighters in Pakistan.[4]

## Consequences

Funding was supplemented by Britain's MI6, Saudi Arabia and China, and the war became the "Vietnamese quagmire" Brzezinski had predicted, with Soviet withdrawal beginning in 1987 and completing in early 1989.[4] Asked in 1998 whether he regretted an operation that had led to the creation of the Taliban, Brzezinski replied: "What is more important in world history? The Taliban or the collapse of the Soviet empire?"[4] Badlands Media frames Cyclone as the bridge the CIA, the security state and the [[military-industrial-complex|Military-Industrial Complex]] used to move from endlessly fighting the Cold War to endlessly fighting the [[war-on-terror|War on Terror]], and links it to the transformation of Afghanistan into the source of roughly 80% of the world's [[opium-production-in-afghanistan|opium]].[4] [[israel]] also played a role, with Israeli military instructors training thousands of mujahideen fighters and selling them weapons seized from the Soviets during the Yom Kippur War.[4]

## Team B origins and the narcotics dimension

[[paul-fitzgerald|Paul Fitzgerald]] and [[elizabeth-gould|Elizabeth Gould]] argue in [[valediction-three-nights-of-desmond|Valediction: Three Nights of Desmond]] that the clandestine Operation Cyclone was premised on the lies of [[zbigniew-brzezinski|Zbigniew Brzezinski]]'s [[team-b|Team B]] analysis of Soviet ambitions to dominate the world, which justified a programme using billions of dollars in taxpayer money to fund the growth of mujahideen terrorist cells and narcotics in a bid to light a fire under Russia's soft underbelly and suck the unsuspecting Soviets into a bloodletting that would be sold to a western population as "Russia's Vietnam."[5] In their account the money pit that Cyclone became sponsored the terrorist movements that soon morphed into [[al-qaeda|Al-Qaeda]] and produced the world's largest heroin production zone in the heart of Mackinder's World Island.[5]

The programme's antecedent, on this reading, was the February 1979 killing of Ambassador [[adolph-dubs|Adolph Dubs]], whose covert diplomacy with President [[hafizullah-amin|Hafizullah Amin]] threatened to keep the Soviets out of the Afghan trap altogether.[5] Over forty years later, the results are well known: the Soviet Union was bled, leading to its dissolution under Gorbachev, and the world was given Islamic terrorism funded, armed and trained by CIA, MI6 and ISI operatives.[5]

Organised crime syndicates also grew their influence in leaps and bounds as the world centre of opium production moved from Cambodia, Vietnam, Laos and Myanmar to more fertile soil in Afghanistan, providing both the funding to light the region on fire for decades and a new opium war against all of civilisation.[5] The article adds that the conspicuous integration of the [[drug-enforcement-administration|DEA]] and [[cia|CIA]] during this period, coinciding with the heroin boom and with the flooding of crack cocaine into American ghettos under CIA director [[george-hw-bush|George H. W. Bush]] — himself a defender of the Team B takeover of U.S. intelligence estimates — cannot be ignored.[5]
