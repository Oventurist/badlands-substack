# A Hypnotist's Take on Israel, Palestine, and Q

"A Hypnotist's Take on Israel, Palestine, and Q" is an opinion essay published by Badlands Media on October 23, 2023, written by the pseudonymous contributor American Hypnotist. Subtitled "Has the Mockingbird Media Finally Destroyed Itself?", the piece applies the vocabulary of clinical hypnosis and persuasion to mass media coverage of the Israel–Palestine conflict, arguing that the emotional saturation of that coverage is causing the machinery of Mockingbird propaganda to fail rather than succeed.

The article opens with an epigraph attributed to Clif High — "Listen up, butthead! If you don't choose a side, you won't be able to have your emotions torqued & be manipulated!" — and with the author's framing premise: "The world we live in is fake. The hard part is figuring out **specifically how** it's fake. And the fact that everyone is yelling doesn't help either."

## Disinformation and emotional heightening

The author defines disinformation as "the art of blending truth and falsehood into a dizzying web designed to stir the passions of the masses and lead them towards specific actions." He recalls a hypnosis teacher telling him that "if I can heighten someone's emotions then I can give them a hypnotic suggestion," and draws the corollary that the key to remaining un-hypnotized is to remain as emotion-neutral as possible amid chaos.

Citing polling from Rasmussen, the article states that 58% of Americans today believe the media is deliberately misleading them, and predicts the figure will climb sharply in the coming months. It qualifies this optimism, however: the author suspects that many of those same 58% still believe *their* preferred outlet could not possibly be deceiving them, because "old tribal loyalties and strong emotions are keeping people tethered to their old programming." The Israel–Palestine conflict, he argues, is nonetheless "proving to be a wrecking ball for many people's sense of tribal-loyalty, on both the right and the left."

The essay uses the physics of an avalanche as its central metaphor: at first only a few patches of snow slide out of place, but this destabilizes the whole structure until it collapses at once — a "point of no return." The author wonders whether the Israel–Palestine conflict will serve as that point for "the edifice of mockingbird programming that has ensnared the minds of generations."

## Propaganda as directed emotional discharge

Recalling a Photoshop mini-course he took in 2015, the author says the single lasting lesson was that "anybody can make anything look like anything" — a problem magnified since by deepfakes, and one that in his view justifies near-categorical skepticism of everything found online or off. Yet society, he writes, demands that people pick sides and start hurling rocks before they have any reasonable opportunity to determine the facts.

The article argues that a key feature of divisive propaganda is that it must not only "activate the amygdala" but do so in a way that provokes a pre-programmed response. It is not enough for social engineers that people be fearful or enraged; the essential ingredient is that the enraged take their emotions out in a direction the engineers have set up. Anger, in the author's simile, is like rocket fuel, but a rocket is useless if it cannot be aimed. He contends that audiences have now reached a point where they are "losing sight of *where* to direct their emotions," which he reads as a critical breakdown in Mockingbird social-engineering technology — "a critical crack in the dam," though not proof that the public is out of the woods.

Two cultural artifacts are offered as evidence of this breakdown: a skit by comedian Ryan Long posted to X, and a comic strip by the cartoonist Stonetoss titled "Whodunit." The essay also invokes [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s characterization of the mainstream media as the "enemy of the people," presenting it as a description of media's function in preventing the public from seeing "the puppeteers pulling the strings behind the scenes."

## The author's stated position

The author sets out his own stance in two propositions: first, that "criminal enterprises masquerading as 'government' are, without exception, wrong"; and second, that innocent civilians must not be held personally culpable for the actions of such governments. The goal, he writes, should not be for one side to lay waste to the other but for the removal of criminal elements from these governments. Beyond that he declines to commit, stating: "I refuse to be goaded into taking a stance without sufficient reason for doing so." Being legitimately "awake," in his formulation, means recognizing that the world is full of fake news designed to hijack cognition by first hijacking emotions and needs. He asks how someone living halfway across the world is supposed to know precisely what is happening when propagandists from every corner of the Earth skew the story in different directions for knowable and unknowable reasons.

## Nominalizations and hypnotic repetition

A substantial section is devoted to the hypnotic concept of the nominalization — an intangible noun. The author asks how one keeps two or more groups fighting forever, and answers: by making sure they fight over words they cannot define in the first place, and by silencing the truth-tellers in their midst. His example is "fascism": one cannot put it in a wheelbarrow or study it under a microscope, and absent an agreed definition the word becomes "nothing more than an excuse to attack random people," retaining utility only as a hypnotic trigger. A nominalization is likened to an empty cup into which almost any meaning can be poured; the article notes that people who act monstrously toward others routinely call it "love."

Nominalizations and hypnotic repetition are described as the two main pillars of modern propaganda: attach as much emotion as possible to a word that has no commonly agreed definition, then repeat it endlessly until the learned response becomes automatic — comparable, the author says, to how quickly a person responds when someone calls their name. Learning to recognize nominalizations in real time, he claims, "will make you relatively invincible to all forms of Mockingbird propaganda."

## Breakdown, pain, and the compliance ladder

The article warns that having one's reality torn asunder can be comparable to torture, and connects this to [[q-anon|Q]]'s repeated warnings of "pain incoming." People generally do not change, the author argues, unless they are in pain in some sense; the pain of social ostracization alone will cause people to keep reshaping themselves to conform to shifting norms, a mechanism marketing and persuasion literature calls the "compliance ladder."

The spell breaks, in his account, when the social environment degrades past the point where it can deliver coherent instructions to its members about how to act and think — "You need a functioning ladder, you see." The bar for coherence is very low for many people, but some minimum is necessary, because a person literally cannot walk forwards and backwards at the same time. As long as a program is *executable*, it functions; when it ceases to be executable it "short circuits," an experience the author calls primordially terrifying for the person undergoing it.

## Q as a destination for broken narratives

The final section argues that the mainstream media, like any pathological liar, "NEEDS to be utterly discredited" — framed not as malice but as a survival imperative, and only a first step. For the average person, the author writes, the [[q-anon|Q]] movement is loosely associated with the idea that everything we see is scripted and fake, and the wide diffusion of that idea is "giving people a place to go when their old narratives break down."

Because the Middle East conflict is quasi-religious in nature, receives nonstop air time, and reaches back thousands of years, the article holds that it is guaranteed to affect people deeply, raising fundamental questions of right and wrong and of truth and falsehood for millions simultaneously — extraordinary timing given the general collapse of faith in media. Both the "right" and the "left" are described as watching their internal coherence crumble, since party affiliation no longer predicts where a person stands: "'Peer pressure' doesn't work if you don't know what you're supposed to do about it!"

From this the author makes his central prediction: "I believe WWIII will be like a car that won't start—or at least has a lot of trouble getting started—prompting people to look under the hood. No amount of 'rage-fuel' can repair a broken engine!" Those who look under the hood, he says, will see the same truth exposed by decorated general Smedley Butler in the 1930s — that war is a racket and the puppeteers play both sides against each other. While acknowledging that far more than the Israel–Palestine conflict is driving the transformation, the author predicts this conflict will be the final straw for many: "The avalanche is really getting started, now."

The piece closes with Badlands Media's standard disclaimer that articles and features represent the opinions of contributing authors and do not necessarily represent the views of Badlands Media itself, along with a pointer to the author's own outlet, American Hypnotist.
