# MAGA Schism

The **MAGA Schism** is the term used by Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] in a November 18, 2025 essay to describe the fracturing of the broad, loose-knit coalition that carried [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] to victory in the [[2024-us-general-election|2024 presidential election]].[1] Writing exactly one year after that victory, DeLarme argued that the sense of triumph and excitement that had prevailed among the coalition's factions had turned to apprehension, and that "the greater coalition is in tatters."[1] The essay was framed around the question of whether the chasm could be bridged, and was headed by a quotation attributed to [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]]: "Conservative inc. are the clapping seals of the uniparty."[1]

## The bedrock exception

DeLarme allowed that the fracture was not universal: there would always be a bedrock of "ride-or-die" Trump devotees who never question or criticize anything the administration does, people for whom the rollercoaster of the Trump saga has shaped their political outlook and for whom the fight has become part of their identity.[1] He characterized this camp as unlikely to waver, but also as only a marginal faction of the greater coalition.[1]

## Taxonomy of factions

The essay set out a personal taxonomy of the factions within the greater MAGA, or [[america-first-movement|"America First"]], movement.[1]

- **Garden-variety Republicans** — voters and politicians who cling to traditional Republican orthodoxy, vote red regardless of circumstance and view the [[republican-party|GOP]] as "their team" rather than a barrier to America's greatness.[1] Shaped by years of [[fox-news|Fox News]] and establishment rhetoric, they supported Romney and Bush, were firmly anti-Trump even after his 2016 nomination, and embraced him only once resistance became politically untenable; the faction contains both duplicitous never-Trump [[rino|RINOs]] and genuine Trump supporters still ensnared by conventional Republican groupthink.[1]
- **[[con-inc|Con Inc.]]** — figures such as [[ben-shapiro|Ben Shapiro]], [[steven-crowder|Steven Crowder]] and [[dinesh-dsouza|Dinesh D'Souza]], described as largely fitting the garden-variety Republican mold with a younger, more polished veneer, tracking the establishment line on foreign policy while concentrating on culture-war battles and "owning the libs."[1]
- **Reluctantly right-leaning libertarians** — the constituency of Ron Paul and [[pat-buchanan|Pat Buchanan]], typically proponents of the [[mises-institute|Mises Institute]] and the [[austrian-school-of-economics|Austrian School of Economics]], emphasizing classical liberal principles with a conservative tilt, reduced government intervention, property rights, free markets and traditional values.[1]
- **Populist nationalists** — the [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]], [[peter-navarro|Pete Navarro]] and [[russ-vought|Russ Vought]] faction: not quite mainstream Republican but certainly mainstream MAGA, the inner-circle progenitors of Trump's America First messaging in term one and instrumental propagandists for Trump 2.0, willing to criticize Trump — and occasionally [[israel|Israel]] — with a degree of nuance not typically found on Fox News.[1]
- **Zionists, Christian or otherwise** — a subset of mainstream Republicans that seeks alignment with the populist-nationalist camp but is increasingly viewed by other factions as at odds with America First ideology, with views ranging from a strategically constructive [[zionism|U.S.-Israel]] partnership to fervent, almost thoughtless pro-Israel zeal rooted in religious or ideological conviction; named figures include [[laura-loomer|Laura Loomer]], [[ted-cruz|Ted Cruz]], [[mike-huckabee|Mike Huckabee]] and [[randy-fine|Randy Fine]].[1]
- **Anti-war, anti-establishment** — a faction that approaches issues with greater nuance, recognizing that both parties are influenced by corporations, intelligence agencies and the [[military-industrial-complex|military-industrial complex]]; its thought leaders include [[scott-horton|Scott Horton]], [[glenn-greenwald|Glenn Greenwald]], [[john-mearsheimer|John Mearsheimer]] and [[jeffrey-sachs|Jeffrey Sachs]], and it spans the left ([[max-blumenthal|Max Blumenthal]], [[aaron-mate|Aaron Maté]]) and the right ([[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]], [[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]], [[megyn-kelly|Megyn Kelly]]).[1] Opposing neoconservatism, it rejects U.S. funding of Israel's actions in Gaza and the West Bank.[1]
- **Content clowns** — a category DeLarme said one knows when one sees, naming [[ian-carroll|Ian Carroll]], [[dom-lucre|Dom Lucre]] and [[candace-owens|Candace Owens]], and possibly [[alex-jones|Alex Jones]], while conceding that Jones was an instrumental part of the big-tent coalition.[1]
- **The "Why Can't We Be Friends" crowd** — those trying to hold the coalition together, refraining from comment on polarizing topics, especially U.S.-Israel policy.[1] [[matt-taibbi|Matt Taibbi]] is described as taking flak from both sides for not taking sides, while [[mike-benz|Mike Benz]], though critical of anti-Semitism laws and of conditioning grant funding on a grantee's willingness to combat anti-Semitism, refuses to take a position because he believes the coalition's survival matters more.[1]
- **Q-informed "cozy popcorn lovers"** — a self-aware, skeptical group encompassing the broadest range of opinion, viewing much public discourse as superficial theater, which lets them see past the left/right divide and resist prepackaged narratives, but can tip into excessive credulity; grifters and self-proclaimed insiders muddy the waters, and many in the group invoke the phrase "Saving Israel for Last," whose meaning DeLarme called unclear and speculative.[1]

Beyond these, DeLarme identified a further spectrum within the "far-right" anti-Israel crowd, running from the [[nick-fuentes|Nick Fuentes]]/[[groypers|Groyper]] brand of America First to what he called genuine neo-Nazi types such as [[andrew-anglin|Andrew Anglin]].[1] He hesitated to count them in the coalition at all, believing they had a more deleterious than positive effect on the movement and doubting they saw themselves as part of it, noting that Fuentes has been extremely critical of Trump and said publicly that he did not vote for him.[1] DeLarme conceded the taxonomy was imperfect, with countless factions within factions and blended outlooks.[1]

## Polling decline

DeLarme reported that mounting criticism from Trump voters had driven the president's approval rating from a peak at his second-term inauguration to a record low by mid-November 2025, joking that he had weighed "the Pro-Trump fake polls against the anti-Trump fake polls to get a more accurate fake poll average."[1] The drop was most pronounced among independents, at more than 20 points, and among younger voters, with every state except Idaho shifting negative according to [[the-economist|The Economist]].[1] [[rasmussen-reports|Rasmussen]]'s more conservative-leaning methodology still captured the erosion, with strong disapproval rising to 45 percent.[1]

Trump had initially enjoyed an unprecedented 60 percent approval rating among voters under 40 per Rasmussen, but support among those under 30 had dipped below 40 percent.[1] Younger voters, DeLarme wrote, had expected a move toward economic populism and a rejection of corporatism's erosion of capitalism, along with accountability and reform on the weaponization of the justice system and an overreaching healthcare state, and had seen no progress.[1] Their enthusiastic support for [[doge|DOGE]]'s aggressive dismantling of government structures indicated that their priority lay in transforming the system rather than sustaining the objectives of the international foreign policy establishment and the corporations profiting from them.[1] For most Republicans, by contrast, it was "business as usual" in Trump 2.0.[1] Trump's rating nonetheless remained higher than [[joe-biden|Biden]]'s at the equivalent point, though Biden had faced backlash over the chaotic Afghanistan withdrawal.[1]

## Grievances driving the split

DeLarme prefaced his list with an author's note stating that the grievances were those voiced by factions of Trump's base rather than his own criticisms, that the behind-the-scenes dynamics and future direction of the administration were unknown, and that he was reserving judgment less than a year into the term.[1] The listed factors were:[1]

- **Failure to reduce inflation and deliver affordability** — despite pledges to "skyrocket incomes" and eliminate inflation, 19 percent of 2024 Trump voters disapproved of his handling of prices, with voters reporting no real tax breaks or price reductions and expressing "buyer's remorse."[1]
- **Overemphasis on Israel and foreign aid** — a vocal subset felt Trump had prioritized "Israel First," continuing funding for Gaza and West Bank operations and showing insufficient criticism of Israeli actions, alienating anti-interventionists who expected an end to "Zionist wars."[1]
- **Ukraine and Russia** — some were disappointed by perceived softness toward [[vladimir-putin|Putin]], others thought him too soft on [[ukraine|Ukraine]] and were frustrated by continued funding, and more still were upset that he had not ended the war on "day one" as promised, though Trump himself admitted the promise was hyperbole.[1]
- **General foreign adventurism** — criticism for starting or escalating conflicts, importing Argentinian beef at the expense of U.S. farmers, and inviting "terrorists" to the [[white-house|White House]], all seen as betraying isolationist promises.[1]
- **[[epstein-files|Epstein files]]** — Trump's reversal on releasing the documents, and his description of supporters demanding them as "weaklings," fueled accusations of hypocrisy and cover-up.[1]
- **The [[h-1b-visa-program|H-1B]] fiasco** — some see the visas as essential while others regard them as another betrayal of America First, insisting the United States led in technological innovation long before the program was created.[1]

Voters were also described as frustrated by the lack of progress on drilling and energy independence, pharmaceutical deregulation, and by policies such as 50-year mortgages and 15-year car loans viewed as detrimental to workers.[1] DeLarme said he raised the grievances not to sway opinion but because they must be addressed to prevent losses in the House and Senate at the midterms and to ensure success for the prospective 2028 candidate.[1]

## Acceleration in November 2025

The fragmentation went "into overdrive" in the weeks before publication.[1] DeLarme quoted his Badlands colleague [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] — "Can whoever removed a chromosome from MAGA this week put it back already?" — and said [[michael-flynn-jr|Flynn Jr]], whom he described as far more immune to partisan orthodoxy and blind allegiance than the vast majority of the "new media," had expressed what many were feeling.[1] He observed that Trump appeared to be moving away from his most stalwart supporters and closer to those who had proven themselves conduits for the neoconservative agenda, and noted that [[jordan-sather|Jordan Sather]] had a compelling theory about the fallout between Trump and [[marjorie-taylor-greene|Marjorie Taylor Greene]].[1]

DeLarme declined to assert that the estrangement was an elaborate ruse in which Trump was secretly aligning with [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]] and Greene, while not ruling it out, and raised the possibility that for operational security Trump was deliberately distancing himself from his biggest supporters in order to align with figures such as [[lindsey-graham|Lindsey Graham]], [[randy-fine|Randy Fine]] and [[mark-levin|Mark Levin]].[1] He added that he still regarded the situation as immensely preferable to a [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]] presidency.[1]

## "The Big Divide": foreign policy

Asked to identify the single greatest source of friction, DeLarme named foreign policy.[1] Trump had run in all three of his elections on ending endless wars and reining in the neocons, the foreign policy establishment and the military-industrial complex, chastising the Bushes, McCains and Cheneys and the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.[1] By contrast, Trump 2.0 appeared outwardly to be aligning with establishment Republicans such as Lindsey Graham while distancing itself from even mild critics — producing two competing readings within the base: that Trump is simply drawn to whoever flatters him most and reflexively shuts out dissenters regardless of past loyalty, or that the shift is a deliberate strategy of cozying up to the establishment for political cover while sidelining politically risky voices.[1]

The essay traced the architecture of the "forever wars" of the 1990s and 2000s to the [[project-for-the-new-american-century|Project for the New American Century]], the neoconservative think tank active from 1997 to 2006 that served as the neocon hotbed of the Clinton and Bush Jr. administrations.[1] Its fruits, per DeLarme, included the invasion of Iraq, the war in Afghanistan, the broader War on Terror doctrine and diplomatic cover for Israeli military operations, along with calls for war with Syria and Iran that are still being pushed in Trump 2.0.[1] He profiled key members — [[william-kristol|William Kristol]], [[robert-kagan|Robert Kagan]], [[paul-wolfowitz|Paul Wolfowitz]], [[richard-perle|Richard Perle]], [[john-bolton|John Bolton]], [[dick-cheney|Dick Cheney]], [[elliott-abrams|Elliott Abrams]] and [[norman-podhoretz|Norman Podhoretz]] — emphasizing their ties to Israel, and concluded that [[neoconservatives|neoconservatism]] and [[zionism|Zionism]] are deeply intertwined with little meaningful separation between them.[1] He declined to say whether that was good or bad, noting only that it appears fundamentally at odds with Trump's stated commitment to ending forever wars.[1]

## The donor question

DeLarme argued that without the support of both [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] and the right-leaning pro-Israel network — including the [[benjamin-netanyahu|Netanyahu]] government, [[miriam-adelson|Miriam Adelson]], the [[republican-jewish-coalition|Republican Jewish Coalition]] and [[bernie-marcus|Bernie Marcus]] — Trump's second term probably would not have materialized.[1] Pro-Israel donors provided over $200 million, including more than $100 million from Adelson alone, amplifying Trump's ground game in key states and solidifying his appeal among Jewish and evangelical voters; without it the race could have been tighter, potentially costing swing-state margins in Pennsylvania or Nevada, where Adelson's influence through her Las Vegas casino empire was pronounced.[1]

Many argue the arrangement was "kayfabe," with Trump courting donors through pro-Israel pledges such as West Bank annexation hints because otherwise he would lack the resources to counter Harris's billion-dollar machine.[1] DeLarme's crucial point was that the same machine could easily have been deployed against Trump had he taken a less accommodating line: in 2024, the moment a Republican candidate shows even modest daylight on Israel policy, those dollars and that organizational muscle flip from ally to adversary almost overnight, as seen with [[ron-desantis|Ron DeSantis]] in the primaries and with past figures like Pat Buchanan and [[rand-paul|Rand Paul]], who were frozen out of big donors and friendly media.[1] The backing was therefore "less a gift freely given and more a price of admission to remain viable on the Republican side in 2024."[1]

Asked whether this meant Trump was captured, DeLarme said he did not see Trump as someone who would swear complete fealty to any power center, but that he is indebted to these people, and that the resulting tightrope is one of several explanations for his complete reversal on Epstein transparency.[1] He also noted that the anti-war crowd, furious over the strikes on [[iran|Iran]] and the sinking of boats allegedly carrying fentanyl — a pretext he called dubious — rarely acknowledges that Trump stopped short of the full-scale kinetic regime-change war in Iran that Israel and the neocons wanted, and that treating Trump as Netanyahu's puppet is far too simplistic.[1]

## Proposed remedies

DeLarme answered his own title question affirmatively, but only if both sides treat the Israel/foreign-policy question as a policy disagreement rather than an identity-defining loyalty test, and only if Trump uses his political capital to force a real debate inside the movement.[1] His six proposals were:[1]

1. Reframe the debate from "Are you with Israel or against the Jews?" to "What does America First foreign policy demand in 2026 and beyond?", keeping it brutally pragmatic — dollars spent, body bags risked, strategic value gained or lost — because the moment the question becomes moral, religious or ethnic, the coalition dies.[1]
2. Have Trump publicly draw a line and enforce it, via a prime-time speech or Truth Social posts affirming love of Israel, the embassy move, the defeat of ISIS and the [[abraham-accords|Abraham Accords]] while declaring that America First means no American boys dying for new Middle East wars, no blank checks and no lobbying money buying his foreign policy.[1]
3. Create an "America First Foreign Policy Council" inside the White House or the RNC, seating [[steve-bannon|Bannon]], Tucker, [[vivek-ramaswamy|Vivek]], [[jd-vance|JD Vance]], Vought and realist pro-Israel voices such as [[jared-kushner|Jared Kushner]] or [[david-friedman|David Friedman]] to hammer out and publish a written doctrine.[1]
4. Depolarize the money by leaning on [[elon-musk|Musk]], [[peter-thiel|Thiel]], Midwest manufacturing billionaires and the crypto crowd to fund a parallel America First super PAC backing primary challengers against Republicans who vote for new Middle East wars or unconditional Israel funding.[1]
5. Stop purity spirals on both ends — dealing with the groypers and neo-Nazis in the public square while telling the "criticize Israel and you're an anti-Semite" crowd publicly and repeatedly that policy criticism is not hate speech.[1] DeLarme argued the young extremists are a liability today but potentially tomorrow's voters.[1]
6. Give the base a win it can feel in its wallet — lower grocery and gas prices and a genuine assault on the administrative and regulatory state via DOGE — because "wins buy time; stagnation breeds civil war."[1]

## Conclusion

DeLarme described himself self-deprecatingly as "a bumbling philistine on a computer" uncertain whether his proposals would work, but insisted that one cannot comment on politics without proposing solutions, and that the public must advocate solutions rather than wait for either faction of the ruling class to decide for them.[1] His closing judgment was that the divide can be bridged, but only from the top: if Trump spends the next six to twelve months drawing clear lines, rewarding loyalty and delivering tangible populist victories, the fractures can scar over, but if he keeps drifting toward what DeLarme called the Graham–Adelson axis and treats every critic as a traitor, the coalition will shatter and 2026 "will be a bloodbath."[1] He ended by noting that Trump has done the impossible before, and that the question is whether he still believes the movement he built is bigger than any single donor, ally or grudge.[1]
