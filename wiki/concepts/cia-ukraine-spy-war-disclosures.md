# CIA–Ukraine "Spy War" Disclosures (2024)

In late February 2024, as the war in Ukraine entered its third year, two major American legacy outlets published unusually candid accounts of the United States' role in the conflict: a [[new-york-times|New York Times]] investigation titled "The Spy War: How the C.I.A. Secretly Helps Ukraine Fight Putin," and a [[washington-post|Washington Post]] report on lessons the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] had drawn from two years of war.[1] Badlands Media treated the pair as a coordinated narrative shift by establishment media rather than as isolated scoops.[1]

## The New York Times account

The Times reported that the intelligence partnership between Washington and Kyiv had become a linchpin of Ukraine's ability to defend itself, with the [[cia|C.I.A.]] and other American intelligence agencies providing intelligence for targeted missile strikes, tracking Russian troop movements, and supporting spy networks.[1] Crucially, the paper stated the partnership was not a wartime creation and that Ukraine was not its only beneficiary: it had taken root a decade earlier, coming together in fits and starts under three very different U.S. presidents and pushed forward by key individuals who often took daring risks.[1] The relationship had transformed Ukraine — whose intelligence services were long seen as thoroughly compromised by Russia — into one of Washington's most important intelligence partners against the Kremlin.[1]

## The Washington Post account

The Post reported that the U.S. military was undertaking an expansive revision of its approach to warfighting, largely abandoning the counterinsurgency playbook that characterized Iraq and Afghanistan in order to prepare for a larger conflict with more sophisticated adversaries such as Russia or China.[1] The war had made clear to the Pentagon that battlefield calculations had fundamentally changed: precision weapons, fleets of drones and digital surveillance can reach far beyond the front lines, posing grave risk to personnel wherever they are.[1] Officials described the war as "an active and bountiful research opportunity" for American military planners, and a classified year-long study of lessons learned from both sides — led by 20 officers examining ground maneuver, air power, information warfare, sustaining and growing forces, and long-range fire capability — was to inform the next National Defense Strategy.[1]

## Badlands interpretation

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] wrote that the Times piece, describing a CIA "spy war" waged in Ukraine for a decade, was going viral "for all the right reasons on our side of the Info War, and all the wrong reasons" on the other.[1] What he found most notable was that this apparent about-face by what he called the narrative-setter of the [[globalist-hegemon|Globalist Hegemon]] was being mirrored by a series of parallel rhetorical retreats at other mainstream outlets.[1] Where the Times framed the CIA — among the most hated agencies in the world, and the one that had drawn the most fire from Vladimir Putin that month — as clandestine heroes, the Post backtracked in a different theater, effectively reporting that the U.S. military-industrial complex and [[deep-state|Deep State]] viewed the Ukraine–Russia conflict as a bountiful opportunity.[1] However much that language was couched in asides about key learnings and military strategy, he argued, it stood out to an audience grown both weary and increasingly wary of the central narrative and its progenitors.[1]
