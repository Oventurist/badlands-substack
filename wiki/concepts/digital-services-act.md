# Digital Services Act

The Digital Services Act (DSA) is a European Union regulatory framework governing online content. Badlands Media contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] characterizes it as "highly repressive" and cites it as the paradigmatic example of a censorship regime justified by the pretext of protecting people from [[hate-speech|hate speech]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Significance in the essay

In "A Glaring Double Standard," the DSA functions as evidence that the same rationale advanced by American campus activists and, later, by post–October 7 conservatives has already been enacted into law abroad by what DeLarme calls the interagency, international blob.1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) He argues the reasoning is identical across jurisdictions and causes, differing only in which group is designated as requiring protection — "some snowflakes are more precious than others."1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard) The DSA thus stands as the international counterpart to the domestic [[antisemitism-awareness-act|Antisemitism Awareness Act]] and [[executive-order-14188|Executive Order 14188]] in the broader project of [[government-sponsored-censorship|government-sponsored censorship]].1(https://badlands.substack.com/p/a-glaring-double-standard)

## Enforcement against X (October 2023)

Within days of the [[october-7-attacks|October 7 attacks]], the [[european-commission|European Commission]] used the DSA to give [[x-corp|X]] 24 hours to address illegal content and disinformation regarding the conflict or face penalties, then formally opened an investigation into the platform.[2] CEO [[linda-yaccarino|Linda Yaccarino]] replied to EU official [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] in a letter posted to X, saying the company had "redistributed resources and refocused internal teams who are working around the clock," had removed hundreds of Hamas-affiliated accounts and thousands of posts, and had assembled a leadership group to assess the situation.[2] EU officials asked the company to begin responding to investigators as early as October 18.[2]

[[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued that the [[european-union|EU]] was already using the Israel–Palestine conflict as a pretense for ramping up censorship, describing the DSA as the bloc's escalation on the United Kingdom's [[online-safety-bill|Online Safety Bill]] and its unspoken doctrine as one in which "the only free speech you should need is the freedom to agree with the government and the corporatocracy."[2] In the context of [[fifth-generation-warfare|Fifth-Generational Warfare]], he wrote, the tight control of information flow is essential to Western centers of power, and the campaign amounts to an unabashedly totalitarian effort to root out dissent — resting on the assumption that the general population is stupid and that the undiscerning mass will police the fringe before dissenting information can spread.[2]

## Transition from voluntary commitments

When [[twitter|Twitter]] withdrew from the EU's voluntary [[eu-code-of-practice-on-disinformation|Code of Practice on Disinformation]] in late May 2023, Commissioner [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] warned that "beyond voluntary commitments, fighting disinformation will be legal obligation under #DSA as of August 25," and that enforcement teams would be ready.[3] The exchange illustrated the Act's function as the binding successor to the bloc's earlier voluntary content-moderation arrangements.[3]

## First penalties against X (2025)

By April 2025, [[european-union|European Union]] regulators were preparing major penalties against [[x-corp|X]] for breaking the landmark law, in what four people with knowledge of the plans described as a move likely to ratchet up tensions with the United States by targeting one of President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s closest advisers, [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]].[4] The penalties were set to include a fine and demands for product changes, were expected to be announced that summer, and would be the first issued under the Act.[4]

European authorities weighed how large a fine to issue as they considered the risks of further antagonizing Trump amid wider trans-Atlantic disputes over trade, tariffs, and the war in Ukraine; one person said the fine could surpass $1 billion as regulators sought to make an example of X and deter other companies from violating the law.[4] EU officials said the investigation into X was progressing independently from tariff negotiations after Trump announced major new levies that week.[4]

## First formal infringement proceedings (December 2023)

On December 18, 2023, the [[european-union|European Union]] opened infringement proceedings against [[x-corp|X]] over suspected failure to combat content disinformation and manipulation — the first such probe brought under the Digital Services Act.[5] Commissioner [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] said the action responded to suspected breaches of X's transparency obligations, its duties to counter illegal content and disinformation, and what the EU described as a "deceptive" user-interface design relating to the platform's blue check marks.[5]

The [[european-commission|European Commission]] said it acted on the basis of its preliminary investigation, including analysis of the risk assessment report X submitted in September, the platform's transparency report published on November 3, and X's replies to a formal request for information that concerned, among other matters, the dissemination of illegal content in the context of [[hamas|Hamas]]' attacks against Israel.[5] X responded that it "remains committed to complying with the Digital Services Act and is cooperating with the regulatory process," while urging that the process remain "free of political influence."[5] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the proceedings as an attempt by Brussels to suppress the most powerful information-dissemination platform in the world after establishment institutions lost control of it.[5]

## €120 million fine against X (December 2025)

On Friday, December 5, 2025, the [[european-commission|European Commission]] fined [[x-corp|X]] 120 million euros (about $140 million) following a two-year investigation under the Digital Services Act, citing the platform's "deceptive" blue checkmark and a lack of transparency in its advertising repository.[6] [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] responded dismissively on X and the following day escalated, calling for the [[european-union|European Union]] to be abolished "and sovereignty returned to individual countries, so that governments can better represent their people."[6]

Senior U.S. officials joined the criticism: Secretary of State [[marco-rubio|Marco Rubio]] called the fine "an attack on all American tech platforms and the American people by foreign governments."[6] Badlands contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] framed the episode as part of a broader Trump–Musk effort to identify the EU explicitly as the adversary and to challenge its model of narrative control, censorship, and economic interdependence.[6]

## Application to the Israel-Hamas conflict (October 2023)

[[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] returned to the Digital Services Act days after the October 7 attack, writing that the Israel situation would likely lead the [[european-union|European Union]] to demand that [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] censor speech on X.[14] Musk, he wrote, would either comply — shadow banning or removing accounts that question the specific details of the official narrative — or face fines and possibly lose access to all of Europe, a test of whether he is "a hero or a zero."[14]

## The DSA as the censors' remaining instrument (2025 assessment)

Badlands Media's April 2025 analysis argued that with the U.S. portion of the [[censorship-industrial-complex|censorship-industrial complex]] being gutted by the Trump administration, the primary hub for censorship had become centralized within the European Union and NATO, and that of the available legal instruments the Digital Services Act had "the most teeth."[8] The article situated the DSA alongside Canada's [[online-streaming-act|Online Streaming Act]] (Bill C-11) and the UK's [[online-safety-bill|Online Safety Bill]] as the tools catalogued in a pre-2024-election piece on the multinational offensive against free speech.[8]

Fully in effect across the EU since February 17, 2024, the DSA is officially presented as regulating online platforms to enhance "user safety, transparency, and accountability"; Badlands characterized this as a whitewashed mission statement for a regime that imposes "obligations" on digital services, particularly Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) and Very Large Online Search Engines (VLOSEs).[8] The article noted that the law was used to apply pressure to [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] following the October 7 Hamas attack on Israel.[8]

## Entry into force and scope

The Digital Services Act became fully applicable on February 17, 2024, with enforcement ramping up through 2025.[9] It applies to all online platforms operating in the European Union, imposing stricter rules on Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) such as [[x-corp|X]], which has more than 45 million monthly EU users.[9] [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] has been one of the law's primary targets, and X was the first VLOP subjected to a formal investigation.[9]

Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] grouped the DSA with the United Kingdom's [[online-safety-act-2023|Online Safety Act]] and Canada's [[online-streaming-act|Online Streaming Act]] as legal frameworks passed despite widespread opposition — top-down impositions rather than democratic consensus, which in his view undermines their legitimacy.[9] He listed the DSA among the flashpoints of the wider [[censorship-war|censorship war]] and noted that it remained to be seen how proponents such as former commissioner [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] would respond to the American legal counteroffensive against [[alexandre-de-moraes|Alexandre de Moraes]].[9]

## Origins and rollout timeline (Carlson, 2023)

Erik Carlson's September 2023 essay "The Digital Services Act" dates the beginning of the [[european-union|European Union]]'s work on the Act to December 15, 2020, in the middle of the Covid-19 period, and argues that its stated purpose of protecting Europeans from online dangers masked a desire to regain control of a slipping Covid narrative.[11] As public compliance with Covid measures faded, he writes, the effort was placed on the back burner without being abandoned.[11] The Act's advertised targets were online grifters, hate speech and disinformation, with the EU itself reserving the power to define each category, and its scope covered online marketplaces such as [[amazon|Amazon]], social media platforms such as Facebook and [[twitter|Twitter]], and search engines such as Google, beginning with the largest firms before extending to everyone else.[11]

Carlson lays out a chronology he regards as too neatly aligned with [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s acquisition of Twitter: Musk announced his intention to buy the platform on April 14, 2022, and EU policymakers reached agreement on how the DSA would be rolled out and enforced eight days later on April 22, 2022.[11] Musk completed the purchase on October 27, 2022, and DSA rules took effect twenty days later on November 16, 2022.[11] The [[european-commission|European Commission]] defined a "large online company" as one reaching at least 10 percent of Europeans, then some 45 million users; platforms and search engines had to publish user numbers by February 17, 2023; the first designations were made on April 25, 2023; and regulation of the designated large companies began four months later on August 25, 2023.[11] Full application to all companies, large and small, was set for February 17, 2024 — in Carlson's reading, "just in time" for the European and American political seasons.[11]

## Penalties and the compliance calculus

Companies found in violation of the Commission's definitions of hate speech, disinformation or fraud face fines of 6 percent of global revenue, with repeat offenders banned from Europe altogether — an outcome Carlson likens to the way the Chinese Communist Party excludes many online platforms from China.[11] Applying the 6 percent figure to X's projected $3 billion in 2023 global revenue, he calculates an exposure of roughly $180 million per violation.[11] He suggests this financial and market-access pressure may explain why Musk, after handing internal records to journalists exposing [[fbi|FBI]] and [[state-department|State Department]] involvement in Twitter moderation, never delivered on his promise to expose [[anthony-fauci|Dr. Anthony Fauci]] and Big Pharma.[11]

## Compliance posture of X

Citing a Guardian report, Carlson notes that X quit the EU's voluntary code of practice early on but said in June 2023 that it would comply with the rules after a delegation of EU officials led by internal market commissioner [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] visited the company's California headquarters to "stress test" its moderation and compliance systems.[11] Breton welcomed the change of heart while warning there would be "no half measures" on sanctions, said the EU would be extremely vigilant ahead of the 2024 European elections, and stated that he had told Musk and CEO [[linda-yaccarino|Linda Yaccarino]] that "Twitter should be very diligent in preparing to tackle illegal content in the European Union," adding that "fighting disinformation, including pro-Russian propaganda, will also be a focus area."[11]

## Alleged coincidences and predicted outcomes

Carlson catalogues a series of events he considers suspiciously timed around the Act. On August 19, 2023 — days before DSA regulation of large platforms began — the [[open-society-foundations|Soros Open Society Foundations]] announced it would curb its activities across the EU starting in 2024, and shortly afterward [[alex-soros|Alex Soros]] claimed a Trump win in 2024 would be more devastating to globalism and to Europe than to the United States, a statement he later disputed having made.[11] Carlson reads the withdrawal as a signal that Europe is now "under control," and agrees that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] threatens Europe — specifically the [[city-of-london|City of London]] and the [[british-cabal|British Cabal]] — while posing no threat to the American people.[11] Three days after the regulations took effect, Trump declared in all caps that [[truth-social|Truth Social]] was "the greatest & 'hottest' form, system & platform of communication in America, & indeed the world, today," which Carlson interprets as a signal.[11] He also notes that the day after enforcement began, word emerged that [[tucker-carlson|Tucker Carlson]] — who had been using X to reach a large audience — was interested in hosting [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]].[11]

Carlson forecasts three possible outcomes: "truthers" banned from X and [[amazon-web-services|AWS]]; "truthers" shadow banned; or X and Amazon permitting unmanipulated speech and consequently facing massive fines and a possible European blackout.[11] He anticipates a communication blackout resembling October 2020, when accounts were removed for questioning masks, Covid vaccines, Ivermectin and Hydroxychloroquine, the authenticity of the Hunter Biden laptop, or the election results, and argues the key difference now is that Truth Social — outside DSA scope until February 2024 — and [[rumble|Rumble]] operate their own cloud infrastructure.[11]

## As a European safe haven for the Deep State (September 2024)

Revisiting the subject in September 2024, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] noted that he had written about the Digital Services Act a year earlier and that it had since returned to the news cycle.[12] He described England as cracking down on anything it deems offensive online, including claims that it would extradite people to face charges and punishment there — a threat he dismissed as intimidation, arguing that an Iowa farmer who "likes" a tweet about men competing in women's sports might be harassed by the [[fbi|FBI]] but will not be taken away by [[mi5|MI5]].[12]

He grouped the arrest of the [[telegram|Telegram]] CEO in France with the DSA regime as "a clear fascist suppression of free speech," and observed that [[alexander-vindman|Alexander Vindman]] had warned [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] he could be next.[12] Because the United States is a Facebook and X country, Carlson argued, shutting Telegram down would not badly disrupt the US elections, but Telegram is much larger in European countries; combined with the possibility that neither Musk's nor [[mark-zuckerberg|Zuckerberg]]'s platform would be permitted in Europe if both backed [[donald-trump|Trump]], the effect would be complete European isolation from the truth.[12]

Carlson's conclusion was that England and Europe are using the Act to insulate the continent and create a safe space where the [[deep-state|Deep State]] can exist after Trump retakes power in the United States, and he predicted the next big push would be to make extradition from Europe far more difficult so that the network can avoid prosecution elsewhere.[12]

## Aggressive clauses and the campaign against X (DeLarme analysis, 2024)

Writing in October 2024, [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] described the Digital Services Act as the legal framework used by EU official [[thierry-breton|Thierry Breton]] to target [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] and [[x-corp|X]] over the preceding year.[13] He characterized several of its clauses as deliberately aggressive: Section 10 detailing the crimes of transmitting "harmful, false and threatening communications" while refusing to define clearly what counts as "harmful" or by what criteria something is judged "false," and clause 156 making it a crime to send a message containing false information.[13] In the next breath, he wrote, clause 157 grants immunity to every newspaper, television channel and streaming service.[13]

DeLarme concluded that under such a regime only information and opinions deemed appropriate by what he called the International Corpo-Government amalgam would be allowed to see the light of day, and that this is plainly stated in print for anyone willing to make a modicum of effort to understand what the [[european-union|EU]] is foisting upon them.[13] He grouped the DSA with Canada's C-11 [[online-streaming-act|Online Streaming Act]] and the UK's [[online-safety-bill|Online Safety Bill]] as prongs of a single multinational censorship campaign, and warned that if the EU concluded views permitted on X constituted criminal disinformation, Musk and X itself could be held criminally liable — the precedent the [[pavel-durov|Durov]] indictment was designed to establish.[13]
