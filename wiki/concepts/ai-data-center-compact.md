# AI Data Center Compact

The AI Data Center Compact is a draft voluntary agreement reported in February 2026 under which the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration would seek public commitments from the world's largest technology companies regarding the energy and water footprint of their artificial intelligence data centers.[1]

## Reporting

On Monday, February 9, 2026, *Politico* reported that two administration officials granted anonymity to discuss private conversations said the administration was courting major technology firms to publicly commit to the compact.[1] *Politico* claimed to have seen a draft and described commitments designed to ensure that energy-hungry data centers do not raise household electricity prices, strain water supplies, or undermine grid reliability — and to ensure that the companies driving the demand also carry the cost of building the new infrastructure required to serve it.[1]

Badlands commentary described the compact as targeting the Big Tech titans specifically — [[openai|OpenAI]], [[microsoft|Microsoft]], Google, [[amazon|Amazon]] and Meta — and as forcing them to bear 100% of infrastructure costs: new power generation, transmission upgrades, and long-term contracts to shield residential ratepayers from price spikes, alongside water-positivity mandates, grid reliability commitments, and community mitigation and planning obligations.[1] The design was characterized as preempting an energy crunch as data center demand threatens — or, in the contributor's framing, promises — to triple by 2028.[1]

## Narrative context

The compact surfaced on the same day as the administration's full repeal of the Obama-era [[endangerment-finding|endangerment finding]], described in Badlands coverage as the largest act of deregulation in American history.[1] Contributor [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the surface-level narrative fixated on the rollback while the compact and related "counter-deployments" went largely unnoticed.[1]

A related *Politico* report published the same week, "Poll shows Democrats hold edge over Trump in energy cost battle," found that 37% of US adults believed Democrats care more about protecting them from spiking natural gas, heating and utility bills, versus 25% who said Republicans care more.[1] Burning Bright read the poll as a narrative deployment against Trump's domestic energy acceleration agenda, framing an abundance push as the cause of a scarcity model he argues was deliberately engineered, and painting a fossil-fuel transitional push and nuclear renaissance as the culprits behind rising bills rather than the decentralized, sovereign solution to them.[1]

Separately, the *Wall Street Journal* published an opinion piece titled "Even Trump Can't Go Back to the Future," arguing that structural shifts in US energy — rising electricity demand driven partly by data centers and AI, and a 15-year move toward natural gas and renewables driven by cheap gas and plant retirements rather than regulation alone — limit how far executive action can reverse current trends.[1]

## Commentary on precedent

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that requiring corporations to enter compacts "feels right" in an age where corporations are more powerful than nation-states, particularly technology companies.[1] She argued that AI is disrupting every industry, that firms harnessing it intelligently will leapfrog competitors while those chasing shiny objects will waste money and become irrelevant, and that AI companies profit from both outcomes — which is precisely why infrastructure is required.[1] She characterized Trump's position as insisting that planned growth be centrally planned, which is why he seeks to prohibit states from acting independently while luring "corporate countries" into what amount to treaties.[1] While saying she hated the precedent, she judged the premise sound: allowing companies to define the future without accounting for the interests of and impacts on people is how the "America last" dystopia arose.[1] She invoked the way solar fields reshaped the landscape as a cautionary parallel to AI's potential effects.[1]
