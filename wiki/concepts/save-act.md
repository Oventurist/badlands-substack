# SAVE Act

The SAVE Act, designated H.R. 22, is federal legislation that would require documentary proof of U.S. citizenship to register to vote in federal elections.[1]

## Legislative history

According to Congress.gov, the House of Representatives passed the bill 220–208 on 10 April 2025 and sent it to the Senate the same day.[1]

In February 2026 the [[congress|US House of Representatives]] again passed the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act, requiring documentary proof of US citizenship — such as a passport or birth certificate — to register to vote in federal elections.[2] House Republicans backed the measure as an election-integrity step aimed at preventing noncitizen voting, while House Democrats opposed it on the grounds that it could create barriers for eligible voters who lack ready access to documentation.[2] The bill was described as facing significant opposition in the [[united-states-senate|US Senate]], where it would need bipartisan support to overcome a filibuster.[2]

## Commentary

Writing in the [[badlands-brief|Badlands Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the SAVE Act "a good piece of legislation," arguing that proving citizenship before voting is basic and that opponents were "stupid or evil."[2] She nonetheless argued that good legislation passed by what she called illegitimate lawmakers who refuse to acknowledge their own legitimacy problem "will not save the nation," describing the effort as "a rug."[2] She noted that Republicans in [[congress|Congress]] had promised for years to save elections with the SAVE Act despite holding full control of government for over a year, and concluded that there can be no real elections until there is accountability for fraudulent past ones — accountability she doubted the beneficiaries of those elections would pursue.[2]

## Public opinion

[[rasmussen-reports|Rasmussen Reports]] said a 8–10 March 2026 survey of 1,098 likely U.S. voters found 63% supported the SAVE Act and 32% opposed it; the firm also published question wording for a 10–12 May 2026 election-integrity survey of 1,060 likely voters.[1]

## Relationship to administrative verification

The legislative push paralleled executive action under the [[save-program|SAVE program]], through which the Trump administration ran at least 67 million voter registrations through [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]] systems, with [[uscis|USCIS]] reporting that 60 million checks identified about 24,000 potential noncitizens.[1] Critics warned the checks could remove valid voters from the rolls before the November elections.[1]

## Role in the February 2026 shutdown negotiations

During the partial government shutdown that began on January 31, 2026, a bloc of House Republicans sought to attach the SAVE Act — which would require proof of US citizenship to register to vote in federal elections — to the Senate-passed spending package.[3] The approach was opposed by Democrats and had not been included in the Senate bill, and Democratic leaders rejected adding it, pressing instead for changes to immigration enforcement policy including unmasking federal agents, ending roving patrols, and increasing oversight of [[department-of-homeland-security|DHS]] operations.[3] President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] urged House Republicans to pass the Senate measure as written so the government could reopen, leaving the SAVE Act's inclusion the central open question for Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]]; see [[january-2026-government-shutdown-standoff|the January 2026 government shutdown standoff]].[3]


## Renewed calls, June 2026

After late mail ballots reversed the election-night result in the [[2026-los-angeles-mayoral-election|2026 Los Angeles mayoral race]], President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and congressional Republicans used the episode to renew calls for the SAVE Act and other measures requiring proof of citizenship and stricter voter identification.[4] Administration officials pointed to California's multi-week counting process and the [[red-mirage|Red Mirage]] pattern as evidence of potential fraud, while the state's top federal prosecutor in Los Angeles said his office had multiple election-fraud investigations underway.[4]

## Senate debate over married women (April 2025)

As the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act headed to the Senate in April 2025, debate grew over how the bill might affect voting by millions of married women, particularly those who have changed their last names.[5] The legislation, introduced in response to fears about voter fraud, seeks sweeping reforms to voter registration, most notably a requirement to prove US citizenship before registering.[5] Opponents argued the measure amounted to voter suppression; Democratic Representative [[ilhan-omar|Ilhan Omar]] of Minnesota wrote on social media that "This voter suppression bill will disenfranchise millions of voters, especially married women."[5]

Badlands commentary offered two responses. One held that eliminating the secret ballot would remove the need for voter ID altogether, since people would then be accountable for voting while ineligible.[5] The other argued that critics such as Omar raise married women because the SAVE Act has the potential to identify and prosecute fake voters, and because the public broadly supports the principle that only eligible voters should vote.[5] That commentary contended that keeping illegal, dead, fabricated or otherwise ineligible voters on the rolls sustains an election fraud machine, that long-promised government eligibility checks have repeatedly been shown not to occur meaningfully, and that field registration drives by groups such as Rock the Vote and figures like Michele Obama and Scott Presler do not verify eligibility.[5] It concluded that after years of relaxed registration controls the establishment is watching its machine be exposed and dismantled, leaving "think of the married women!" as its only acceptable argument — from the same actors, the writer said, who demanded vaccine papers to buy food.[5]
