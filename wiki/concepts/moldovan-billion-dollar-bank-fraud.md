# Moldovan Billion Dollar Bank Fraud

The Moldovan billion-dollar bank fraud, popularly known as the "theft of the century," was a scheme carried out between 2011 and 2014 in which roughly $1 billion — representing about one-eighth of [[moldova|Moldova]]'s GDP — was stolen from the country.[1] It was orchestrated by the oligarch [[vlad-plahotniuc|Vlad Plahotniuc]] working closely with Israeli-Moldovan businessman [[ilan-shor|Ilan Shor]] and fellow oligarchs [[vyacheslav-platon|Vyacheslav Platon]] and [[vlad-filat|Vlad Filat]].[1]

## Mechanism

The model was simple and based on the use of offshore shell companies, which laundered money and received fraudulent loans issued through the Moldovan Central Bank.[1] That bank had itself taken over three major financial institutions — Banca de Economii, Unibank and Banca Sociala — which extended fraudulent loans to businesses controlled by Ilan Shor and others within Plahotniuc's network totalling roughly $1 billion.[1] Some $100 million was used directly by Plahotniuc to purchase banks, businesses, hotels and other luxury real estate around Europe and Moldova.[1]

## Accountability

Although the facts of the fraud could not long be kept secret, Plahotniuc's control over the levers of justice — especially the Constitutional Court, packed with his partisans — ensured that little could be done to bring him to justice.[1] Filat was sentenced in 2016 to three years in prison for bribes and participation in the heist; Shor was charged with laundering and embezzlement in absentia but never saw a jail cell; and Platon was found guilty in April 2017 and given 18 years.[1] Plahotniuc never faced a single charge in Moldova, though Russian courts convicted him of attempted murder in 2017 and of drug trafficking and money laundering in 2019.[1]

The scandal devastated public confidence in a nation ranked lowest on the European Union poverty index, where nearly 50% of the male population lives and works abroad as cheap labour; Plahotniuc's disapproval rating reached 96%, and the [[democratic-party-of-moldova|Democratic Party]] lost power to the Socialists under [[igor-dodon|Igor Dodon]].[1]

## Scale and consequences

Contemporary accounts placed the sum stolen at $1 billion, variously described as 15% or 12% of Moldova's GDP, drained from the country's three largest banks in late 2014.[2] The Open Dialogue Foundation called the theft "a real shock to the country and its citizens," noting that within a few months the national currency depreciated by 42%, producing a currency crisis, and that as details emerged it became clear the former regime was directly involved in looting the banking system.[2] Kroll's investigation found the central bank money was funneled into three banks majority-controlled by [[ilan-shor|Ilan Shor]] — Sociala, Unibank and Banca de Economii, then the nation's largest — with reserves falling from $2.8 billion to $1.8 billion in under three years.[2] The heist collapsed two pro-European coalition governments before [[vlad-plahotniuc|Plahotniuc]] formed a ruling coalition under prime minister Pavel Filip in February 2016.[2] Analysts stressed the theft was less a cause than a symptom of a longer crisis — of a system that made such a theft possible in the first place.[2]

## Arrests

Former prime minister [[vlad-filat|Vlad Filat]] was arrested in parliament on October 15, 2015 by masked officers of Moldova's anti-corruption bureau after lawmakers stripped his immunity at the prosecutor's request, with thousands camped out in the capital in protest; he later served a nine-year term for his role in the theft of National Bank reserves.[2] Badlands writer Joe Lange stresses that Filat was the same prime minister who had stood beside [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] in January 2010 to accept a $262 million [[millennium-challenge-corporation|Millennium Challenge Corporation]] compact, and that the theft occurred in 2012–2014, roughly two years after that grant and its USAID-co-financed "innovative financing facility."[2]

## Alleged Libya link

A former chief investigator at Moldova's National Anti-Corruption Center, a member of the Moneyval and Egmont Group anti-money-laundering bodies, told Forbes that "Gadafi money was funneled into European banks through Moldova," and that Moldova had become a conduit of international money laundering in 2009.[2] Lange builds on this to argue that gold missing from [[libya|Libya]] after 2011 was laundered through Moldova, and that the [[podesta-group|Podesta Group]] — retained by Plahotniuc's party for $600,000 in July 2016 — served as the bridge between that operation and Washington; these are the author's inferences rather than documented findings.[2]
