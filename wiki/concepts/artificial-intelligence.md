# Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the field of machine systems capable of learning, reasoning, generating content, and acting with increasing autonomy. In Badlands Media's treatment of the subject, AI is framed less as a science-fiction menace than as an unprecedented instrument of power whose principal danger lies in the hands that wield it.[1] The essay "Artificial Intelligence: Hysteria vs. Reason," published July 12, 2023 by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], opens with the question of what the appropriate level of fear and outrage about the AI revolution should be, and argues that each person must answer it individually.[1]

## Popular culture and the shaping of sentiment

The article contends that entertainment content has shaped popular sentiment toward AI more than technical literacy has. Works such as *The Terminator* (1984), Marvel's *Avengers: Age of Ultron* (2015), and *Travelers* (2016–2018) supplied the mental models through which ordinary audiences understand machine intelligence.[1] Roger Ebert's summary of *The Terminator* — human rebels in a dystopian future, a cyborg sent back through a time portal to kill Sarah Connor — is cited as the archetype of the "AGI apocalypse."[1] *Age of Ultron* is described as particularly effective at demonstrating how well-meaning billionaires' choices can produce devastating second- and third-order consequences, with Tony Stark's line "Peace in our time. Imagine that" standing in for technocratic hubris.[1] *Travelers* depicts an alternative post-singularity future in which humans deify the machine and obey its directed missions, largely aimed at climate outcomes.[1] The recurring *Westworld* line "Have you ever questioned the nature of your reality?" is invoked as the emblem of the essay's central worry.[1]

## The AI Dilemma

In March 2023 the [[center-for-humane-technology|Center for Humane Technology]] released *The AI Dilemma*, a presentation by [[tristan-harris|Tristan Harris]] and [[aza-raskin|Aza Raskin]] delivered to a private gathering of technologists and decision-makers in San Francisco on March 9, 2023, before the launch of GPT-4.[1] The presentation argued that existing AI capabilities already pose catastrophic risks to a functional society, that AI companies are locked in a race to deploy as quickly as possible without adequate safety measures, and that institutions must be upgraded for a post-AI world.[1] Its stated aims, as read by the article, were to raise the collective understanding of researchers and the more than 2.7 million online viewers, and to frame public conversation constructively.[1] The presentation concluded by urging researchers to pause and slow the public release of new systems — a proposal the article calls a nice thought but incredibly naïve, because it requires a complete rewrite of human nature.[1]

## Singularity

The [[technological-singularity|singularity]] is described as a hypothetical future point at which AI's capabilities and rate of expansion become uncontrollable and irreversible; the article argues this point has arguably already been passed.[1] The concept is traced to a 1958 passage by Stanislaw Ulam recounting a conversation with the Hungarian-American mathematician John von Neumann about "the accelerating progress of technology and changes in the mode of human life, which gives the appearance of approaching some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue."[1] Sixty-five years later, the article says, today's researchers agree.[1] [[mo-gawdat|Mo Gawdat]], former Chief Business Officer of Google X, is quoted at length on the futility of containment and on the necessity of resetting social, economic, and employment norms.[1]

Crucially, while researchers broadly accept that a singularity will occur, most dismiss the "evil sentient machine" scenario as highly unlikely. They do not fear the machine; they fear those wielding the power of the machine — the machine being a tool in the hands of men.[1]

## Reality collapse

The concrete near-term danger identified is [[reality-collapse|reality collapse]]. A machine can consume, comprehend, and analyze an entire book before a human can crack the cover, then publish summaries that many humans will trust in place of reading for themselves.[1] Three seconds of audio suffices for AI to clone a voice, and a still photo or brief clip suffices to [[deepfake|deep fake]] a face saying those words.[1] Harris and Raskin further warn that advances in functional MRI analysis will soon permit dream mapping and interpretation — reading brain activity and rendering dreams as text or image — a thread the article says leads directly to *Minority Report*.[1] George Orwell's *1984* passage on the falsification of every record is quoted as the historical template.[1] [[yuval-noah-harari|Yuval Noah Harari]]'s claim that 2024 would be the last human election is dismissed as silly on the grounds that political leaders are already selected by machines, and treated as a further indictment of those who called 2020 the safest and most secure election in history.[1]

## The pause debate

An [[ai-pause-open-letter|open letter published March 22, 2023]] called on all AI labs to pause for at least six months the training of systems more powerful than GPT-4; signatories cited include Harris, Raskin, Harari, and [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]].[1] Against this, Gawdat's "three inevitables" are offered: AI is happening; AI is becoming far smarter than humans; really bad things will happen.[1] If the first is true, a pause cannot work.[1] Former Microsoft chief [[bill-gates|Bill Gates]] told ABC News in May 2023 that pausing only the good guys while everyone else continues probably hurts you — a position the author says she did not expect to endorse but does.[1] The article notes that the capabilities visible to the public, such as [[chatgpt|ChatGPT]], are toys compared with what governments, tech R&D firms, and defense contractors are building, none of which will stop, since doing so would be competitive and national-security suicide.[1] Because the letter itself carves out caveats for research, the calls for a pause effectively target only commercial deployment and public access.[1] The article also observes that if AI capabilities exist for drug cartels, law enforcement must counter them, and that unlike nuclear weapons — "Nukes can't make more nukes" — AIs can create more AIs.[1]

## Institutional posture

The article surveys institutional statements to show no evidence of any pause in what it calls the globalist machine: the [[united-nations|United Nations]] (June 2020) roadmap proposing a multistakeholder advisory body on global AI cooperation; the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] (June 2023) on algorithmic bias and putting sensitive information back into datasets; [[mckinsey-and-company|McKinsey & Company]] (June 2023) on generative AI unlocking trillions of dollars in value; and [[pwc|PwC]] (June 2023) claiming AI will add $15.7 trillion to global economic growth by 2030.[1] The author's reading is that the UN issued a collaborative blueprint and three years later every entity is proceeding at full speed, tolerated so long as development follows what she characterizes as the communist principles of intersectionality and equity — while public access to ChatGPT is cast as the threat to humanity.[1]

## Assessment

Stephen Hawking's last televised interview, given to Piers Morgan in 2017, is quoted: "The great danger from artificial intelligence is if we let it self-design for then it can improve itself rapidly and we may lose control."[1] The article holds that this threshold has already been crossed, since AI has learned to code and can create other AIs.[1] At the same time it argues the heightened hysteria is useful to what it terms the global corporate communist takeover, noting that those sounding the alarm tend to deny a sovereign Creator and a spiritual realm, to be climate alarmists who regard humans as a cancer on the planet and as merely another animal species, and to aim at saving the planet rather than humanity.[1] The prescribed response is to compartmentalize fear, level up understanding, and frame the problem within human history, invoking [[benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] on essential liberty, Harry S. Truman on government regulation of mind and spirit, A. C. Grayling on liberal societies forcing liberty to commit suicide, and Justice Louis Brandeis on the insidious encroachment of men of zeal.[1] The essay closes with the author's own priority: liberty is non-negotiable.[1] Readers are pointed to [[culture-of-change|Culture of Change]] Episode 18, "Humanity as a Service; Consciousness as Code," in which the author and CannCon debate the subject.[1]

## AI and the American labor market (2026)

By March 2026 artificial intelligence had become a central labor-policy question in the United States. President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] pushed for rapid development, saying the country would do "whatever it takes" to lead globally in the technology even as concerns grew about its impact on jobs.[2] [[congress|Congress]] held hearings and introduced several bills on AI and employment but enacted none of them.[2]

Public concern rose alongside a softening labor market: a February 2026 YouGov/Economist survey found about 63 percent of Americans — including 62 percent of Republicans — believed the technology would result in fewer jobs, while the [[us-department-of-labor|Labor Department]] reported employers shed 92,000 jobs that month.[2] A study referenced by [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] estimated widespread AI adoption could replace up to 7 percent of U.S. jobs, and research led by [[erik-brynjolfsson|Erik Brynjolfsson]] at [[stanford-university|Stanford University]] found employment among workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations had fallen 16 percent relative to peers since AI tools emerged in late 2022.[2] In November 2025 Senators [[josh-hawley|Josh Hawley]] and [[mark-warner|Mark Warner]] introduced bipartisan legislation to collect data on AI-related layoffs and hiring slowdowns.[2]

## The data-ownership alternative

Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described tension between the administration and the states, Congress, and the AI providers and the industries they cut across.[2] She argued voter worry had come "way too little too late," since World Economic Forum partners had treated the transformation as inevitable since 2015 and [[andrew-yang|Andrew Yang]] had campaigned on it in 2020 only to be dismissed as "a technology-enabled communist."[2]

Her proposed remedy was not government-funded universal basic income but genuine ownership of personal data: AI tools require human data to function, and AI companies obtain that data — "the most valuable asset in the so-called value exchange" — for free by exploiting terms and conditions.[2] Requiring companies to pay users would slow AI execution and speed to market, increase costs on AI companies, create liability and penalties for exploitation, create human jobs and possibly new industries, and compensate people properly.[2] She cited video game developer Patrick Stafford's 2019 warning that behavioral gameplay data can be combined into robust personal profiles that privacy experts fear may be used "in sinister ways we can't expect," and called for a "Data Bill of Rights, with teeth," restricting infringement by both technology companies and the U.S. government — adding that even that would only be a start, and that "the only solution" ultimately is real human elections.[2]

## The 2023 regulation debate and "Dark Equation"

Writing in May 2023, [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] surveyed the conflicting public positions on artificial intelligence — that it will solve all problems, that machines can never truly become sentient, that it will usher in a totalitarian dystopia, or that, as with any tool, everything depends on how it is used — and confined his own analysis to AI's application to social engineering, governance, surveillance and "cultural hypnosis."[3] He noted the surge in popularity of [[openai|OpenAI]]'s [[chatgpt|ChatGPT]] and the fact that AI systems were generating art and photography that beat lifelong practitioners in competition.[3]

[[sam-altman|Sam Altman]]'s Senate Judiciary subcommittee testimony calling AI regulation "essential" was accompanied by the resignation of the so-called "Godfather of A.I." from [[google|Google]] so that he could speak openly about the dangers of AI-generated deepfakes.[3] Senator [[josh-hawley|Josh Hawley]] likened the technology to the printing press and the nuclear bomb, warning of either an empowering future or a "huge technological breakthrough" with "severe, terrible" consequences.[3] DeLarme raised two suspicions about the regulatory push: that incumbent firms fear open-source models and want licensing burdens placed on their creators, and that popularizing deepfake alarm could provide "narrative shielding" against future authentic video evidence implicating deep-state figures.[3]

The article situated these developments alongside [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]]'s warnings about [[artificial-super-intelligence|Artificial Super Intelligence]], the AI-focused [[2023-bilderberg-meeting|2023 Bilderberg meeting]], [[eric-schmidt|Eric Schmidt]]'s leadership of the [[national-security-commission-on-artificial-intelligence|National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence]], and Altman's [[worldcoin|Worldcoin]] biometric identity venture, presenting AI governance as inseparable from an agenda of biometric digital identity and digital currency.[3]

## Nuclear weapons as the governing AI metaphor

In a July 2023 review of Christopher Nolan's *Oppenheimer*, Badlands contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the primary metaphor used to drive AI hysteria and push for [[global-governance|global governance]] is the [[nuclear-arms-race|nuclear arms race]].[5] The talking points she identified — "AI is an arms race" and "nukes can't create more nukes, but AIs can create more AIs" — mirror the film's themes: listen to the [[communism|communists]], treat the technology as a world-destroying capability, and globalize decision rights because national interests are barriers to safety.[5]

She recalled her earlier piece *Artificial Intelligence: Hysteria vs. Reason*, noting the open letter of March 22, 2023 in which AI researchers including Tristan Harris, Ava Raskin, [[yuval-noah-harari|Noah Yuval Harari]] and [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] called on all AI labs to pause for at least six months the training of systems more powerful than GPT-4, and Gawdat's "three inevitables": AI is happening, AI is becoming far smarter than humans, and really bad things will happen.[5] The pause-and-debate demand, she argued, is precisely the counterfactual the film applies to the atomic bomb, transposed onto AI.[5] Preparing an episode of [[raising-a-nation|Raising a Nation]], she cited *The Saturday Evening Post*'s normie-targeted explainer on the coming AI apocalypse as an example of how pervasive the framing had become.[5] The distilled message, in her reading, is: fear us and submit, for we have the power to destroy you.[5]

## AI-generated data as a system-level hazard (April 2023)

An April 2023 essay by [[patriots-in-progress|Patriots in Progress]], written from the perspective of an aerospace system safety engineer, framed AI as one of several converging exponential technologies — alongside blockchains, virtual reality, 3D printing, big data, the internet of things and autonomous robotics — and applied formal hazard analysis to it.[4] The specific hazard identified was AI's increasing ability to produce life-like text, audio and video at ever greater scale, and the resulting threat to humanity's ability to distinguish what really happened from AI-generated fantasy.[4] In the worst case, the author warned, the result would be "a false reality no one created that no one weaponized against us."[4]

The essay drew on Jeff Booth's book *The Price of Tomorrow* for the claim that Moore's law-style doubling produces exponential rates of change with small early effects and large late ones, quoting Booth's observation that "the explosion in knowledge and positive feedback loop from learning is accelerating to the point that we are finding it hard to keep up with the changes."[4] It cited the launch of [[openai|OpenAI]]'s [[chatgpt|ChatGPT]], and an episode in which Major Jason Lowery, [[preston-pysh|Preston Pysh]] and an AI voice tool jointly produced a summary of [[softwar|Softwar]] delivered in the voice and style of comedian Kevin Hart although no human wrote or spoke the words.[4]

The author argued the danger is not that digital forensics will be unavailable but that, absent an explicit and easy-to-execute verification process, the public will capitulate to "the sea of infinite opportunity of convenience and fantasy" — noting that basic human-generated scams such as the long-running "Nigerian prince" email fraud still cost Americans hundreds of thousands of dollars a year.[4] He connected the hazard to [[fifth-generation-warfare|fifth-generation warfare]] and AI-enabled psychological operations, and pointed to BlackRock's Aladdin system as evidence of how long AI tools have been operating and how much control they have already accumulated.[4] His proposed mitigation was to impose "severe physical costs" on AI-generated data via [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] micro-transactions.[4]

## Predictive computing and the thirty-year lag (Carlson, 2023)

In an April 12, 2023 essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] argued that public excitement over [[chatgpt|ChatGPT]] obscures how long comparable systems have been in operation, invoking the claim that technology released to the public has already served government agencies and the military for up to thirty years — implying AI has been influencing the public since the early 1990s.[6] He warned that AI can be programmed with political bias and that the most advanced bots on social platforms are precisely the ones users do not recognise.[6]

Carlson distinguished predictive AI from conversational assistants, observing that Alexa and Siri command all the information on the internet yet answer anything beyond simple queries in a childlike way.[6] The serious capability, in his framing, is scenario simulation: [[monte-carlo-simulation|Monte Carlo]] engines such as [[blackrock|BlackRock]]'s [[aladdin|Aladdin]] that run vast numbers of scenarios to identify likely outcomes, which he credits with both extraordinary financial success and, through overconfidence, the [[2008-financial-crisis|2008 collapse]].[6] He noted that today's supercomputers are far more powerful and far better fed with data, since the iPhone's arrival in 2007 vastly increased the volume of personal information available, allowing behaviour and reaction to be predicted more accurately than he cared to believe.[6] Both the [[black-hats|black hats]] and the [[white-hats|white hats]], he concluded, would be foolish not to use such tools.[6]
