# "Voters Strike Back: Litigating against Modern Voter Intimidation"

"Voters Strike Back: Litigating against Modern Voter Intimidation" is a 2015 publication of the NYU Social Justice Law Review identified by defendants in [[naacp-v-useip|*NAACP et al. v. USEIP et al.*]] as the origin of the plaintiffs' novel legal theory; the plaintiffs' expert witness cited it in his official report.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

The article advances two central claims: that Section 11(b) of the [[voting-rights-act-of-1965|Voting Rights Act of 1965]] "was a deliberate attempt to expand the existing laws against voter intimidation, including by eliminating any legal requirement of racial targeting"; and that "today… voters are deterred from voting through subtler tactics, such as aggressive poll-watching, anonymous threats of harm, frivolous and excessive voter registration challenges, and coercion by employers."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)

It also offers strategic advice that critics regard as objectively unethical and closely mirrored by the plaintiffs' conduct: bringing a [[ku-klux-klan-act-of-1871|KKK Act]] claim "for the purpose of political messaging," using the Act to "brand" a ballot-security group or party committee with "deeply embarrassing associations" in order to "help drive settlement," and defining success as "stopping the defendant's conduct."[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back) At trial, the lone plaintiff representative to testify admitted that stopping the defendants' conduct was the overriding goal.[[1]](https://badlands.substack.com/p/always-fight-back)
