# Natural Law – The Science of Justice

**"Natural Law – The Science of Justice"** is an essay by [[lysander-spooner|Lysander Spooner]] that serves as the principal text discussed in the Badlands Media article "On Nations and Natural Law" (5 October 2025) and in the associated programme [[no-treason-podcast|The No Treason Podcast]].[1]

## Argument

The essay defines its subject as "the science of mine and thine — the science of justice," which Spooner identifies with the science of all human rights: of a man's rights of person and property, and of his rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness.[1] It is, he writes, the science which alone can tell any man what he can and cannot do, have or say without infringing the rights of another, and it is likewise "the science of peace," the only science that can state the conditions on which mankind can live in peace with one another.[1]

Those conditions are twofold: that each man do toward every other all that justice requires — paying debts, returning borrowed or stolen property, making reparation for injuries — and that each man abstain from what justice forbids, including theft, robbery, arson and murder.[1] From these Spooner derives three fundamental legal rights, which are simultaneously duties: to be dealt with honestly, not to be intentionally and undeservingly hurt, and to be given what one is due, summarised in the ancient maxim "to live honestly, to hurt no one, and to give everyone his due."[1]

## Evidence and method

Spooner supports the existence of justice as a natural principle by observing that children understand its rudiments before they can name them and independently of instruction, and that men must know sentiments and ideas before they can know the words describing them.[1] He then advances fifteen "If/Then" propositions: if justice is not a natural principle, every appeal and struggle for justice in history has been for a mere fantasy, and there can be no such thing as injustice, all crimes becoming mere events like the falling of rain.[1] Justice being part of nature, it is immutable and cannot be changed by any inferior power, so that attempts by men "whether calling themselves governments, or by any other name" to substitute their own will for justice constitute usurpation and tyranny.[1] Without such a principle the world would be an "abyss of moral darkness."[1]

## Reception

The Badlands Media essay recommends the remainder of the work as "well worth the read," situating it alongside the wider libertarian literature and the writings of [[murray-rothbard|Murray Rothbard]].[1]
