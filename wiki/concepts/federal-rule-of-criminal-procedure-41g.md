# Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(g)

Rule 41(g) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure provides a mechanism by which a "person aggrieved" by an unlawful search and seizure, or by the deprivation of property, may move for the return of property or for restrictions on its use. Because the rule does not require the movant to challenge the validity of the underlying warrant, it can be used to seek protective conditions rather than suppression.

## Use in the Fulton County records dispute

The February 17, 2026 [[badlands-brief-0a2|Badlands Brief]] reports that the [[naacp|NAACP]] and other non-governmental organizations invoked Rule 41(g) in an emergency motion filed in the US District Court for the Northern District of Georgia, seeking court-imposed restrictions on how the [[fbi|FBI]] and the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] may use election materials seized from [[fulton-county|Fulton County]] in January during the [[fulton-county-election-raid|Fulton County election records seizure]].

The filing does not directly challenge the validity of the search warrant. Instead, the plaintiffs argue that Fulton County voters hold protectable privacy and constitutional interests in their personal voter data — including Social Security numbers, dates of birth, driver's license numbers, and ballot-related information — and therefore qualify as "persons aggrieved" within the meaning of the rule. This theory extends the rule beyond its typical application to the owner of seized property, reaching individuals whose personal information is contained within records held by a government body.

The relief sought would confine federal use of the materials to the criminal investigation described in the warrant, prohibiting use for national voter roll programs, immigration enforcement, unrelated civil enforcement, interagency data sharing, compilation of voter databases, and public disclosure. The plaintiffs also request access controls limiting review to personnel assigned to the investigation, a detailed inventory of seized materials, disclosure of who has accessed the records, and a protective order governing storage and handling. They assert both individual and associational [[legal-standing|standing]], the latter on the theory that the seizure undermines voter trust and diverts organizational resources.
