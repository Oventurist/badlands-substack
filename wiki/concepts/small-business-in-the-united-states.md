# Small Business in the United States

Small businesses constitute the overwhelming majority of American enterprise. In 2022 there were 33.2 million small businesses in the United States, roughly 99.9% of all US businesses.[1] Most American companies are not publicly traded on the stock market; most are private.[1]

## Central to the "market is not the economy" argument

This statistical fact anchors the argument advanced by Badlands Media contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] that the stock market is not the economy and the economy is not the stock market, because 99.9% of US businesses are not part of the stock market at all.[1] When the [[sp-500|S&P 500]] fell 35% in March 2020 and largely recovered within six months, Carlson argued that what had recovered was only the perceived value of the 0.1% of businesses that are listed.[1]

## Damage from the lockdowns

During the [[covid-19-lockdowns|COVID lockdowns]], small private businesses were forced to close while large corporate stores remained open, chiefly because grocery sales conferred essential status on chains such as [[target|Target]], [[walmart|Walmart]] and [[costco|Costco]].[1] Restaurants without drive-throughs or take-out — disproportionately privately owned, family-run establishments — were essentially unable to stay afloat, while publicly traded corporate chains with drive-throughs captured their spending.[1] Businesses with an online presence, notably [[amazon|Amazon]], took large market share from small brick-and-mortar competitors, whose sales were "sucked up by the online retail conglomerate."[1]

Carlson estimated that roughly a third of small businesses did not or would not survive the lockdowns and their aftermath, with some still suffering lasting damage amid a further economic downturn.[1] The consequence, in his framing, is that large corporate businesses now hold an even bigger share of the market, having "basically ate their competition."[1]

## Interpretation

Carlson characterized this consolidation as a concentration of power and wealth, representative of a dying middle class and the beginning of a two-class system dividing rich from poor.[1]
