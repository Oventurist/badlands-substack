# The Patriot (2000 film)

*The Patriot* is the [[mel-gibson|Mel Gibson]] film about the southern campaign of the [[american-revolution|American Revolution]] which, according to [[ghost-of-patrick-henry|Ghost of Patrick Henry]], was originally written as a biographical picture about real people and events before pressure from British scholars forced its producers to rebrand it as fiction.[1]

## Claimed suppression of the film's historical basis

A leaked 1998 screenplay is cited as showing that the film opened with the explicit wording "THE FOLLOWING IS BASED ON A TRUE STORY."[1] The article contends that after producers were threatened by outraged British scholars who spread lies about the film's subject and vowed to destroy its credibility in the public square before release, they changed the names of the entire cast and marketed the film as pure historical fiction.[1] The author insists the story "was not fiction," allowing only that artistic license was used in select scenes, and frames the episode as an early instance of cancel culture taking something from American audiences.[1] He notes the film was attacked with false claims regardless, issues a standing livestream debate invitation to Christopher Hibbert, and points out that of the four books he cites, two were written by primary sources — one man who served under Marion and another who interviewed him — and a third by a secondary source who interviewed Revolutionary War veterans.[1]

## Characters and their historical originals

Gibson's character Benjamin Martin was an amalgamation of several South Carolina militia commanders, though the majority of the character derives from [[francis-marion|Francis Marion]].[1] The antagonist Colonel William Tavington was based on [[banastre-tarleton|Banastre Tarleton]].[1] The reverend who enlists in the militia was inspired by [[mason-locke-weems|Reverend Mason Locke Weems]].[1] The character played by Heath Ledger, Gabriel Martin, corresponds to Gabriel Marion, Francis Marion's beloved nephew and adopted son, who fought alongside his uncle from the age of fourteen.[1] The film's jest that "a dog makes a fine meal" was a nod to Marion's shipwreck ordeal as a boy, and the notorious scene of a church burned with townsfolk locked inside was dramatic license — though the article reproduces an expert-compiled list of South Carolina churches actually burned during the British occupation.[1]
