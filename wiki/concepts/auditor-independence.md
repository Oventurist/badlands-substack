# Auditor Independence

Auditor independence is the professional and regulatory requirement that an auditor be free of any relationship, interest or appearance of bias that could compromise the impartiality of an audit.[1] In the Badlands Media essay "US Elections Must Be Independent," [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] describes it as foundational to public trust in capital markets, and argues that the same principles are conspicuously absent from the administration of US elections.[1]

## Training and attestation regime

The author spent her formative professional years in the [[securities-and-exchange-commission|SEC]] audit world, working from October 2003 to March 2017 as an employee of a Big Four SEC audit firm — not as an auditor, but on internal communications and change programs and later for the chief operating officer of the firm's consulting vertical.[1] Independence was nevertheless a government requirement for every employee, because public trust demands that the institution itself be above reproach given its immense power over capital markets.[1]

Required independence training covers all aspects of financial transactions and potential avenues for fraud, and — because Big Four auditors audit global businesses — extends to auditor independence in countries the US State Department considers to have corruption problems.[1] That portion of the training, covering bribery, anti-trust, money laundering and related crimes, was lengthy and culminated in an exam.[1] Its purpose was to ensure that those charged with safeguarding public trust in capital markets guard against corruption and can be held accountable if they do not; once independence has been attested to, "I didn't know" can never excuse a violation.[1] Employees were trained, examined and required to sign an annual attestation that they would not violate independence in fact or in appearance, as a condition of employment.[1]

## Standards

SEC auditors are governed by the [[public-company-accounting-oversight-board|Public Company Accounting Oversight Board]] (PCAOB), whose Auditing Standards set out the elements and principles of independence.[1] The essay cites three:

- AS1005.01: "In all matters relating to the assignment, an independence in mental attitude is to be maintained by the auditor or auditors."[1]
- AS1005.02: the auditor "must be without bias with respect to the client since otherwise he would lack that impartiality necessary for the dependability of his findings."[1]
- AS1005.03: it is of utmost importance to the profession that the general public maintain confidence in the independence of independent auditors; public confidence would be impaired by evidence that independence was actually lacking, "and it might also be impaired by the existence of circumstances which reasonable people might believe likely to influence independence."[1]

The last standard is central to the essay's argument: it is not only actual conflicts of interest but the *perception* of conflicts that can irreparably harm public trust.[1]

## The three threats

The three most common and consistent threats to independent audits and auditors are familiarity, self-interest and self-review.[1] When a public company lies about its financials or defrauds shareholders, one or more — often all — of these elements are present, as in the case of [[enron|Enron]] and [[arthur-andersen|Arthur Andersen]].[1]

## Application to elections

Ashe in America maps the same three threats onto US elections: familiarity, in the very little daylight between government entities and the election vendors and labs they oversee; self-interest, in elected officials' personal and professional stake in election outcomes and audits; and self-review, in election officials auditing their own work.[1] She notes there is no reported independence training for US elections entities, workers, labs, vendors, officials or oversight functions, no test required to keep one's job, and — beyond rhetoric — no focus on public trust.[1] Her conclusion is that no publicly traded company would be permitted to audit itself, and that the elections industry must likewise be independent and impervious to corruption.[1]
