# The Problem of Asia

*The Problem of Asia: Its Effect Upon International Politics* is the 1900 book by [[alfred-thayer-mahan|Alfred Thayer Mahan]] setting out his prescription for Western policy toward China and the Asian landmass.[1] Where [[william-gilpin|William Gilpin]] celebrated the awakening of China and other ancient civilizations, Mahan expressed only horror and disgust at the same prospect.[1]

Mahan wrote that "it is scarcely desirable that so vast a proportion of mankind as the Chinese constitute should be animated by but one spirit," and warned that should China "burst her barriers eastward, it would be impossible to exaggerate the momentous issues dependant upon a firm hold of the Hawaiian islands by a great civilized maritime power."[1] He also used the book to dismiss continental rail development, asserting that "a railway competes in vain with a river… because more facile and copious, water traffic is for equal distances much cheaper and because cheaper, more useful."[1]

The book is saturated with social Darwinism, defining the political differences between the branches of humanity then dubbed "Teutonic," "Slavic" and "Oriental" as rooted in racial constitution: "there are well recognized racial divergencies which find concrete expression in differences equally marked of political institution, of social progress and of individual development."[1] Mahan concluded that "the Oriental, whether national or individual does not change" and that "the East does not progress."[1]

Mahan described China as a carcass to be devoured by an American eagle, writing that "if life departs, a carcass can be utilized only by dissection or for food; the gathering to it of the eagles is a natural law, of which it is bootless to complain."[1] Advocating an Anglo-American partnership to subdue and "civilize" China after the [[boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]], he held that "of all the nations we shall meet in the East, Great Britain is the one with which we have by far the most in common."[1] On the means to be used, he was explicit that progress comes through force and war: "every step forward in the march that has opened in China to trade has been gained by pressure; the most important have been the result of actual war."[1]
