# Trojan Horse Methodology

"Trojan Horse methodology" is a term coined by [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] writer [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] to describe what he characterizes as the globalist practice of baiting and deceiving the general population into an illusion of benevolence and of the trustworthiness of a broken civilization.[1]

## The argument

Deschamps holds that most people perceive society as corrupt "but not all that bad," and certainly not willfully malevolent.[1] In his framing, the globalists are partially trustworthy on the surface, and that surface reliability functions as the wooden skin of the Trojan Horse: because everyday systems such as financial transfers work 99.99% of the time, the population generalizes that trust across society as a whole, and explains away the parts that plainly do not work — the justice system, the political process — as mere incompetence rather than calculated malevolence.[1]

He identifies this move with Hanlon's razor — "never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity" — a maxim he argues he has debunked in separate writing.[1]

## Application

The concept was introduced in commentary on the September 2023 [[2023-ukrainian-defense-ministry-purge|Ukrainian defense ministry purge]].[1] Deschamps argued that when leaders such as [[volodymyr-zelensky|Volodymyr Zelensky]] or [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] visibly remove a corrupt actor, the gesture instils faith in the stability of the system — "look: we punished a bad actor, that makes us good!" — and thereby functions as a self-deceiving smokescreen behind which systemic and industrialized abuses continue.[1]

He further argued that the inner core of the Trojan Horse is so disturbing that people reflexively black it out when they encounter it, citing the police who saw the contents of Anthony Weiner's laptop and the tolerance of the justice system toward child pornography, child trafficking, and abuses within child protective services — a reflexive aversion he treats as itself an intentionally generated effect.[1] The lubrication that allows the horse to be swallowed, in his account, is false moral virtue: audiences are made to feel good about supporting Ukraine, which requires that its hero appear heroic.[1]
