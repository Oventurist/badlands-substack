# Hermeticism

Hermeticism — also rendered Hermetism — is a philosophical system based on the purported teachings of [[hermes-trismegistus|Hermes Trismegistus]], a legendary Hellenistic figure formed from the syncretic combination of the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian god [[thoth|Thoth]].[1] The term *Hermetic* derives from the medieval Latin *hermeticus*, which in turn derives from the name of Hermes.[1] The subject was surveyed for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] in a February 17, 2023 primer titled "Exploring Hermeticism," presented as a deep dive into "a controversial set of teachings."[1]

## Scope of the corpus

The various teachings attributed to Hermes span several centuries, roughly c. 300 BCE to 1200 CE.[1] The term "Hermeticism" is most commonly used to describe the religio-philosophical system put forward by a specific subgroup of these writings known as the "Religio-philosophical Hermetica," the most popular of which are the [[corpus-hermeticum|Corpus Hermeticum]] — a collection of seventeen Greek Hermetic treatises written between c. 100 and c. 300 CE — and the [[asclepius|Asclepius]], a treatise of the same period surviving mainly in Latin translation.[1] These texts dwell on the oneness and goodness of God, urge purification of the soul, and expand on the relationship between mind and spirit.[1]

"Hermeticism" can also function as an open-ended term referring to a wide variety of philosophical systems that draw on Hermetic writings, or merely on subject matter generally associated with Hermes.[1] Most notably, [[alchemy|alchemy]] often went by the name "the Hermetic art" or "the Hermetic philosophy."[1]

## Origins

Hermeticism is commonly believed to have originated during Late Antiquity, the 3rd to 7th centuries CE, though others hold that the teachings may reach as far back as the 15th to 13th centuries BCE.[1] On the popular account, Hermeticism emerged in parallel with early Christianity, [[gnosticism|Gnosticism]] and [[neoplatonism|Neoplatonism]], with believed overlap with the Chaldaean Oracles and late Orphic and Pythagorean literature.[1] The Corpus Hermeticum itself has been dated by modern scholars and translators to the second century or earlier, and the works were reintroduced to the mass consciousness during the Renaissance after having been seemingly lost to the ages — or, DeLarme suggests, intentionally obscured.[1]

## Reputation and reception

Hermeticism frequently attracts suspicion because it was practiced by [[freemasonry|the Freemasons]] and the [[hermetic-order-of-the-golden-dawn|Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn]], but DeLarme argues that the teachings themselves are relatively neutral, and that it is the way they are applied that determines whether they serve good or ill.[1] Many Christian writers, among them Lactantius, Augustine, [[giordano-bruno|Giordano Bruno]], [[marsilio-ficino|Marsilio Ficino]], Campanella and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, regarded Hermes Trismegistus as a wise pagan prophet who foresaw the coming of Christianity, and appropriated Hermetic material in support of the doctrine of [[prisca-theologia|prisca theologia]].[1]

## Core texts and principles

The best-known Hermetic works discussed in the Badlands survey are the [[emerald-tablet|Emerald Tablet]], source of the dictum "As above, so below," and the [[divine-pymander|Divine Pymander]], a discourse between Hermes and a being named Pymander.[1] DeLarme observes that beneath their flowery language and distorted translations these texts largely set out the same material later condensed in [[the-kybalion|the Kybalion]] as the seven Hermetic principles: mentalism, correspondence, vibration, polarity, rhythm, cause and effect, and gender.[1]

He recommends that readers determine the meaning of these principles for themselves, describing the study as "an adventure best spent inward, and not tainted by the distortions of others," and closes with Bruce Lee's adage to "absorb what is useful, discard what is useless, and add what is specifically your own."[1]
