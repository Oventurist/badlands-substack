# Twenty-Second Amendment Third-Term Loophole

The **Twenty-Second Amendment third-term loophole** refers to a widely discussed constitutional argument that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] could return to the presidency after 2029 despite the two-term limit, by being elected vice president on a ticket whose presidential nominee resigns immediately after inauguration.[1]

## The scenario

A March 2025 *Daily Mail* feature sketched the scenario in narrative form: at noon on Inauguration Day, Saturday, January 20, 2029, [[jd-vance|James David Vance]] takes the oath of office before the U.S. Capitol and then declares, "I resign" — elevating his running mate, Trump, to a third presidential term amid crowds chanting "Four More Years."[1] The article noted that the 22nd Amendment limits presidents to two terms, but reported that experts asked whether Trump could nonetheless remain in the White House beyond that answered "yes."[1] Under such a chain the presidency could theoretically extend as far as 2037.[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands contributor [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] responded that "the most glaring loophole in the Constitution is that we're not operating under the Constitution," adding that if this was how the establishment wished to sell the idea to the public, he was "game."[1] He further argued that Trump had been "elected" in three fake and unconstitutional elections, and therefore "his clock hasn't even started."[1]

## Trump's public remarks and the amendment's history

Even before his re-election, Trump would occasionally float the idea of remaining in office beyond the two terms permitted by the Constitution, and by spring 2025 there were indications he was serious.[2] He told journalists at the [[white-house|White House]] that "people" were asking him to run again, and when asked whether he wanted to, replied: "I don't know, I never looked into it. They do say there is a way you can do it."[2] In a phone call with NBC News he did not rule out seeking a third term, saying "I like working… I'm not joking."[2]

The obstacle is the 22nd Amendment, which states: "No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice."[2] The two-term tradition traces to [[george-washington|George Washington]], the first president, who retired voluntarily after serving from 1789 to 1797, and for almost 150 years all of his successors adhered to the unspoken rule.[2] The only president to serve longer than two terms was [[franklin-d-roosevelt|Franklin D. Roosevelt]], who ran for a third and fourth term during the Second World War and died in 1945 a few months into his fourth presidency.[2] Even before his death, [[congress|Congress]] had concluded that Roosevelt's long presidency should remain a one-off exception justified by extraordinary circumstances; in 1947 it approved the 22nd Amendment and submitted it to the state legislatures, and by 1951 the requisite states had ratified it.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] responded to the DW report on the question with the quip that "the only thing better than three scoops ... is four," urging readers not to dismiss Trump's "latest memetic deployment" about a fourth term and instead to consider it in light of [[devolution|Devolution]] theory.[2]
