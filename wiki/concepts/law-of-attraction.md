# Law of Attraction

The **law of attraction** (LOA) is the doctrine, popularized in the West toward the end of the 20th century, that thought and language shape material outcomes.[1] In the Badlands Media essay "The Extraordinary Power of Digital Soldiers" (July 31, 2023), the author writing as [[american-hypnotist|American Hypnotist]] argues that whether or not the LOA as commonly taught is legitimate, the "dark occultists" he believes run the world almost certainly believe a particular version of it — otherwise their persistent use of symbolic ritual would be inexplicable.[1]

## The alleged two-tier doctrine

The essay's central claim about the LOA is that the ruling elite promoted a "solipsistic" version of it to the masses while reserving a more scientifically rational version for themselves.[1] This is presented as an instance of a general pattern in which the cabal sequesters knowledge as it sequesters wealth, so that most "mainstream" knowledge consists of partial half-truths; on this reasoning the most effective way to bury genuine knowledge is to erect a false look-alike in its place.[1]

## Criticism of the mainstream version

The author holds that LOA teaching as commonly delivered is unsound and often immoral because it is indistinguishable from [[solipsism|solipsism]], the doctrine that only the Self exists.[1] LOA gurus, he writes, teach that all fortunes and misfortunes are self-created without exception and that the individual is at fault for everything that happens to him.[1] His principal objection is moral: morality has no basis in a universe containing only one mind, since morality requires two or more beings.[1] Solipsism is therefore described as antithetical to both Christianity and science and incompatible with Western civilization, leaving the LOA in its popular form with nothing of value to offer.[1]

The essay situates the LOA's popularization after the 1960s as a watershed for "the controllers," likened to a culture-wide lobotomy, and attributes it to occult fraternities that have for centuries sought to replace a West founded on Christian ethics and Greco-Roman philosophy with a society organized around "do what thou wilt" and hostile to objective and scientific thinking.[1] James Billington's *Fire in the Minds of Men* is recommended as a history of these groups, which the essay refers to collectively, following popular usage, as the [[bavarian-illuminati|"Illuminati"]].[1] These fraternities are said always to have preached "eternal revolution," which the author notes "Obama-types simply call *change*," a campaign he says went into high gear after the Second World War.[1]

## The "real" law of attraction

Having rejected the solipsistic reading, the essay proposes a reconstructed version grounded in [[cognitive-theoretic-model-of-the-universe|the Cognitive-Theoretic Model of the Universe]].[1] If reality is a living language, then human beings are pieces of that same language and share an identity with the whole of reality; embedding consciousness in reality through a common universal metalanguage is said to make the LOA "a far more sensible concept," and to make more tractable such phenomena as quantum entanglement, telepathy, premonitions, and synchronicities.[1]

The reconstructed principle is summarized as: the universe talks to itself, about itself, and in the process creates itself — God at the highest level, human beings at the human level.[1] Because creation is mediated by language, the more skilled a mind is with language the more powerfully it can influence creation, to the point that one mind might "out-manifest" another simply by being more coherent.[1] "Skill with language" here means the process of meaning-making in its most general sense, including mathematics, rather than fluency in any particular tongue; the resulting maxim is "I speak/write, therefore I create."[1]

The essay also uses the CTMU's distinction between physical proximity and linguistic or descriptive proximity — the latter said to be largely unaffected by the former — as a plausible mechanism by which symbolic ritual could affect real-world events.[1] This is offered as the rationale behind ritual spellcasting, understood as the theory that local symbolic acts can alter nonlocal events through the "spelling" of words.[1]
