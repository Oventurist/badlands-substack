# Red Sea Shipping Crisis

The Red Sea shipping crisis refers to the campaign of drone and missile attacks launched by the [[houthis|Houthis]] against commercial vessels transiting the Red Sea beginning in late 2023, and the debate in the United States over the [[joe-biden|Biden]] administration's failure to restore naval deterrence.[1]

## Escalation

By December 2023 it had become clear that a full-blown Houthi war on commercial shipping was underway, with container ships coming under drone and missile attack on a daily basis, triggering delays and rising prices on goods as major liners abandoned Red Sea transit altogether.[1] American warships stationed in waters off Yemen came under direct attack without the Commander-in-Chief ordering a military response.[1]

## Political dispute

Weeks before the crisis peaked, U.S. defense officials complained that the [[pentagon|Pentagon]] was being restrained from striking Houthi positions in Yemen by the [[white-house|White House]].[1] Biden stood accused of downplaying the threat; *Politico* reported that some current and former military officials were frustrated by the administration's initial response to the Houthis' Sunday attacks on shipping, while top brass pushed for a more forceful response lest the Iranian proxies grow more emboldened.[1] The *Wall Street Journal* published a commentary headlined "U.S. Naval Deterrence Is Going, Going, Maybe Even Gone," pointing to the consequences of the U.S. Navy coming under attack and failing to hit back decisively.[1]

Analysts attributed the passivity in part to Biden's desire to make nice with the Iranians in order to keep global energy prices down ahead of the 2024 election, producing a posture of "looking the other way"; some warned it was already too late to reestablish deterrence.[1]

## Badlands analysis

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] of [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] said he had put [[jon-herold|Jon Herold]] on the story ahead of the following week's Power Hour because it carried "the gravitational effects of [[devolution|Devolution]]."[1] He cautioned that distinguishing actuals from narratives in any theater of the Info War is nearly impossible when separated from the action by oceans, continents, cultures and a media matrix built on obfuscation.[1] What he took from the episode was that whatever was happening in the Red Sea had so far failed to trigger a U.S. military response under the framing of National Essential Functions, and that Biden either had no desire — or, more likely in his view, no ability — to intervene.[1]

## Operation Prosperity Guardian

On December 19, 2023, Secretary of Defense [[lloyd-austin|Lloyd Austin]] announced [[operation-prosperity-guardian|Operation Prosperity Guardian]], a U.S.-led multinational coalition to safeguard Red Sea shipping, initially naming Britain, Bahrain, Canada, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Seychelles and Spain as participants.[3] Two days later he said Greece and Australia had joined, bringing the total to twenty countries, at least eight of which declined to be publicly named.[3] Several allies qualified their involvement: France kept its ships under national command, Italy sent the frigate Virginio Fasan but outside the coalition framework, and Spain refused to participate in anything not led by NATO or coordinated by the EU, while Britain committed the destroyer HMS Diamond.[3]

## January 2024 escalation

On the night of Thursday, January 18, 2024, [[houthis|Houthi]] fighters launched two anti-ship ballistic missiles at the M/V Chem Ranger, a Marshall Islands-flagged and Greek-operated, U.S.-owned tanker, at 9 p.m. local time.[2] [[centcom|U.S. Central Command]] reported that the missiles fell into the water without striking the vessel, that there was no damage and no injuries, and that the tanker continued on its way.[2]

By that point the Houthis had launched more than thirty attacks from Yemen against commercial shipping since late November 2023, a growing problem for the Biden administration that showed no signs of stopping.[2] The United States had responded with retaliatory strikes on five occasions since an initial round the previous week, yet the Houthis continued to target merchant ships.[2] The hostilities disrupted global trade and forced several of the world's largest shipping companies to abandon the Red Sea shortcut in favour of the long route around Africa.[2] The [[pentagon|Pentagon]] maintained that U.S. strikes were damaging Houthi infrastructure and forcing the Iranian-backed militia to scale down its attacks, even as the group proved resilient.[2]

Separately, senior Houthi official [[mohammed-al-bukhaiti|Mohammed al-Bukhaiti]] told the Russian outlet Izvestia that Russian and Chinese vessels would be granted safe passage, insisting the waters around Yemen were safe for any ship not linked to certain countries, particularly [[israel|Israel]], while attacks on vessels "in any way connected with Israel" would continue.[2] Reporting noted that Houthi attacks had in practice struck ships with no apparent Israeli connection.[2]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that in terms of "Actuals" the crisis amounted to an escalating kinetic blockade forming across one of the most important east–west trade routes, disproportionately affecting the West because of prior sociopolitical and military conflicts.[2] While Western framing cast [[china|China]] and [[russia|Russia]] as cooperating beneficiaries of the chaos, he judged it equally likely that the Iranian proxies were deliberately muddying the waters in an already tense great-power standoff.[2] In narrative terms, he concluded, one party was being attacked from all sides: "The Biden Admin is more isolated and embattled than ever."[2]

## Maersk attack and Iranian warship deployment

On December 31, 2023, an Iran-backed [[houthis|Houthi]] missile struck a Maersk container ship, prompting Maersk to halt Red Sea transits and drawing a U.S. helicopter response that destroyed three small boats and killed ten rebels.[4] Houthi spokesman [[yahya-sarea|Yahya Sarea]] warned of "consequences and repercussions," framing the campaign as deterrence of Israel-related commercial vessels in support of the Palestinian people.[4] [[iran|Iran]] then dispatched a warship to the Red Sea, which Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read as "a major marker of attempted intimidation" amid an escalation pattern in the Suez approaches.[4] He also noted the return of the [[uss-gerald-r-ford|USS Ford]] carrier to the United States, replaced by another carrier reportedly carrying 2,000 marines.[4]

## January 9, 2024 mass drone and missile barrage

On the night of January 9, 2024, the [[us-navy|US Navy]] shot down 21 [[houthis|Houthi]] missiles and drones launched from Yemen, according to [[us-central-command|US Central Command]], in one of the largest Houthi attacks in the Red Sea in recent months.[5] The military called it a "complex attack" carried out by the Iranian-backed militants, coming amid rising tensions around Israel's war against Hamas in Gaza and fears of a wider regional conflict.[5]

The barrage, launched at about 9:15 p.m. Yemen time, included 18 one-way attack drones, two anti-ship cruise missiles and one anti-ship ballistic missile, and was directed toward international shipping lanes in the southern Red Sea where "dozens" of merchant vessels were transiting.[5] Two defense officials had earlier told CNN that the barrage totaled 24 drones and missiles.[5] CENTCOM said no ships were damaged and no injuries resulted.[5]

## Russian naval deployment

In late March 2024, Russian warships from the Pacific Fleet crossed the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait and entered the Red Sea, according to the state-run Tass news agency.[6] The detachment included the missile cruiser Varyag and the frigate Marshal Shaposhnikov, which the Pacific Fleet's press service said were carrying out "assigned tasks within the framework of the long-range sea campaign"; the ultimate destination and the reason for the deployment were unclear from the report.[6] The vessels entered a maritime region plagued by [[houthis|Houthi]] attacks and crowded with naval ships from multiple nations.[6] Earlier that month the Houthis had told China and Russia their ships could transit the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden unmolested, in exchange for possible political support in bodies such as the United Nations Security Council.[6]

## MSC United attack (December 2023)

On 26 December 2023 the [[houthis|Houthis]] claimed a missile strike on the container ship MSC UNITED, stating that "the naval forces of the Yemeni Armed Forces carried out a targeting operation against the commercial ship 'MSC UNITED' with appropriate naval missiles."[7] The vessel was reportedly bound for Pakistan when it came under attack; no crew injuries were reported and damage was being assessed, and the Houthis said the ship had rejected three warning calls before being fired upon.[7] The incident coincided with a fresh Houthi missile attack on [[israel|Israel]], and occurred while the [[joe-biden|Biden]] White House was still struggling to assemble its multinational naval coalition.[7]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the Suez-adjacent escalation was the major international crisis receiving the least attention, despite Iran-backed militias threatening a substantial share of global trade routes.[7] He highlighted two trendlines in the emerging multipolar order: the rebuke of [[operation-prosperity-guardian|Operation Prosperity Guardian]] by supposed allies in the UK, France and Australia, who he said did not trust the sitting administration to uphold its side of any military agreement; and the priming of [[egypt|Egypt]] for a central role in settling both the Suez crisis and the wider Gaza conflict — all with [[donald-trump|Trump]] strategically on the sidelines.[7]

## Eternity C attack (July 2025)

On Monday, July 7, 2025, at least two crew members of a Greek-owned vessel were wounded and two were missing in the Red Sea, according to Greek government officials and the vessel's owner.[8] In the second comparable attack in the Red Sea within 24 hours, the Liberian-flagged cargo ship *Eternity C* was targeted by sea drones and skiffs off Hodeidah, 50 nautical miles west of the Yemeni capital Sanaa, which is controlled by the Iran-aligned [[houthis|Houthi]] rebels.[8] No party claimed responsibility for the strikes.[8]

## Multinational warning to the Houthis (January 2024)

In early January 2024 the United States joined ten other countries in warning the Iranian-backed [[houthis|Houthi]] rebels that if they continued to attack shipping they would "bear the consequences," language widely read as suggesting the threat of military action against targets in Yemen.[9] U.S. deputy ambassador Christopher Lu told an emergency [[united-nations-security-council|U.N. Security Council]] meeting that the Houthis had carried out more than 20 attacks since November 19, and the rebel group announced that Wednesday morning it had targeted another container ship.[9] The warning came amid questions over whether President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] was being tough enough on the Yemeni group and fears that escalation in the region would disrupt world trade.[9] The Houthis, who support [[hamas|Hamas]] in its war against Israel following the October 7 attacks, used missiles, drones, fast boats and helicopters, claiming to target ships linked to Israel in revenge for its military campaign in Gaza.[9]

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[paul-fleuret|Paul Fleuret]] characterized the episode as another instalment of a recurring pattern in which a world conflict escalates while Biden's response is "weak, feckless, and useless," likening anything Joe had to say on the matter to "stopping a freight train with a BB gun."[9] He argued that each day of visible weakness woke more of the American public to the state of the White House and would ultimately drive voters toward [[donald-trump|Trump]] in November.[9]
