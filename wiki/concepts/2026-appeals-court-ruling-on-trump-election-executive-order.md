# 2026 Appeals Court Ruling on Trump's Election Executive Order

In late July 2026 a federal appeals court blocked key portions of [[donald-trump|President Donald Trump]]'s executive order on election administration in 23 Democrat-led states, ruling that the administration could not enforce several provisions while litigation continued.[1] The decision preserved a district-court injunction issued by Judge Talwani for the plaintiff states and set up a likely [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] showdown over the scope of presidential authority in regulating federal elections and the balance of power between the federal government and the states.[1]

## Contents of the executive order

The order sought to impose new federal election requirements, including proof-of-citizenship measures and restrictions affecting mail voting.[1] As described in contemporaneous reporting, it directed the [[department-of-homeland-security|Department of Homeland Security]] to compile lists of confirmed US citizens eligible to vote and provide them to states, instructed the [[united-states-postal-service|US Postal Service]] to establish new standards for mail-in ballots, and directed the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]] to prioritize investigations into state and local election officials who issue federal ballots to people deemed ineligible to vote.[1]

## Procedural posture and timing

The appellate ruling denied a Justice Department request to lift the injunction rather than resolving the merits.[1] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] observed that the decision "does not settle the underlying dispute over presidential power but keeps Talwani's injunction intact while the case winds through the courts, a delay that could push final resolution dangerously close to the midterms."[1]

## Badlands commentary

Badlands coverage framed the suing states as contesting the administration's ability to govern the Postal Service, describing this as a conflict over authorities and established decision rights: states do not hold decision rights over federal executive agencies, yet under the ruling the President was held not to either.[1] The commentary posed the resulting constitutional question directly — if neither the states nor the President may exercise the Postal Service's decision rights, who is constitutionally authorized to do so.[1]

The Brief predicted that the Supreme Court would decide dangerously close to the midterms, calling this the kind of drama characteristic of what it terms fake election narratives, while noting that history suggests the Court declines to make changes so close to an election.[1] Because the dispute concerns presidential authorities — battles that had largely been resolving in Trump's favor — the commentary expected this one to as well, and treated the clarification of such matters as valuable in itself.[1]
