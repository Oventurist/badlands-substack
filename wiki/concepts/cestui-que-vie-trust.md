# Cestui Que Vie Trust

The **Cestui Que Vie trust** is a historical English legal device concerning trusts held for the benefit of a person whose life measures the trust's duration. In the Badlands Media essay [[soul-governance|Soul Governance]], the term is repurposed as the key mechanism of what the article calls soul theft.[1]

The essay describes Cestui Que Vie trusts as "active instruments of soul assumption," created to operationalize the jurisdictional claim asserted in [[unam-sanctam|Unam Sanctam]] by [[boniface-viii|Pope Boniface VIII]].[1] Under this reading, an individual who cannot affirmatively prove that he is alive "with spiritual sovereignty" is presumed lost at sea and therefore treated as a vessel or a thing rather than a living man or woman.[1] The essay draws the maritime analogy to its conclusion: the person becomes cargo and the [[vatican|Vatican]] becomes the port.[1]

The trust device is presented as inseparable from the [[strawman-theory|Strawman]] doctrine, in which registration at birth generates a legal fiction distinct from the living person — an entity that can be taxed, surveilled, fined, regulated, and detained.[1] The essay attributes the drafting and administration of this apparatus to the [[jesuits|Jesuits]], described as "software engineers of empire" who converted courts into temples and reframed "understanding" as "standing under."[1]

The essay's remedy is not litigation but withdrawal of consent: it holds that no claim of this kind binds a person who refuses to identify with the legal fiction, and that "when the soul remembers, the contracts collapse."[1]

Mainstream legal scholarship treats Cestui Que Vie as an ordinary and largely obsolete feature of English trust and property law, most notably the Cestui Que Vie Act 1666, and courts have consistently rejected claims built on the interpretation described above.[1]
