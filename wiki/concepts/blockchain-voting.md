# Blockchain Voting

**Blockchain voting** is the proposed use of [[blockchain]] distributed-ledger technology to replace or supplement the existing United States election infrastructure of proprietary hardware and software developed by private election technology vendors.[1] In the Badlands Media article "Blockchain, Good. Blockchain Voting, Bad.", published 3 March 2023 by [[holly-at-altitude|Holly at Altitude]], the author argues that while blockchain is a valuable technology in general, its application to voting is at best a well-meaning misapplication of technology and at worst "a sick joke."[1]

The article states that the push for blockchain voting is quietly gaining momentum, with beta testing programs already started in counties across the U.S. and [[blockchain-voting-companies|blockchain voting companies]] (BVCs) pitching election officials on the benefits of their systems.[1] It contends that almost all Americans — and the election officials making adoption decisions — are woefully uninformed about the underlying technology, how it is best applied, and the consequences of using it for voting.[1]

## Definitions and the spectrum of proposals

The article observes that definitions of blockchain voting vary wildly.[1] At one end of the spectrum, blockchain enthusiasts with no grasp of how the U.S. election system works offer vague headlines such as "Blockchain can be used for voting," without any plan for how it would actually work.[1] In the middle, advocates propose piecemeal applications — using a blockchain to verify voter identification, or uploading completed ballots to a chain for transparency and tracking — ideas the author concedes have some merit but which rest on the false premise that solving isolated problems can meaningfully fix the system overall.[1]

At the other end, BVCs pitch step-by-step blockchain voter "user journeys" focused on the individual voter's experience, wants, and needs.[1] The author describes these offerings as predictably similar regardless of vendor: "a stew of many convoluted steps" including smartphone app downloads, QR codes, bar codes, public and private keys, verification steps, relays of encrypted and non-encrypted information, serialized ballots, scanning, tear-off paper ballot receipts, and digital wallets.[1]

All versions, the article says, ultimately promise the same three things: that ballots uploaded to a blockchain create a secure record that cannot be changed or deleted; that voters can track their own and everyone else's ballots on the chain; and that voters can trust their ballots will be counted quickly and accurately.[1]

## The technology-and-elections premise

The article grounds its argument in the history of election technology, holding that the introduction of technology through the [[help-america-vote-act|Help Americans Vote Act]] (HAVA) in 2002 turned U.S. elections into a multi-billion-dollar industry overnight, created the conditions for an overly complicated, privately developed, owned, and managed election "machine," and put elected and non-elected officials with almost no technical skills and zero cybersecurity expertise in charge of evaluating, selecting, purchasing, running, auditing, and verifying the mandated technology.[1] HAVA is described as having fundamentally changed the system designed by the Founding Fathers, shifting voting from a decentralized civic process — paper ballots cast at local precincts on election day and hand-counted by local officials and volunteers — to a centralized, tech-driven, and now corrupted apparatus.[1]

Against that background the author frames the central question: whether there is any rationality in replacing one corrupted multi-billion-dollar industry with another prone to the same failings.[1] Blockchain voting, in her view, "only shifts and creates more problems; it does not solve them."[1] The author notes she is personally a fan of blockchain technology and expects its potential to be realized eventually, but not now and not in a citizen's voting process.[1]

## Case 1: Ownership and profitability

The article's first objection is that blockchain voting can never be genuinely "ownerless" and that profitability is guaranteed.[1] Existing vendors enjoy privacy protections under the law — intellectual property, internal communications, financial reporting — that let private companies legally block access to information needed to manage, oversee, monitor, and audit election equipment, damaging transparency, accountability, auditing, administration, investigations, and trust.[1] Advocates counter that because open blockchains are not owned by any single entity, blockchain voting resolves these problems; the author disputes this.[1]

She notes that BVCs are not clear about which chain their products use, and that it is unknown whether they are building proprietary chains or using open ones.[1] The worst case is a private blockchain developed by a BVC — giving one company control of the entire blockchain infrastructure — or one developed by government, which would leave elections state-owned but run by private companies with little chance of public accountability.[1] Patenting company technology would likewise block public and official access to critical technology, mimicking the situation created by existing electronic voting equipment vendors.[1]

The article further asks who would own the extensive supporting infrastructure — apps, interfaces, connections, equipment, APIs, and software — required to make blockchain voting usable by the average voter, and how ownership would work for the components integrating the chain with legacy government election equipment and databases.[1] Open-sourcing the code and claiming decentralized operation does not dismiss the core problem, because at some point the BVC or blockchain creator had full control of the technology, and there is no telling how that genesis affects the system's integrity.[1] Outstanding questions include who funded the BVC, who developed the technology, whether partnership or integration deals exist with other companies or the government, and what privacy and security measures the company guarantees, tests, and verifies.[1]

On blockchain governance, the article notes that neither BVCs nor advocates have explained a governance plan or the reward system for running a supposedly decentralized, open chain — raising the question of whether the election system would be monetized — and asks whether there will be an initial coin offering, who decides initial valuation, how many tokens will be issued, and what the plan is for burning, mining difficulty, and forking.[1] Fundamentally, the author writes, a BVC is a for-profit company, and whoever develops the technology and infrastructure controls and profits from it; in an industry where power, influence, and wealth are at stake, BVCs are at risk of corruption just like their predecessors.[1]

## Case 2: Security and integration

The second objection is that blockchain voting will always be part of the established, vulnerable, corrupted system.[1] Running an election is far more complex than a single voter's experience: officials manage thousands of transactions during administration, with database and IT management at the core, from voter databases that issue, process, track, manage, and store ballots.[1] The article asserts that election officials completely lack the technical experience and skillset to secure their infrastructure, leaving equipment vendors, tech contractors, and some government IT employees in control of the entire process in nearly every jurisdiction.[1]

Because the current system processes paper ballots, it must integrate the physical world of people and paper with multiple databases, hardware, software, computers, and peripherals such as routers and scanners; integration is identified as one of the key weaknesses of technology-driven election systems, since each piece of equipment, connection, API, app, and smartphone is a cybersecurity vulnerability.[1] Advocates sell voters on convenience, security, transparency, and trackability, and assure tech-illiterate officials that administration becomes much easier, while touting authentication features such as unique QR codes, private-key confirmation, and identity verification with selfie video and photo uploads.[1]

The author's rebuttal is that BVCs have misdiagnosed the problem: replacing a sliver of the system, or securing ballot images on a chain, still requires integration into an already-corrupted system and cures none of the systemic problems of weak cybersecurity, lack of transparency, and distrust of technology.[1] Databases and blockchains fall prey to the same corruption issue — a blockchain cannot verify whether data entered into it is authentic, and cannot tell whether a digital ballot image is forged, counterfeit, or a copy of an original.[1] BVCs also fail to address persistent integrity issues from state-managed centralized voter databases, universal [[mail-in-voting|mail-in voting]], and electronic voting and counting, the three factors the article says allow manipulation at scale and with which blockchain voting would necessarily be integrated.[1]

It is impossible, the article argues, to cyber-secure any process, connection, action, or equipment occurring outside the blockchain, and that outside layer cannot be eliminated because physical ballots and IDs must be scanned, imaged, and transferred for entry into the system; no hardware or software, including phones, can be completely secured, and systems are routinely breached and data manipulated via peripherals such as smartphones and email.[1] Election administration requires officials to access, use, manipulate, transfer, and store data in real time under election laws, statutes, and rules — tasks for which blockchain is ill-equipped by design, since chains are built for cryptographically securing, recording, and tracking data, not manipulating it.[1] Current proposals also offer no plan guaranteeing that ballots are accurately counted — a separate third-party counting system would be required — or that post-election auditing and ballot reconciliation comply with the differing laws of each locality and state, which change annually or more often.[1]

## Case 3: Centralization and trust

The third objection holds that centralization destroys trust in systems.[1] American trust in the election system continues to decline because of catastrophic security failures, election maladministration, and technology distrust, symptoms the author says are amplified by increasing centralization.[1] As an illustration, only a handful of electronic voting equipment companies exist worldwide; the top three are vertically integrated and control around 90% of the U.S. election market, and nearly all equipment used in U.S. elections is manufactured in China.[1] The article states that countless billions of dollars, critical military data, and valuable intellectual property have been lost through equipment and software breaches, and that nation-state attacks on critical infrastructure are a persistent, relentless threat — citing the [[solarwinds|SolarWinds]] breach as one of the most devastating, undetected by the government for months and with damage still unknown.[1]

Blockchain is trusted because it has so far proven secure, but the author stresses that this trust extends only to security within chains themselves, which derives from decentralized operation; the linkage of blockchain to decentralization does not make blockchain *voting* inherently secure or trustworthy.[1] Centralization is unavoidable in implementation, because even applying a chain within the current system requires integrating it into untrusted election infrastructure.[1] Centralized systems, she notes, include businesses, vendors, markets, and governments, all prone to breach and corruption, and decentralization used for cybersecurity in part of a voter's journey does not apply outside the chain.[1] Implementation would inevitably require BVCs to work with the electronic voting companies that created and control the broken system, so blockchain voting "starts off corrupted to some degree."[1] Finally, because implementation depends on for-profit BVCs, even a proliferation of competing vendors would never be numerous enough to decentralize control over the entire election system.[1]

## Summary of the argument

The author attributes the misdirected push for blockchain voting to two factors: the obvious and overwhelming motivation to restore integrity and trust to elections, and a cohort of tech-forward people who see blockchain's potential but do not fully understand the election system.[1] Faced with a problem, she writes, people revert to familiar tools — "surgeons cut, politicians spend, techies tech" — which works only when the problem is correctly diagnosed.[1] Because the scope, depth, and inner workings of the "U.S. election machine" are misunderstood, proposed applications are riddled with blind spots and the negative consequences of implementation are ignored.[1] The article closes with the standard note that [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] features represent the opinions of contributing authors.[1]
