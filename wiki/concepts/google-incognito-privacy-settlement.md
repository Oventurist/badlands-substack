# Google Incognito Privacy Settlement

The Google Incognito privacy settlement is the resolution of a class-action lawsuit alleging that [[google|Google]] tracked users of the Chrome browser's private "Incognito" mode without their knowledge, disclosed in a court filing on Monday, April 1, 2024.[1]

## The lawsuit

The class action was filed in 2020 and accused Google of misleading users about how Chrome tracked the activity of anyone using the private Incognito browsing option.[1] The suit alleged that Google's marketing and privacy disclosures did not properly inform users of the kinds of data being collected, including details about which websites they viewed.[1]

## Settlement terms

The settlement details were filed in San Francisco federal court and set out the actions the company would take to change its practices around private browsing.[1] Google agreed to destroy billions of data points that the lawsuit alleged it improperly collected — a trove reflecting millions of users' web-browsing histories — to update disclosures about what it collects in private browsing, and to give users the option to disable third-party cookies in that setting.[1]

The agreement did not include damages for individual users, though it allows individuals to file claims; plaintiff attorneys had already filed 50 in California state court.[1] Attorney [[david-boies|David Boies]], who represented the consumers, said the settlement requires Google to delete and remediate "in unprecedented scope and scale" the data it improperly collected, calling it "an historic step in requiring honesty and accountability from dominant technology companies."[1] Google did not immediately respond to a request for comment.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] that data "is always being generated, collected, refined, analyzed, and used, for predictive purposes and often sold."[1] She noted that technology capabilities had exploded over two decades: 10 to 15 years earlier it was nearly impossible to derive business value from the mass of unstructured data companies generated — log files, event streams, cached web and app data — most of which is not personally identifiable and is governed under looser regulation than healthcare information or Social Security numbers.[1]

In her assessment there is no real way to keep companies from gathering user data, because the modern regulatory compliance landscape focuses on masking identifiable information as it enters systems, meaning the data is collected first and masked afterward.[1] Incognito mode, on this reading, is "just a pinky promise by Google that they are going to appropriately mask your identity when they collect and use your data."[1] She characterized the legal terrain as gray and the cases as unprecedented because individuals do not own their own data — a state of affairs she called an arguably more important civil rights issue than censorship, if less headline-grabbing.[1]
