# Decentralized Finance (DeFi)

Decentralized finance, commonly abbreviated "DeFi," refers to the ecosystem of financial services — lending, trading, staking, and payments — built on public blockchains rather than on banks and other centralized intermediaries. Advocates present it as the technological answer to a century of central banking, arguing that decentralized digital asset classes are the future of money as Americans brace for a possible decline of the US dollar.[1]

## Skepticism about the label

Badlands Media contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] reported that she agreed with DeFi advocates about the underlying problems of centralized finance and the need for decentralized solutions, but remained skeptical that "currency as code" is the answer.[1] Independent journalist [[ryan-matta|Ryan Matta]], asked to define DeFi, replied bluntly: "The first thing you need to understand is that there's no such thing as 'DeFi.'"[1] His argument was that [[proof-of-stake]] blockchains — the dominant design in the sector — are not meaningfully decentralized, because validators are chosen according to how many coins they have staked and project teams typically retain enough stake at launch to control and change the rules of a chain.[1]

## Scale of losses

The sector has been a magnet for exploitation. According to CoinTelegraph, DeFi projects saw "$3.6 Billion lost from all attacks on all project types" in 2022, an increase of 47.4 percent over the previous year's $2.4 billion in security-exploit-related incidents.[1] Large-dollar failures such as [[ftx|FTX]] draw mainstream coverage, while smaller frauds — retail investors scammed out of $20,000–$30,000 — rarely make the news and leave victims with little recourse in a highly volatile asset class.[1]

## Ecosystem and adoption

More than 21,000 different cryptocurrencies had come into existence by 2023, spanning several categories of digital asset.[1] A 2021 Pew Research Center study found that 16 percent of Americans had ever invested in, traded, or used a cryptocurrency; by early 2023, after the late-2022 market collapse, the Financial Health Network's FinHealth Spend study found only 11 percent of consumers held cryptocurrencies or digital assets.[1] Those deeply engaged — mining, developing projects, or day trading — comprise a far smaller group still.[1]
