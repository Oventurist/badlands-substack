# The Art of the Deal

*The Art of the Deal* is the business book authored by [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]], widely read within the [[maga-coalition|MAGA]] movement and frequently invoked as a lens for interpreting Trump's political conduct.[1] In a December 3, 2024 Badlands Media essay, contributor [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] used the book as the interpretive framework for understanding the controversy surrounding Trump's second-term cabinet selections.[1]

## Core principle as described

Carlson argued that the book's central takeaway is that a good deal is one in which every party walks away feeling satisfied — feeling to some degree that they won, or at minimum that the outcome was acceptable.[1] If one party believes it was the clear loser, it will likely never negotiate with the other party again, foreclosing future business.[1]

Achieving mutual satisfaction, in this reading, requires concessions: paying somewhat more than one might have, in exchange for preserving an ongoing relationship.[1] The approach also requires removing pride from negotiations. A counterparty may have been dishonest, disloyal, or may have "stabbed you in the back" in past dealings; the dealmaker can either let pride destroy the transaction or adopt an "all is fair in war and business" posture and close it.[1]

## Application to the 2024 cabinet selections

Carlson applied this framework to the [[2024-trump-cabinet-selection-controversy|dispute over Trump's cabinet picks]]. He noted that although Trump would hold office for four years, he was realistically guaranteed congressional majorities for only the first two, since a president's party often loses one or both chambers at the midterms — at which point, in Carlson's account, real change stops.[1]

Trump therefore had [[rino|RINOs]] and [[neoconservatives|NeoCons]] in [[congress|Congress]] and the [[united-states-senate|Senate]] "over a barrel," but pressing that advantage too hard could push them to vote with Democrats and block his agenda.[1] Extending concessions — potentially including a cabinet pick or two that the base disliked — was, in this argument, the price of a functioning working majority.[1] Carlson acknowledged that many readers would prefer to give the RINOs and NeoCons nothing, but countered that a two-year legislative stalemate would harm everyone, and that America could not wait for those figures to be primaried out.[1]

He added a further inversion of the framework: any nominees who ultimately failed to be confirmed were, in his view, likely picks Trump never intended to confirm.[1] Under that reading, the art of the deal consists of making the opposing parties believe they had won something when in fact they had won nothing.[1]

## Reception and framing

The essay presented Trump's billionaire status in the "cutthroat world of real estate" as evidence that he understands both dealmaking and the necessity of concessions.[1] It is an opinion piece, and Badlands Media notes that contributor articles represent the views of their authors rather than the outlet.[1]
