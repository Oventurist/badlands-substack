# Cass Review

The Cass Review is the independent review of youth gender medicine in the United Kingdom's National Health Service, spearheaded by the British pediatrician [[hilary-cass]].[1] Its final report concluded that youth gender medicine "is an area of remarkably weak evidence, and yet results of studies are exaggerated or misrepresented by people on all sides of the debate to support their viewpoint," adding that "we have no good evidence on the long-term outcomes of interventions to manage gender-related distress."[1]

## Methodology

Cass and her colleagues reached that conclusion after an ambitious yearslong effort to interview clinicians, parents, and patients about their experiences with the National Health Service's youth gender medicine system.[1] She also commissioned a sizable bundle of independent systematic reviews evaluating both the evidence base for puberty blockers and hormones and the quality of recommendations published by influential bodies such as the World Professional Association for Transgender Healthcare.[1] Dozens of studies were collected and evaluated by a team at the University of York, culminating in what The Dispatch characterized as a damning verdict on the present state of youth gender medicine and the professional guidelines surrounding it.[1]

## Findings and framing

In her report Cass clarifies that her goal is not to question whether some young people are "really" transgender.[1] She acknowledges that some young people are in tremendous distress about their gender and does not deny that some may benefit from blockers and/or hormones; her argument is simply that powerful medical treatments should be underpinned by quality evidence, which in this field is clearly not the case.[1] Cass also emphasizes the need to ensure that youth referred to gender clinics receive proper screening and assessment before medical interventions are undertaken, especially for the growing subset of such youth who are autistic or who have mental-health comorbidities that some experts believe can significantly complicate the diagnostic process in these settings.[1]

## Reception and Badlands commentary

The Dispatch observed that anyone who reads the Cass Review and then reads most recent mainstream American media coverage of youth gender medicine will be "gobsmacked" by the divergence.[1] Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief]], [[simon-esler]] framed the UK announcement that the gender-affirmative model is dangerous and lacks evidence as exposing the information warfare localized to North American entities, arguing that the collapse of the narrative in the UK was finally reaching American shores.[1] Esler characterized the affair as evidence of Deep State failure, contending that scientific and academic institutions in America had been radicalized so far that international pushback spotlighted their loss of credibility.[1]

Esler further argued that framing the dispute as mere political polarization is inadequate and should be replaced by recognition of sophisticated psychological and information warfare, while crediting the article with accurately depicting compromised media, academia, and activism participating in what he called the largest child abuse scandal in human history.[1] He noted that the coverage effectively put NBC, CNN, GLAAD, the Endocrine Society, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and some twenty left-wing journalists on notice for spreading what he described as dangerous mal-information, and concluded that the review provides new ammunition for persuading left-leaning audiences on the issue.[1]

## April 2024 release and US reaction

The review's release in April 2024 was covered in the United States as a landmark study that undercut the claim that gender-dysphoric adolescents who do not receive "gender-affirming care" will kill themselves.[2] Reporting in [[the-epoch-times|The Epoch Times]] summarized the findings as confirming that children presenting with sudden-onset gender dysphoria are frequently suffering from other mental health diagnoses rather than true gender dysphoria, and that such children disproportionately have mental-health issues stemming from difficult family situations or domestic abuse and are more likely to be neurodiverse or to carry a co-morbid autism spectrum disorder diagnosis.[2] The release came days after the National Association of Intercollegiate Athletics announced that it would bar transgender-identified men from women's athletic events across its 239 small private member schools.[2]

Writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] predicted that "the pendulum will swing back drastically on this season of gender affirming care," and that history would judge the medical industry most profoundly for crimes against humanity, especially against children.[2] She argued that gender dysphoria is a complex mental illness co-indicated with other complex mental illnesses such as major depressive disorder and bipolar disorders, and often with early childhood abuse and other nonconsensual sexual traumas.[2] She characterized the contemporary medical establishment as embracing eugenics and widespread medical experimentation out of a profit motivation, noting that "transgender patients are patients for life" and that gender-affirming care constitutes a major revenue stream, while cautioning readers not to abandon empathy altogether because "evil uses vulnerable people as props all the time."[2]

## Role in the Tavistock closure

Badlands contributor [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] wrote that when a review of the [[tavistock-institute|Tavistock]] gender clinic's practices was carried out by Dr. [[hilary-cass|Hilary Cass]], the result was the clinic being shut down, even though the clinic claimed its Gender Identity Development Service was closing only because the review recommended a regional rather than a single national service and not because of safety concerns.[3] He argued the subsequent changes told a different story.[3]

Esler emphasised that the review's interim report defined the "affirmative model" as "a model of gender healthcare that originated in the USA," overtly identifying the United Kingdom's copying of the American model as the problem.[3] The concerns cited by the review included a significant and sharp rise in referrals, poorly understood marked changes in the types of patients referred, scarce and inconclusive evidence to support clinical decision-making, and operational failures of the single gender clinic model as evidenced by long wait times for initial assessment.[3]
