# Toronto Rain Tax

The "rain tax" is the popular name for a stormwater charge contemplated by the city of Toronto in 2024, under which residents would be taxed according to the amount of rainwater falling on their properties.[1]

## Structure of the proposal

The city formally described the measure as a wastewater usage tax, calculated from the volume of rainwater generated by residents' hard surfaces such as driveways and roofs.[1] Properties with more hard surface area would incur higher charges on the rationale that impermeable surfaces contribute runoff capable of overwhelming the municipal sewer system during storms.[1] The proposal was in a feedback phase of consideration under Mayor [[olivia-chow|Olivia Chow]] when it was reported.[1] Alongside shock and mockery, critics raised practical objections about the feasibility of accurately measuring and enforcing taxes tied to individual property characteristics.[1]

## Enforcement mechanism

Under the scheme as described, private landowners would be charged on the basis of drone footage obtained by the city combined with the property's water usage — a combination of aerial surveillance and utility billing that critics characterized as dystopian.[1]

## Criticism

Conservative coverage in Canada framed the measure as a communist encroachment, on the view that it sits only a few steps short of the state declaring that it owns rainwater on behalf of "the people."[1] Badlands Media writer [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] accepted that framing but argued the more objectionable feature was the city's use of the policy rollout to advertise the achievements of its Toronto Water utility, given reporting that Toronto drinking water contains dangerously high levels of painkillers, blood thinners, and chemotherapy drugs.[1] Esler noted that the city's presentation used billion-dollar waterway management projects as the pretext for the new charge, and summarized the arrangement as taxpayers being charged billions "to poison us with water tainted by pharmaceuticals."[1]

## Precedents and outlook

Similar rain-based taxation schemes already function in other Canadian cities as well as in Philadelphia, Seattle, and elsewhere in the United States.[1] Esler urged readers to watch these programs as latent instruments through which climate-change narratives can be used to raise private utility bills according to the weather.[1]
