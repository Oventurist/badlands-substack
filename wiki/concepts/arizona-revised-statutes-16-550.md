# A.R.S. § 16-550

Arizona Revised Statutes § 16-550, titled "Receipt of voter's ballot; cure period," is the provision of Arizona election law that governs how county officials must handle an early (mail-in) ballot when it is returned.[1] The statute provides that "on receipt of the envelope containing the early ballot and the ballot affidavit, the county recorder or other officer in charge of elections shall compare the signatures thereon with the signature of the elector on the elector's registration record."[1]

## The "shall compare" requirement

The operative words of the statute are "shall compare." In the May 2023 remanded trial of [[lake-v-hobbs|Lake v. Hobbs et al.]], attorneys for [[kari-lake|Kari Lake]] repeatedly hammered the point that the statute requires an actual comparison, and that approving a signature without comparing it against at least one exemplar signature held in the county database is not [[signature-verification|signature verification]] at all.[1] The defendants' own witness testified to the same understanding of the statutory requirement.[1] Under this reading, so-called Level 1 review means that a human being looks at the signature on the ballot affidavit envelope and compares it to at least one exemplar in the county's records.[1]

A corollary advanced by the plaintiffs was that if machines rather than humans were performing the comparison — as the plaintiffs alleged occurred through a batch upload of more than 45,000 approvals — then signature verification under Arizona law and statute did not take place.[1]

## Role in litigation

Count III of Lake's original election contest alleged that [[maricopa-county|Maricopa County]] failed to comply with § 16-550(A).[1] The [[arizona-supreme-court|Arizona Supreme Court]] remanded that count to the trial court, directing it to determine whether the claim failed to state a claim for reasons other than laches, or whether Lake could prove her claim under A.R.S. § 16-672 and establish that votes were affected "in sufficient numbers to alter the outcome of the election" based on a "competent mathematical basis to conclude that the outcome would plausibly have been different, not simply an untethered assertion of uncertainty."[1]

In closing argument, Lake attorney [[kurt-olsen|Kurt Olsen]] told the court, "Your Honor, the election was unlawful…16-550 was not complied with. Defendants don't dispute our expert. They don't dispute the evidence."[1] Judge [[peter-thompson|Peter Thompson]] nonetheless dismissed the claim, reasoning that accepting the plaintiffs' theory would require the court to rewrite both the Elections Procedures Manual and Arizona law to insert a minimum time for signature verification and to specify the variables to be considered in the process.[1]
