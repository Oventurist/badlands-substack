# Social Contract

The social contract, as described in the Badlands Media essay "Cracks in the Curtain," is the idea of people willingly working to support each other as a group in exchange for individual benefits: to receive the benefits produced by the group, each member of that group has to do their fair share.[1]

## Origins in shared burdens

The essay derives the concept from the situation of a person living alone, who must cook, clean, stay warm, and guard the homestead, and whose quality of life takes a hit whenever illness forces a day off.[1] When such people recognize that they share common needs and interests, they can partner up to make life easier for themselves and for others in the arrangement.[1] The resulting idea of sharing the burdens of life — "let us live together and share the burdens of life so that all might live a better life" — gives birth to the social contract, reducing the work needed to survive while increasing quality of life, and leading to shared ventures, group enterprise, and ultimately communities and societies.[1]

## Trust, stewardship, and money

Because the failings of one member ripple out to everyone — as when the person responsible for gardening falsely claims to have planted winter crops — a system to measure each person's work contribution becomes useful, a role [[money|money]] can serve by assigning point values or credits to goods and services.[1] Someone who receives a community benefit without doing their work is in debt to the community, and debt in this sense becomes a tool for motivating labor, though the need to use debt at all indicates that the community has some measure of sickness and malfunction.[1]

Trust is presented as the central feature both of money and of the social contract: the ability to rely on something or someone, and to be confident that it will be there when needed.[1] A breach of trust creates ripple effects that destabilize other parts of life and generate further failures, and the more trust breaks down in a society, the more chaos results.[1] The arrangement therefore requires stewards, also called representatives — people trusted to represent one's interests in society — and trusting others to do their fair share while trusting representatives to honor their word produces stability and confidence in society and the economy.[1] As stability breaks down, the essay argues, debt-based money is needed more and more to motivate people to work.[1]

## Breakdown

Ideally, in this account, a society would not need debt to motivate citizens at all: members would want to serve others and enjoy the rewards of a good life together as a grand family.[1] Such altruistic values are said to emerge when a singular and common identity forms at the heart of a social group, usually through a spiritual cosmology or religion, provided it is defined completely and objectively.[1] Where no such cosmology exists, or where it has been degraded or weaponized — which the essay asserts is the present condition — society cannibalizes itself, descending into competing social castes, tribes, and cliques that recognize no benefit in serving others.[1] Out of that chaos emerges the law of the jungle, in which the rule of law is replaced by "might is right" and "he who wields the most force accumulates power."[1] The underlying cause is identified as a loss of confidence in the community's ability to provide for its members: as confidence decreases, criminality increases, opening the door to a battle between criminals for control.[1]
