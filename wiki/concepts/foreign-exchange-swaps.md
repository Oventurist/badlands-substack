# Foreign Exchange Swaps

Foreign exchange (forex) swaps are agreements in which two parties exchange one currency for another, used principally to hedge currency risk.[1] They are traded in the foreign exchange market, the largest market in the world, where trillions of dollars change hands daily.[1]

## Users and purpose

Corporations, financial institutions and central banks all use forex swaps.[1] When the US dollar strengthens sharply, as it did in 2022, companies that earn revenue in foreign currencies but report in dollars see their earnings hurt; buying forex swaps protects them from unfavourable exchange rates.[1]

## Off-balance-sheet character

Because of accounting rules, forex swaps are often unrecorded on balance sheets and are therefore highly opaque.[1] The January 2023 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay "Master and Commander" characterises a market trading trillions daily with no balance-sheet requirement as a massive bubble and a systemic danger to a [[global-debt-system|global debt system]] built on a [[fiat-currency|fiat currency]] backed by nothing.[1]

## Scale of hidden dollar debt

Citing Visual Capitalist's Elements, the essay reports that no less than $65 trillion in unrecorded dollar debt circulates through the global financial system in non-US banks and shadow banks, against global GDP of roughly $104 trillion.[1] An estimated $39 trillion of that is held by non-US banks and $26 trillion by overseas shadow banks — unregulated financial intermediaries.[1] This dollar debt exploded over the previous decade as years of monetary easing and ultra-low interest rates pushed investors to search for higher yields, and today the unrecorded debt is worth more than double the dollar debt officially recorded on those institutions' balance sheets.[1] As interest rates rose, forex swap activity increased further amid higher market volatility as investors sought to hedge currency risk.[1]

## Foreign Exchange Departments

The essay also examines the bank units that transact this business. The main function of a bank's foreign exchange department is to make money by speculating on whether a particular currency will rise or fall against another, with banks competing fiercely through experienced market traders and millions of dollars in currency exchanged daily.[1] Each bank maintains direct links to its national foreign exchange market via dedicated phone lines and computers, with screens providing constantly updated statistical and analytical data and complex programs attempting to predict currency movements; instant buy or sell decisions can make or lose substantial sums in seconds.[1]
