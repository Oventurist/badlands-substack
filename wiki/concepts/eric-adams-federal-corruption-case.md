# Eric Adams Federal Corruption Case

The federal corruption case against New York City Mayor [[eric-adams|Eric Adams]] made him the first sitting NYC mayor to face criminal charges, exposing him to up to 45 years in prison if convicted on all counts, which include bribery and fraud.[1] Adams pleaded not guilty and denied wrongdoing.[1]

## Expansion of the case

On Wednesday, October 2, 2024, federal prosecutors said they were pursuing "several related investigations" that could expand the case to include additional charges and co-defendants.[1] At a status hearing that day, Assistant U.S. Attorney Hagan Scotten said it was "quite likely" prosecutors would seek a superseding indictment and "likely" that other defendants would be charged, adding that it was "possible" Adams himself would face more charges.[1]

Adams' legal team filed a motion on Monday, September 30, to dismiss the bribery charge, arguing that the conduct alleged by prosecutors did not reach the threshold for bribery, and accused the federal government of leaking information about the investigation.[1] U.S. District Judge Dale E. Ho did not immediately set a trial date; prosecutors said they expected the trial to run about four weeks.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], characterized the related investigations and prospective superseding indictment as evidence that the mayor's RICO scandal was only beginning.[1] The Brief has argued that the investigation has the potential to publicly deconstruct the public-private partnerships that run the country, noting that those already implicated comprise a network of elected and appointed officials, law enforcement, NGOs and corporations, with implications spanning bribery, corruption and fixing.[1]

The commentary linked the case to broader activity by the [[southern-district-of-new-york|Southern District of New York]], invoking the parallel prosecution of [[sean-combs|Sean "Diddy" Combs]], and concluded that corruption had been the widespread status quo for a long time — not confined to the mayor's office or to New York — and that the only way to restore public trust is accountability.[1]
