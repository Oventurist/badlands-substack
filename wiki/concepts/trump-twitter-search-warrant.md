# Trump Twitter Search Warrant

The **Trump Twitter search warrant** was a federal search warrant issued in January 2023 that gave investigators working for Special Counsel [[jack-smith|Jack Smith]] access to large volumes of data associated with former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s account on Twitter, the platform since rebranded as X.[1] Court filings unsealed on August 15, 2023 revealed that the material obtained included draft posts, location data and direct messages, and that the company had initially refused to comply with the demand.[1]

## Scope of the warrant

The warrant, issued as part of Smith's election interference investigation, sought access to all tweets made from the @realdonaldtrump account, including deleted posts, drafts, liked posts and retweets.[1] Twitter was additionally directed to hand over direct messages "received by, stored in draft form, in or otherwise associated with the subject account including attachments, multimedia, header information, metadata, and logs."[1]

## Litigation and non-disclosure order

Twitter contested the request and challenged an attached non-disclosure order that barred it from informing Trump or his lawyers before producing the data.[1] Federal investigators argued that secrecy was necessary to prevent the former president from tampering with evidence and harming the investigation, and the judge who signed the warrant agreed, describing the company's request as "extraordinary" given Twitter's concession that it had "never before" sought comparable treatment for any other user.[1] During the hearing the judge asked Twitter's lawyer whether the company's owner and then-chief executive [[elon-musk|Elon Musk]] "wants to cozy up with the former President, and that's why you are here?"[1] Twitter ultimately complied and produced the responsive documents on February 9, three days after the judge's mandated deadline, and was fined $350,000 for missing it.[1]

## Flight-risk finding and secrecy

Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] wrote that the disclosure of the subpoena of Twitter by Special Counsel Jack Smith was surprising in a number of respects, including the hefty $350,000 fine imposed by U.S. District Court judge [[beryl-howell|Beryl Howell]] for a three-day delay while the company sought to address the demand.[2] He argued that the two most surprising and concerning elements were that the subpoena was secret and that Howell justified it in part on the ground that Trump was a flight risk, neither of which seemed warranted even if the subpoena was otherwise justified.[2]

Turley noted that Smith had subpoenaed and obtained a search warrant related to Trump's account on Twitter, now X, together with a nondisclosure order prohibiting the company from disclosing the existence or contents of the warrant to Trump or anyone else — despite the fact that Trump already knew he was under investigation.[2] The court reasoned that Trump might change his course of conduct, which Turley considered unlikely given that Trump had been most consistent in his social media practices and had remained entirely undeterred by criticism of his posting.[2]

## Deschamps commentary

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argued that the effort to gather evidence from X without informing Trump was a transparent attempt to cheat by denying him the chance to prepare a valid defense, and that the flight-risk rationale made no sense given that Trump had never failed to appear and is one of the most recognizable figures in world history.[2] He defined [[lawfare|lawfare]] as the use of legal process to retard, hinder or prevent justice in principle, and in practice to starve out and waste an opponent's resources so as to win outside of merit, concluding that an outcome produced by manipulation and coercion cannot reflect truth or justice.[2] He suggested the [[cabal|Cabal]] believed it was putting Trump on trial when in fact it was itself on trial, and that even if the globalists managed to rig Trump out of the 2024 race they would "legally win but lawfully lose," spending what little social capital they had left.[2] Deschamps also read the $350,000 fine as evidence for his contention that the modern court system, possibly up to the Supreme Court, functions as a for-profit private mediation business installed some time in the 19th century, likely after the Civil War, meaning the United States has lacked a genuine venue for settling matters of law for over a century.[2]

## Badlands commentary

Writing for [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]], [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the episode particularly alarming even in an age when "privacy is a myth," arguing that the weaponized federal government had gone beyond collaborating with social media companies to suppress speech and had used its full power to reach into a president's unsent drafts.[1] She characterized prosecution based on draft messages as "essentially prosecuting thought crimes," warning that anyone who has ever composed an angry response and saved it rather than sending it could see that act used against them.[1] She contended there was a strong case that the action violated the Fourth Amendment, but that the federal government no longer recognized limitations on its own power, so that "what they do to Trump is a precursor for what they will do to anyone who challenges them."[1] She singled out the conduct of Musk and Twitter in fighting the government and absorbing the fine as a high point of the story, noting the irony that the federal government "literally exists to protect our rights."[1]
