# Netflix–Warner Bros Merger

The Netflix–Warner Bros merger is a $108.5 billion acquisition announced in December 2025 under which [[netflix|Netflix]], the world's largest streaming company, would absorb Warner Bros.[1] The transaction sent shock waves through Hollywood and immediately drew organized opposition from within the industry.[1]

## Terms and scope

The deal would add to Netflix's catalogue franchises including Game of Thrones, titles from the DC Universe, Dune and Harry Potter.[1] It was concluded after a weeks-long bidding war between Netflix and Paramount.[1]

## Backlash

Hollywood's elite launched an aggressive campaign against the acquisition, with director [[james-cameron|James Cameron]] describing the buyout as a "disaster."[1] Over the weekend following the announcement, the [[writers-guild-of-america|Writers Guild of America]] said the merger "must be blocked," arguing that "the world's largest streaming company swallowing one of its biggest competitors is what antitrust laws were designed to prevent."[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the merger as the predictable endpoint of the [[2023-hollywood-labor-strikes|2023 Hollywood labor strikes]], recalling an October 2023 essay in which she noted that the writers' contract ran only 18 months — until May 2026 — and predicted that studios would hold far greater leverage once generative artificial intelligence matured.[1] The 2023 settlement had delivered a 5% minimum pay increase on ratification, a further 4% in May 2024 and 3.5% in May 2025, along with provisions that AI could not write or rewrite literary material and that AI-generated material would not count as source material, plus a new streaming residual tied to viewership.[1] Ratified by 99% of members after nearly 150 days on strike, the deal left unclear whether writers' room and staffing minimums had been secured.[1]

Writing 25 months later, she concluded that "the end of the writers' strike spelled the end of the industry," that "there are going to be fewer jobs than people," and that "unions always show up a few years too late."[1]
