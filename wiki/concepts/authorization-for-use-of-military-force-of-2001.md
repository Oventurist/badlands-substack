# Authorization for Use of Military Force of 2001

The **Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF)** of 2001 is the joint resolution passed by [[congress|Congress]] shortly after the September 11 attacks, authorizing the President to use all "necessary and appropriate force" against those he determines planned, authorized, committed or aided the attacks, or who harbored such persons or groups.[1] In [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]]'s January 2024 Badlands essay "War Pigs," the AUMF is presented as the central legal instrument invoked to justify open-ended American military action abroad, including [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]'s bombing of [[yemen|Yemen]].[1]

## Scope and expansion

DeLarme cites a Congressional Research Service report published May 11, 2016, which found that the 2001 authorization had by then been invoked "37 times in connection with actions in 14 countries and on the high seas."[1] The countries reached through the authorization include Afghanistan, Cuba (Guantanamo Bay), Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Georgia, Iraq, Kenya, Libya, the Philippines, Somalia, Syria and Yemen.[1] Although the resolution was first used against Al Qaeda, it was later stretched to cover targets with no involvement in the September 11 attacks; the 2011 intervention that ended with the killing of [[muammar-gaddafi|Muammar Gaddafi]] under [[barack-obama|Barack Obama]] and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] was among the operations in which the AUMF was cited.[1]

## Badlands critique

DeLarme describes the AUMF as "a neocon's wet dream," arguing that the [[neoconservatives|neoconservatives]] had conceived of such a blanket authorization well before 9/11 as "the ultimate workaround to initiate endless wars," and that the events of September 11 merely provided the occasion to enact it.[1] He declines to adjudicate the legal debate in scholarly terms, proposing instead that the authorization be judged "by its fruit" — that is, by the two decades of open-ended military operations it enabled.[1] He also situates it within a broader theory of executive power that, in his account, was radicalized during the Bush–Cheney years through expansive readings of Article Two of the Constitution.[1]

DeLarme's conclusion is that the founders opposed a standing army available at a president's discretion, that the President is commander-in-chief only once Congress has declared war, and that the argument that Biden possessed the authority to attack Yemen is fundamentally "a neocon's argument."[1] He calls for the AUMF to be repealed and, in the interim, for Congress to withhold the funding necessary to continue the Yemen bombing campaign.[1]

## Relationship to the War Powers Resolution

The AUMF is frequently offered alongside the [[war-powers-act|War Powers Resolution]] as authority for unilateral presidential strikes.[1] DeLarme argues the two are routinely conflated and misrepresented: the War Powers Resolution confers no new offensive authority, and the AUMF is a 2001 statute whose subject matter — the perpetrators of 9/11 — bears no evident connection to the Houthi movement in 2024.[1]
