# Deportation of US Citizen Children (2025)

In April 2025, attorneys and advocacy organizations reported that three children who are U.S. citizens were deported to Honduras alongside their mothers, including a four-year-old undergoing treatment for metastatic cancer.[1] The cases became a focal point in the wider dispute over due process in the [[trump-administration|Trump administration]]'s immigration crackdown.[1]

## The cases

According to a news release issued by the American Civil Liberties Union, the National Immigration Project, and other organizations, one mother was deported with her two-year-old, while a second was deported with her four- and seven-year-old children.[1] All the women were detained when they attended routine check-in meetings with officials in Louisiana as part of the Intensive Supervision Appearance Program (ISAP), according to attorneys and court records.[1]

Gracie Willis, a lawyer and raids response coordinator at the National Immigration Project who represents the two-year-old through a family friend acting as petitioner, said: "We are seeing in real time due process eroded... That is deeply concerning and these cases are an illustration of that."[1]

## Commentary

Badlands contributor Chris Paul framed the episode as a prelude to a Fourteenth Amendment challenge, arguing that birthright citizenship — which he characterized as resting on flawed court precedent — was being used to claim the children as U.S. citizens and, by extension, to argue that their parents must be permitted to remain in order to avoid family separation.[1]

Contributor Ashe in America argued that the underlying legal question had been settled in the Elián González case, in which a Democratic president and his attorney general pressed a child's immigration claim to the Supreme Court.[1] Her position was that a five-year-old could not make an immigration claim in opposition to his father, and that it followed that a two-year-old — or a four-year-old with cancer — could not make one in opposition to a mother who wished to take the child with her.[1] She acknowledged the emotional force of the cancer case while characterizing the broader argument as "emotional blackmail."[1]
