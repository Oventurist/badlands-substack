# Purple Revolution

**Purple Revolution** is the name given, in Badlands Media commentary, to an alleged domestic [[color-revolution|color revolution]] mounted against [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] following his victory in the 2016 presidential election and culminating in the 2020 election and the events of January 6, 2021.[1] The framing treats the removal or attempted removal of a duly elected official through censorship, propaganda, violence and election tampering as the defining signature of a color revolution, with a foreign-installed or domestically preferred replacement taking the ousted figure's place.[1]

## Definition and antecedents

The author contrasts the standard encyclopedic definition of a color revolution — a series of often violent protests and accompanying changes of government in post-Soviet states and the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, triggered by election results widely viewed as falsified, distinguished by internet-based organizing and heavy non-governmental organization involvement — with an applied definition centred on the ouster of an elected leader by sabotage.[1] The [[1953-iranian-coup|1953 Iranian coup]], in which the CIA and MI6 replaced Iran's leader with the Shah, is offered as the model case.[1] Color revolutions are conventionally identified by a color, and the article notes that searching for "Purple Revolution" returns more results about the musician Prince than about the alleged operation against Trump.[1]

## The purple signal

The article's central symbolic claim is that senior figures signalled participation through the wearing of purple.[1] On November 9, 2016, six days after the election, [[bill-clinton|Bill Clinton]] wore a purple tie and [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]] wore a purple shirt with purple lapels during her concession appearance.[1] Former Deputy Attorney General [[rod-rosenstein|Rod Rosenstein]] wore a purple tie when he testified before the [[senate-judiciary-committee|Senate Judiciary Committee]] on June 3, 2020 about the FISA application process used in the FBI's [[crossfire-hurricane|Crossfire Hurricane]] investigation.[1] FBI Director [[christopher-wray|Christopher Wray]] wore a purple tie when sworn in on July 12, 2017, and again on February 5, 2020 when addressing a congressional committee on election interference.[1]

## Wray, censorship and the 2020 election

Wray told the House Judiciary Committee that Russia was engaged in "information warfare" heading into the 2020 election through a covert social media campaign of fictional personas, bots and disinformation, while stating that law enforcement had seen no ongoing efforts to target election infrastructure as in 2016.[1] He also said that in a country with a First Amendment "the FBI is not going to be in the business of being the truth police and monitoring disinformation online."[1] The article contrasts this with later disclosures, after Elon Musk's purchase of Twitter, that the FBI had some 80 agents working full time at Twitter monitoring and censoring users.[1] Three interpretations of Wray are offered: that he was oblivious to his own bureau, that he was a senior [[deep-state|Deep State]] actor, or that he was a "grey hat" pushing implausible narratives deliberately.[1]

## The Summer of Love and the June 2020 mutiny

The article describes the 2020 "Summer of Love" as a pivot from Covid-era fear to racial division once lockdown compliance began collapsing, with [[black-lives-matter|BLM]] and [[antifa|ANTIFA]] riots restoring control through hysteria.[1] Anti-lockdown protests were treated as super-spreader events while the [[2020-george-floyd-riots|George Floyd riots]] were treated as permissible.[1]

A compressed sequence of events in early June 2020 is presented as evidence of coordinated mutiny: on June 1, Trump announced he might use federal troops to end the rioting; on June 2, former president [[george-w-bush|George W. Bush]] publicly supported the protests; on June 3, Rosenstein testified in his purple tie while General [[james-mattis|James Mattis]] denounced Trump as a threat to the Constitution and Secretary of Defense [[mark-esper|Mark Esper]] publicly opposed the use of federal troops; General [[john-kelly|John Kelly]] denounced Trump on June 5 and General [[colin-powell|Colin Powell]] on June 7.[1] Some figures invoked the [[twenty-fifth-amendment|25th Amendment]] to remove Trump, which the author characterizes as literal mutiny by officers over whom Trump held command authority.[1]

## Q interpretation and military intelligence

Addressing readers who cite [[qanon|Q]] for the proposition that "the military is the only way," the article stresses the conditional: the military is the only way *once it is fully controlled*, reading the marker 11.3 as 2020 Election Day.[1] Q post 4415 of June 4, 2020 is read as describing former and active military leaders turning on Trump, and post 4424 of June 5, 2020 as indicating that the chain of command had been infiltrated at the highest levels — contradicting earlier readings of Q post 26 that the military had already been cleaned out.[1]

The personnel changes immediately after the election are treated as the moment control was secured: six days after the vote Trump fired Esper and installed [[christopher-miller|Chris Miller]]; the following day Chief of Staff to the Secretary of Defense Jen Stewart was replaced by [[kash-patel|Kash Patel]], Undersecretary of Defense for Policy James Anderson by General [[anthony-tata|Anthony Tata]], and Undersecretary of Defense for Intelligence Admiral Joseph Kernan by [[ezra-cohen-watnick|Ezra Cohen-Watnick]].[1] The author argues Trump held full control of military intelligence only from November 10, 2020 to January 20, 2021, and used that window to collect the "receipts" for future indictments of Deep State actors.[1]

## Milley, Nakasone and January 6

On October 1, 2020, [[national-security-agency|NSA]] head General [[paul-nakasone|Paul M. Nakasone]] met General [[mark-milley|Mark Milley]] to discuss securing the democratic process, an encounter the article leaves deliberately ambiguous.[1] Citing a [[department-of-defense|Department of Defense]] inspector general report, it asserts the DOD rather than the DOJ was the lead agency for January 6 and that Milley was in charge of the [[january-6-capitol-protest|J-6]] operation.[1] The author holds simultaneously that the 2020 election was stolen and J-6 staged to frame Trump as an insurrectionist, and that Trump foresaw and permitted both as a trap, on the theory that the Deep State intended to trigger civil war had Trump won.[1] Milley's later conduct — communications with China, "woke" military policy, the Taliban's capture of US equipment, the Chinese spy balloon, and the Russian pipeline demolition — is floated as possible deliberate sabotage of the Biden administration rather than incompetence.[1]

## Who was not fired

The article notes that Trump did not fire Milley, Wray, or CIA Director [[gina-haspel|Gina Haspel]], while cautioning that non-removal does not imply loyalty; some were simply left out of the loop or fed misinformation.[1] Haspel, subject of post-election rumors that she died in a Ukraine shootout over election servers, is described as alive, sidelined, and subsequently employed by British defense contractor [[bae-systems|BAE Systems]].[1] The author connects her London CIA station chief tours to the claim that the Cabal is headquartered in the City of London and that the "Russia, Russia, Russia" narrative originated there in 2016.[1] Trump's earlier appointment of [[mike-pompeo|Mike Pompeo]] to the CIA, Pompeo's move to State after [[rex-tillerson|Rex Tillerson]]'s firing, and Haspel's promotion are presented as the point at which Trump shifted his intelligence reliance from the CIA to the DOJ under [[william-barr|Bill Barr]].[1]

## Infiltration of the administration

General [[h-r-mcmaster|H.R. McMaster]], appointed National Security Advisor on February 20, 2017 in place of General [[michael-flynn|Michael Flynn]] and fired thirteen months later, is accused of ordering surveillance on Trump's family and [[steve-bannon|Steve Bannon]], and is quoted after J-6 saying Trump and other officials "repeatedly compromised our principles in pursuit of partisan advantage and personal gain" in a "dereliction of duty."[1] By contrast Bannon and Flynn, both removed early, are said to have spent the following seven years defending Trump — the article's test for distinguishing saboteurs from allies pushed outside the administration.[1] Lt. Col. [[alexander-vindman|Alexander Vindman]], whose account of the Zelensky call led to Trump's first impeachment and Senate acquittal, is presented as a further example of military infiltration below flag rank.[1]

[[erik-prince|Erik Prince]], founder of [[blackwater|Blackwater]], is discussed as evidence that the Trump team distrusted the US military enough to seek private-sector counsel: he first denied a transition role, then acknowledged attending the Trump Tower meeting of August 2016.[1] Trump appointed Prince's sister [[betsy-devos|Betsy DeVos]] Secretary of Education and pardoned four Blackwater employees convicted over a 2007 shooting in Iraq that killed 17 people.[1]

## Conclusion

The article's thesis is that Trump inherited a military compromised at its top levels by "TV Generals" promoted to feed the military-industrial complex, that he could remove cabinet officers but not strip career officers of rank, and that the pressure of his presidency forced Deep State actors to expose themselves.[1] It forecasts that 2024 would bring a wave of defectors and accusers alleging misconduct, urging readers to treat the coming attacks as predictable "birthing pains."[1]
