# Harvard/Harris Poll on the Trump Indictment

The Harvard/Harris poll released in June 2023 measured American public opinion on the federal indictment of [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and produced results widely characterized as damaging for Attorney General [[merrick-garland|Merrick Garland]] and the [[department-of-justice|Justice Department]].[1]

## Findings

The poll found that 55 percent of Americans believed Trump's indictment was politically motivated and 56 percent believed it constituted election interference.[1] Only 44 percent regarded the prosecution as "the fair application of the law," meaning less than a majority viewed the indictment as well-founded and justified — a deterioration from earlier measurements.[1] Among partisan subgroups, 83 percent of Republicans and 55 percent of independents viewed the indictment as a political exercise, while support for the indictment was concentrated among Democrats at roughly 80 percent.[1]

The survey also produced unfavorable numbers for President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]]: 65 percent of respondents believed Biden had "mishandled" classified material, and 72 percent took that view of [[hillary-clinton|Hillary Clinton]]'s email scandal.[1]

## Turley's analysis

Legal commentator [[jonathan-turley|Jonathan Turley]] argued that the poll captured the depth of public distrust in the Justice Department and reinforced his assessment of Garland's failure at the midpoint of his tenure as Attorney General.[1] Turley concluded that the Justice Department and the media had "lost the room" with the American people and were primarily appealing to Democrats.[1]

## Methodological criticism

Badlands Media contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] treated the poll as useful chiefly for understanding how the regime interprets shifting voter sentiment rather than as a reliable instrument.[1] She noted that the 60-page results presentation contained no mention of a margin of error, despite margin of error being a standard and essential indicator of polling reliability that can vary from question to question.[1] The methodology summary stated that results were weighted for age within gender, region, race/ethnicity, marital status, household size, income, employment, education, political party and political ideology, with propensity score weighting also applied to adjust for respondents' propensity to be online — yet Harvard provided no specific data on the weighting methods used.[1]

Ashe in America characterized this omission as a "trust us, bro" posture, argued that the undisclosed weighting and missing margin of error likely understated the true share of voters who view the indictment as political and as 2024 election interference, and pointed to what she described as a misleading use of colors in the side-by-side visual presentation of the two key questions.[1] She concluded that the episode further demonstrated that [[harvard-university|Harvard]] is not a reliable source of truth, while noting that even the published results broadly favored a return to normalcy and a rejection of what she termed the cultural Marxism plaguing American society.[1]
