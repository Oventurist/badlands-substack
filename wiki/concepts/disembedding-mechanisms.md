# Disembedding Mechanisms

**Disembedding mechanisms** is a term coined by the sociologist [[anthony-giddens|Anthony Giddens]] and taken up by [[iain-mcgilchrist|Iain McGilchrist]] in *[[the-master-and-his-emissary|The Master and His Emissary]]* for the modern influences whose effect is to separate things from their context and individuals from the uniqueness of place, or *locale*, so that real things and experiences are replaced by symbolic tokens and expert systems replace local know-how.[1] In the March 2024 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] essay "How Your Western Brain Exposes You to 5G Warfare," [[simon-esler|Simon Esler]] treats the concept as the single idea from McGilchrist most pertinent to [[fifth-generation-warfare|fifth-generation warfare]].[1]

## Two operating fronts

Esler, following the Giddens passage quoted by McGilchrist, identifies two fronts on which the mechanisms operate.[1] The first is "the intrusion of distant events into everyday consciousness," which continually removes individuals from their local, embodied context — as when a viewer watches social media footage of bodies in Gaza from home, on a train or in a neighbourhood park.[1] The second is "a random juxtaposition of items of information," the presentation of material stripped of its original context: a three-second clip of the war in Ukraine, followed by a news station's biased narrative, followed by a pharmaceutical commercial.[1] Decontextualization comes first, then random juxtaposition; together they lock cognition into the left hemisphere and shift focus toward symbolic tokens.[1]

## News broadcasting as the exemplary case

The essay treats news consumption, whether broadcast or algorithmic, as the most powerful contemporary example of decontextualization and disembodiment.[1] In a meeting room before the broadcast, one war is chosen over others — the war between Israel and Palestine over the war in Armenia or the conflict in the Congo — and the claim made on the viewer is not general awareness but acute awareness of that particular war.[1] The decontextualization is then compounded: not only is this war deemed more important than others, but one particular interpretation of one fragmented moment of it is elevated above the rest.[1]

Esler describes the viewer in a living room, able to smell dinner cooking and hear children upstairs, whose nervous system is nonetheless "being played like an instrument not by the war itself, but by the nature and severity of the story being told."[1] After a carefully crafted two-to-five-minute segment, the coverage cuts to a story about a dog and a dolphin becoming friends; the social media equivalent is scrolling from a graphic war clip to an AI-generated video of Will Smith eating spaghetti.[1] On social media the clip additionally arrives saturated by likes, comments and the spin of whoever shared it.[1]

## Significance for the cognitive battlefield

Because the process is so intense, frequent and normalized, Esler argues, it goes unquestioned, and its most important effect — training cognition to ignore the benefits of right-hemisphere thinking — goes unnoticed.[1] He insists on distinguishing this ambient *cognitive conditioning* from deliberate, weaponized attempts to modify cognition: the conditioning is the battlefield itself, on which enemy operations are then waged, and it dictates which weapons can be used against a population.[1] Under the influence of the phone and the drone of social media, "where you are doesn't matter," and the people, weather, smells, plants and animals of one's locale slip into irrelevance in a split second, leaving both the disembodied consumer and the consumed content profoundly out of context.[1]
