# Greenbacks

**Greenbacks** were the United States Treasury notes issued during the American Civil War under President [[abraham-lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]], created as a sovereign national currency issued directly by the federal government rather than by private banks. In the account given by Badlands Media contributor [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]], the greenback was the decisive financial instrument that allowed the Union to fund the war without submitting to the terms of Wall Street and City of London lenders.[1]

## Background

Before the war, the American monetary system was radically decentralized. More than 7,000 recognized bank notes circulated in the United States, issued by over 1,496 banks, each of which could put multiple note varieties into circulation with no federal authority supervising them.[1] This condition followed the 1836 killing of the national bank under [[andrew-jackson|Andrew Jackson]] and the passage of the [[independent-treasury-act-of-1846|Independent Treasury Act of 1846]], which prevented the government from influencing economic affairs.[1] Under these conditions no national projects were possible, international investment was scarce, manufacturing collapsed, speculation dominated, and the slavocracy grew in influence between the panic of 1837 and 1860.[1]

Historian [[anton-chaitkin|Anton Chaitkin]], in volume two of *Who We Are: America's Fight For Universal Progress*, described the period as an artificial crisis in which state-chartered banks used deposits for dangerous speculation and issued paper notes promising redemption in gold or silver specie — notes that frequently were not accepted far from the issuing bank's locality. In the crisis of the Union, fear of the future shriveled credit and investment, Southerners stopped paying most debts owed to northern businesses, and the larger northern banks hoarded gold, leaving depositors and note-holders across the country with nothing.[1]

## The bankers' ultimatum

When Lincoln sought loans to prosecute the war, both Wall Street and international banking houses offered them only at excessive interest rates of 20 to 25 percent.[1] Historian Robert Ingraham recorded that in January 1862 Gallatin, head of the New York Associated Banks, presented the bankers' ultimatum to the Treasury: pay for the war through a massive increase in direct taxation; deposit all U.S. government gold in the private New York banks and make those banks the monopoly agent for marketing U.S. government debt, primarily bonds sold in London; suspend the sub-treasury laws regulating banks; and withdraw all government-issued paper currency so that only gold and private bank notes would circulate.[1]

## Creation and operation

Lincoln's alternative rested on Article 1, Section 8 of the U.S. Constitution, under which sovereign control of credit issuance can only be effected through the U.S. Treasury.[1] Following that principle, Lincoln issued the greenbacks, a currency that could only be issued against U.S. government bonds, beginning with the [[legal-tender-act-of-1862|Legal Tender Act of 1862]].[1] The measure formed one leg of a three-step operation that also included the Banking and Currency Acts of 1862 and 1863 and the [[national-bank-act-of-1863|Bank Act of 1863]].[1]

Nationally chartered banks were obliged to deposit at least one third of their capital into the federal treasury in exchange for government notes issued by the Mint and Treasury. Because a federal charter was required to escape the tax on state bank activity, banks found themselves lending to the government — which gave Lincoln the ability to avoid usurious loans from London and Wall Street.[1] Alongside the greenbacks, the Treasury issued [[five-twenty-bonds|5:20 bonds]], which served as the basis for further greenback issuance.[1] The program was accompanied by a strong protective tariff intended to grow American industry.[1]

By the beginning of 1865, $450 million in greenbacks had been issued, making up over half of all currency in circulation.[1] Lincoln credited the patriotism of the people for placing at the disposal of the government the large means demanded by public exigencies, noting that much of the national loan had been taken by citizens of the industrial classes.[1]

## Industrial effects

Greenbacks and 5:20 bonds financed not only the arming, feeding and payment of soldiers but also the large-scale industrial and rail programs begun at the peak of the war, most notably the [[first-transcontinental-railroad|transcontinental railway]], started in 1863 and completed in 1869.[1] These grants and subsidies, made possible by the greenbacks, are said to have increased government spending power by 300 percent.[1] Lincoln's economic advisor [[henry-c-carey|Henry C. Carey]] wrote in his 1865 essay *How to Outdo England Without Fighting Her* that "the 'greenback' has fallen on the country as the dew falls, bringing with it good to all and doing injury to none."[1]

## Destruction

The subversion of the system began quickly with Lincoln's murder, which Ehret attributes to Confederate intelligence operations run through British Canada.[1] Rather than the full reconstruction of the defeated South that Lincoln had planned, a new war was waged against the greenbacks by the [[city-of-london|City of London]] and its American agents on Wall Street, culminating in the [[specie-resumption-act|Specie Resumption Act]], which killed the greenbacks and tied the republic's currency to gold.[1] Chaitkin wrote that immediately after the assassination the Anglo-American financier faction ramped up its attack on Lincoln's national-sovereign economic policy, demanding "honest money" and clamoring for the greenbacks to be taken out of circulation so that gold could be restored to its throne — a contraction that would sharply reduce consumer demand and bring hard times to workers, farmers and businessmen.[1]

Ehret presents the greenback episode as a live lesson for anyone serious about ending the privately run Federal Reserve's control over national finance today.[1]
