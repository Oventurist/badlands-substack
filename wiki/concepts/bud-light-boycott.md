# Bud Light Boycott

The Bud Light boycott was the consumer backlash against [[bud-light|Bud Light]] following a 2023 promotional campaign involving transgender influencer [[dylan-mulvaney|Dylan Mulvaney]], which produced months of slumping sales for parent company [[anheuser-busch-inbev|Anheuser-Busch InBev]].[1]

## Layoffs

In late July 2023, after months of declining Bud Light sales, Anheuser-Busch InBev laid off hundreds of workers at its U.S. offices.[1] The world's largest brewer, which also sells Stella Artois and [[budweiser|Budweiser]], said the cuts would affect less than 2% of its roughly 18,000-strong U.S. workforce and would not touch front-line brewery and warehouse staff.[1] The restructuring eliminated corporate and marketing roles at major U.S. offices in St. Louis, New York and Los Angeles.[1]

Anheuser-Busch Chief Executive [[brendan-whitworth|Brendan Whitworth]] said the company never took such decisions lightly but wanted to ensure the organization remained set for long-term success, adding that the changes would let its teams focus on "brewing great beer for everyone."[1]

## Commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], writing in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], argued that the actions of Bud Light's Harvard-educated former VP of Marketing had life-changing consequences for 645 employees, calling the celebration of Mulvaney's campaign "the worst party ever."[1] She noted the brand was reporting a permanent loss of 25% of its business as a result of the failed campaign while executive compensation was unlikely to be affected, describing the transfer of consequences to rank-and-file employees as immoral but entirely expected.[1] She concluded that every move made in the wake of the "woke disaster" had further deteriorated the brand, making Bud Light one of the most loathed corporations in America.[1]

## Industry-wide impact through 2023

By late December 2023, U.S. beer sales were expected to fall to their lowest level since 1999, an outcome industry experts attributed largely to [[bud-light|Bud Light]]'s collapse following its promotional tie-up with transgender influencer [[dylan-mulvaney|Dylan Mulvaney]].[2] Sales declined by more than 5% in the first nine months of the year, dragged down both by the boycott of the [[anheuser-busch-inbev|Anheuser-Busch]]-owned brand and by the changing habits of younger drinkers, according to Beer Marketer's Insights.[2] Bud Light sales fell by 25% to 30% each month after Mulvaney's April 1 posts touting the brand, and the fiasco unseated Bud Light as the top-selling beer in the United States after two decades of dominance.[2]

Rival brands captured some of that share — Constellation Brands' Modelo overtook Bud Light, and Molson Coors' Coors Light and Miller Lite saw upticks — but their growth did not offset Anheuser-Busch's declines.[2] David Steinman, vice president and executive editor of Beer Marketer's Insights, told the New York Post that "this year in particular AB is driving the decline in the industry."[2]

## Measured on-premise impact

Data published by the hospitality platform Union showed the boycott's effect inside bars and restaurants: [[bud-light|Bud Light]]'s dollar-sales share at thousands of high-volume venues fell from 11.3 percent to 8.73 percent in the first week alone, and its on-premise sales share dropped 34 percent year over year across the second quarter beginning April 1.[3] Union's chief marketing officer Layne Cox reported that guests were by then spending more on [[miller-lite|Miller Lite]] — up 21 percent in the three months to June 30 — than on Bud Light, and that domestic beer brands generally were the chief beneficiaries.[3]

Badlands contributor Justin Deschamps argued the episode mattered beyond beer sales, describing it as a trigger for "much-needed mass disillusionment" with the assumption that mega-corporations represent American values, and as a spur to consumers redefining Americanism and patriotism for themselves.[3]

## Legal and corporate consequences (June 2023)

By June 2023, conservative groups presented the Bud Light and [[target|Target]] boycotts as the mechanism that finally gave them legal traction against corporate activism, because customer defections created demonstrable financial harm.[4] [[scott-shepard|Scott Shepard]] of the [[free-enterprise-project|Free Enterprise Project]] argued that [[esg|ESG]] mandates pursued at shareholders' expense could constitute a breach of fiduciary responsibility exposing companies to litigation.[4]

The boycotts were also credited with prompting a broader corporate retreat: multiple reports said executives, including at [[starbucks|Starbucks]], were reducing advocacy on green and social initiatives amid the Target and Bud Light stock-market disasters.[4] [[breitbart|Breitbart]] cited a June 12, 2023 Wall Street Journal report that finance chiefs and other executives had "significantly quieted down in public settings" about environmental and employee diversity efforts.[4]

Badlands contributor [[johnny-q|Johnny Q]] framed the episode as proof of the power of purchasing power, listing Bud Light, Target and Kohl's as companies made to bear the example of backlash from parents over what he described as the hypersexualization pushed on children, and calling it a case of cancel culture cancelling itself.[4] [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] concluded that companies were willing to upset the "rainbow coalition" because the boycotting side's purchasing power had proven greater, urging readers to shift dollars to the parallel economy.[4]
