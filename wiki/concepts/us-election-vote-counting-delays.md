# US Election Vote-Counting Delays

**US election vote-counting delays** refers to the practice, unusual among democracies, whereby the United States is unable to produce complete presidential vote totals on election night, with tabulation instead extending over days, weeks, or in some cases months.[1] Badlands contributor [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] argued in November 2024 that this is not an accident of scale but a deliberate structural choice, and that it is a principal driver of American distrust in elections.[1]

## The American pattern

DeLarme described the delay as "maybe the most bizarre, yet accepted part of the modern American political tradition," noting that the United States stands almost completely alone in the democratic world in its inability to count all presidential votes in a single day.[1] As an extreme example, California's 2020 Democratic primary did not produce a final vote total until two and a half months after voters cast their ballots.[1] He argued that the public is "already being groomed" not to expect results anywhere near election night.[1]

Reporting cited in the article supported the expectation of delay. [[politico|Politico]] interviewed the head of the [[fox-news|Fox News]] decision desk, Arnon Mishkin, who said he planned to call the race as soon as possible but that a protracted count meant it could take days, putting "the over/under" at Saturday — the same day the call was made in 2020, and roughly when Pennsylvania was expected to report.[1] [[cbs-news|CBS News]] reported that unlike many other states, Pennsylvania can only begin processing mail-in ballots on the morning of Election Day; in 2020 it took four days to call the state, during which police and protesters gathered outside the Pennsylvania Convention Center in Philadelphia while [[al-schmidt|Al Schmidt]] and fellow Board of Elections members oversaw the counting of a record 375,000 mail-in ballots, most from Democratic voters.[1]

## International comparisons

Independent journalist [[glenn-greenwald|Glenn Greenwald]] contrasted the American system with Brazil, a country he characterised as "the anti-Germany" and not known for bureaucratic efficiency.[1] Brazilian elections are held on a Sunday to maximise turnout, voting is mandatory, voters show identification, and the franchise extends to 16- and 17-year-olds, producing a total vote roughly comparable in volume to the United States despite a smaller population.[1] Polls open at 8 a.m. and close at 5 or 6 p.m. depending on the state, and by 9 to 11 p.m. the complete count for presidential, gubernatorial and state legislative races is fully tabulated and certified.[1]

DeLarme also rejected the argument that population size explains the American delay, pointing to Indonesia, which holds the world's largest single-day election.[1] Citing *U.S. News & World Report*, he noted Indonesia has roughly 205 million registered voters with turnout around 75%, a six-hour voting window across three time zones, booths overseen by election officials, party members and independent observers, nationwide poll closure before counting begins, secret ballots, and indelible ink on voters' fingers to prevent duplicate voting, with quick counts from independent survey agencies giving early indications the same day.[1]

## Structural explanations

The article acknowledged counterarguments worth noting: the United States has no federalized election system of the kind found in Brazil, France, Argentina or South Korea, with each state instead responsible for counting and certifying its own totals.[1] Most comparison countries now use electronic voting, often with audited paper backups, while the United States continues to use systems DeLarme calls antiquated.[1] His conclusion was that these are choices rather than constraints: "This is all by choice."[1]

## Consequences for public trust

DeLarme argued that Americans are chastised for even considering the possibility of foul play, but that the question of why the country "makes the conscious choice" to spread tabulation over days and weeks is legitimate.[1] Where results keep changing, ballot locations are unclear, and ballots of different kinds are variously destroyed, lost or disputed, he wrote, there are so many angles for malfeasance that declining faith in the electoral process is unsurprising — particularly given that American election outcomes ripple outward and affect the entire world.[1] Until the United States chooses to fix the problem, he concluded, distrust will continue to grow.[1]
