# Student Loan Repayment Pause

The **student loan repayment pause** was a federal moratorium on payments toward government-held student loans that began in March 2020 and was repeatedly extended through executive action until the [[debt-ceiling-agreement-2023|2023 debt ceiling agreement]] barred any further extension.[1]

## Scope and cost

The moratorium postponed roughly $5 billion per month in federal student loan repayments.[1] Late in 2022 the administration of [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] extended it until 60 days after the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|Supreme Court]] ruled on a separate $400 billion loan forgiveness plan, which would have cancelled up to $20,000 in federal loans per borrower and had been announced but never implemented.[1]

## Termination under the Fiscal Responsibility Act

The debt limit agreement announced by Biden and Speaker [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]] and titled the Fiscal Responsibility Act prohibited the administration from extending the pause any further "except as expressly authorized by an act of Congress," while remaining silent on the forgiveness plan itself.[1] [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] had already assumed the pause would lapse on schedule but noted that the agreement eliminated the possibility of another White House extension, pointing to a restart of payments in September 2023.[1]

## Expected economic effects

Jefferies analysts compared the resumption of monthly payments to the 2013 fiscal cliff, when tax increases reduced consumer spending.[1] [[jpmorgan-chase|JPMorgan]] chief U.S. economist Michael Feroli estimated that the end of the moratorium would cut annual disposable personal income by $38 billion, dragging on household consumption.[1] [[zerohedge|ZeroHedge]] characterised the arrangement as a "nonpayment boondoggle" whose end would precipitate a collapse in household spending.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that the debt ceiling fight was best read as a narrative operation rather than a straightforward fiscal negotiation, contending that the deal stripped progressives of one of the Biden era's signature inducements — perpetual delay of student loan repayment — while accelerating a consumer spending contraction that would in his view widen public awareness of fiscal mismanagement.[1]
