# Tina Peters Appeal Ruling 2026

The Tina Peters appeal ruling of 2026 is the decision of a Colorado appellate court that upheld the criminal convictions of former [[mesa-county|Mesa County]] clerk [[tina-peters|Tina Peters]] while overturning her sentence and ordering that she be resentenced.[1]

## Holding

The court found that Peters's original sentencing violated her constitutionally protected speech under the [[first-amendment|First Amendment]], and remanded the matter to the trial court for resentencing.[1] Peters had been convicted in 2024 of three felony counts of attempting to influence a public official, one felony count of conspiracy to influence a public official, and three misdemeanour counts of professional conduct violations.[1] The [[colorado-court-of-appeals|Colorado appellate court]] left all of those convictions intact.[1]

## The election-records finding

A passage of the opinion quoted at length in the Badlands Brief addresses the factual premise most commonly asserted in public debate about the case. The court wrote that the undisputed evidence showed that, before the Build, Brown had already made several backups of the election records, and that at Peters's behest Hayes had created a forensic image of the voting equipment server before the Build — and that Peters was not charged for any crimes arising out of either of those acts.[1] The court concluded that she had fulfilled her duty as Clerk and Recorder to preserve the election records days before the Build, and that her statutory preservation duty therefore had no bearing on whether she engaged in the illegal conduct charged.[1]

## Commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]], who covered the trial, argued that nearly every version of every narrative about the case — from every political perspective — assumes the opposite of that finding, defaulting to some form of the "election meddling" story.[1] She quoted her own Christmas column describing Peters as, to some, a villain and threat to democracy and, to others, a hero, a canary in the coal mine, a political prisoner and a victim of weaponised government and asymmetrical justice — and, in either case, a sick septuagenarian woman spending the holidays in prison because she believed her sworn duty mattered more than the personal consequences.[1]

Ashe and [[canncon]] state that they have maintained since covering the trial that the case — on its facts, evidence and law — was never about elections, and that the electoral framing is storytelling.[1] She compared the dynamic to the "oathbreaking insurrectionist" campaign of 2023–2024, noting the same vibe and the same people.[1] While finding elements of the decision to celebrate, she also described puzzling and frustrating portions, publishing a longer analysis on her own Substack and promising further discussion on [[why-we-vote|Why We Vote]].[1]
