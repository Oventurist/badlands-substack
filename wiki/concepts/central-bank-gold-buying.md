# Central Bank Gold Buying

**Central bank gold buying** refers to the sustained accumulation of physical gold reserves by national monetary authorities, a trend that accelerated sharply in 2022–2023 and is frequently cited as evidence of institutional hedging against the US dollar.[1]

## The 2022–2023 buying streak

According to data compiled by the World Gold Council and reported by SchiffGold, central bank gold reserves rose by another 52 tons in February 2023, marking the eleventh consecutive month of net central bank gold purchases.[1] Through the first two months of 2023 net purchases totaled 125 tons, the strongest start to a year since 2010.[1]

China was the largest single buyer in February 2023, with the People's Bank of China increasing reported holdings by 24.9 tons.[1] It was the fourth consecutive month of publicly reported Chinese purchases, during which China's official gold reserves grew by 102 tons.[1]

## Interpretation

Commenting for the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]], financial advisor [[kirk-elliott|Kirk Elliott]] argued that although Federal Reserve chairs have publicly attributed central bank gold purchases to "tradition," monetary authorities understand that gold represents the true value on their balance sheets — which he offered as the explanation for eleven straight months of buying.[1] Elliott further noted that China is known for withholding key data about its gold holdings, so publicly reported record purchases imply that actual holdings are higher still.[1] He presented the pattern as a case in which retail investors benefit from following the actions of large global players rather than their rhetoric.[1]

## Related

The buying streak is commonly read alongside [[de-dollarization|de-dollarization]] and the erosion of the [[petrodollar|petrodollar]] arrangement as complementary signals of a shift in the international monetary order.[1]

## Record pace in 2023

Central banks accumulated gold at the fastest pace on record in the first two months of 2023, according to a report by the World Gold Council's Krishan Gopaul: in January and February they collectively bought a net 125 tonnes, the highest year-to-date figure since banks became net buyers in 2010.[2]

## Drivers cited by the banks themselves

Surveys of central banks found recurring themes: gold can be mobilized in a crisis, functions as an emergency reserve or contingency against unforeseen events, and serves as insurance — the Bank of England calls it a "war chest" and the "ultimate asset to hold in an emergency."[2] Banks also cited gold's liquidity and its use in raising foreign-currency liquidity for exchange intervention, its role as an inflation hedge, and the absence of counterparty, credit or default risk in physical metal that no government issues.[2]

## Regulatory trigger

Central banks began large-scale purchases after the 2008 housing bubble and credit crisis, but buying accelerated sharply after the [[bank-for-international-settlements|Bank for International Settlements]] and the Basel Committee on Banking Standards moved gold from a tier 3 to a tier 1 asset in 2019, ending its 50% risk weighting and effectively placing it on the same level as the U.S. dollar.[2] Badlands contributor [[joe-lange|Joe Lange]] argues the buying spree, together with [[gold-repatriation|gold repatriation]], shows that even the institutions running the [[fiat-currency|fiat currency]] system expect a global monetary reset and want physical gold rather than centralized custody.[2]
