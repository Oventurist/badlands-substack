# AWACS Sale to Saudi Arabia

The 1981 sale of Airborne Warning and Control System (AWACS) aircraft to [[saudi-arabia|Saudi Arabia]] was, in Joe Lange's account, the event that most clearly exposes the [[shadow-government]]'s pursuit of funding beyond the reach of [[congress|Congress]].[1] Soon after taking office, the [[ronald-reagan|Reagan]] administration pressed the Senate to approve the sale, provoking a major fight between the White House and Capitol Hill; the sale passed the Senate 52-48, with the total value of the purchase reported at $8.5 billion.[1]

## The secret condition

According to the New York Times, King Fahd and other top Saudi officials agreed in 1981 to aid anti-Communist resistance groups around the world as part of the arrangement allowing them to buy the radar planes.[1] Nothing about this condition appeared in the media narrative or in the congressional fight over the sale.[1] Lange attributes the secrecy to the fact that [[richard-secord|Richard Secord]] and his Iranian-American partner [[albert-hakim|Albert Hakim]] were facilitating the deal on behalf of a shadow government; Secord handled the AWACS sale to the Saudis in 1981, before he retired.[1]

As an example of the arrangement in operation, Fahd and other officials tried in 1984 and 1985 to funnel $15 million to the Nicaraguan rebels through Secord and Hakim, according to an American businessman who declined to serve as the conduit.[1]

## Significance

The 1981 arrangement predates the [[boland-amendment|Boland Amendments]] that restricted funding in [[nicaragua]], so the White House faced no legal bar on aid at the time.[1] Lange asks why, absent any ban, Secord would need to arrange a secret Saudi fund at all, and answers that the entire purpose was to avoid reporting anything to congressional oversight committees.[1] The New York Times itself noted that the disclosure showed the administration used covert means to fund resistance groups years earlier than had been publicly known, that it could not be learned who first proposed Saudi financing, and that covert assistance would have had to be reported to oversight committees even though no explicit ban on military aid to the Contras existed until 1984.[1]

Congressional investigations later uncovered a [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] account in Switzerland holding $250 million donated by the Saudis for the Afghan guerrillas, plus testimony before the Senate Intelligence Committee about roughly $30 million donated for the Contras — at least $280 million outside congressional control, which investigators were unable to trace and about which Congress ultimately did nothing.[1] For Lange this is the practical destruction of the constitutional "power of the purse," the one restraint the agency feared most.[1]
