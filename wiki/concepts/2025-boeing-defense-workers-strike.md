# 2025 Boeing Defense Workers Strike

The 2025 Boeing defense workers strike was a work stoppage by roughly 3,200 machinists at [[boeing|Boeing]]'s St. Louis-based defense unit that began at midnight on Monday, August 4, 2025.[1] It was the first strike action by that local chapter since 1996 and immediately affected production of flagship American defense products.[1]

## Origins

Members of the [[international-association-of-machinists-and-aerospace-workers|International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers]] (IAM) District 837 voted to reject an earlier contract agreement on July 28, 2025, setting the stage for a strike if no deal was reached by the end of that Sunday.[1] Boeing put forward a new offer the preceding week that included some improvements to retirement benefits and rescinded an alternative-workweek proposal that workers opposed, but the company did not increase wages beyond its initial proposal.[1] The union voted on Sunday, August 3, to reject that latest offer, and the walkout began hours later.[1]

## Affected production

The St. Louis workforce is responsible for fighter jet production, including the F/A-18 Super Hornet and the F-15EX, as well as manufacture of key weapons such as the Joint Direct Attack Munition, the MQ-25 tanker drone, and the T-7 trainer aircraft.[1] The same workforce is slated to build the sixth-generation F-47 fighter, a contract Boeing won in March 2025, making the labor dispute strategically significant for U.S. air power modernization.[1]

## Union position

Tom Boelling, directing business representative for IAM District 837, said that "IAM District 837 members have spoken loud and clear, they deserve a contract that reflects their skill, dedication, and the critical role they play in our nation's defense," adding that the union stood "shoulder to shoulder with these working families as they fight for fairness and respect on the job."[1]

## Commentary

Writing in the Badlands News Brief, [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that while a strike over safety would be understandable, striking over an alternative workweek and retirement improvements was misguided in an industry facing expanding automation, artificial intelligence, and robotics — technologies that directly correlate to the availability of U.S. jobs.[1] She tied the dispute to a discussion on the program *Culture of Change* with [[blue-eyes|Blue Eyes]] concerning [[mckinsey-and-company|McKinsey]]'s "new operating model for a new world," examined through the lens of treating the American people as shareholders and the Republic as the organization.[1] Her conclusion was that the future of work implies far fewer jobs — because equal or greater output from fewer workers lowers costs and raises profits — and that the union's decision to strike over *future* contract terms amounted to "self identifying for extinction."[1]
