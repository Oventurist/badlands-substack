# S&P 500

The S&P 500 is a stock market index representing the 500 largest companies in the United States by market capitalization. The index is capitalization-weighted, so the larger a company's total value, the greater its representation in the index.

## Concentration in the Badlands account

Erik Carlson's essay [[america-has-a-cia-run-economy|America Has a CIA-Run Economy]] uses the index as the empirical foundation for a broader argument about corporate power. The article states that the 2025 index carries "the most top-heavy weighting the US has ever known": the top five companies account for 33% of the index weighting, up from 11% in 2010. In fifteen years, the article notes, the weighting of the top five tripled, so that one third of the index sits in 1% of its constituent companies.

Carlson draws two conclusions from this. First, that the S&P 500 no longer appropriately represents the U.S. stock market, because so much of its movement is driven by a handful of firms. Second, that the stock market does not represent the economy at all, since only about 1% of American businesses are publicly traded.

The essay quantifies the skew further by comparing the largest company to the twentieth largest. In 2025 [[apple-inc|Apple]] was roughly ten times the size of Home Depot at number twenty; in 2000 Exxon, then the largest, was only three times the size of Eli Lilly at number twenty. Carlson summarizes this as the top 1% now being ten times bigger than the top 4% of the index.

## Composition over time

The article tabulates the five largest American companies by market capitalization in 2000, 2010, 2020, and 2025. In 2000 the list was diversified across sectors — Exxon, GE, Cisco, [[pfizer|Pfizer]], and Walmart. By 2010 it was Exxon, Apple, [[microsoft]], [[berkshire-hathaway|Berkshire Hathaway]], and Walmart. By 2020 it consisted entirely of pre-AI big technology firms: Apple, Microsoft, [[amazon]], [[alphabet-inc|Alphabet]], and [[meta-platforms|Meta]]. By 2025 the list was Apple, [[nvidia]], Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon — all, in Carlson's description, [[artificial-intelligence|AI]] big-tech companies.

The essay raises the 1911 breakup of [[standard-oil|Standard Oil]] into 34 entities as a possible precedent for reducing the influence of today's index leaders.
