# Originalism

**Originalism** is a school of constitutional interpretation holding that the [[united-states-constitution|Constitution]] should be construed according to the meaning its text carried at the time of ratification, with changes to that meaning made through the formal amendment process rather than through judicial reinterpretation.[1]

## The Lepore Debate

The originalist project has been assailed by many historians, and defenders of originalism concede that some of those scholarly works, while flawed, present serious arguments and occasionally supply useful information for originalists to consider.[1] A sharper dispute arose when Harvard historian [[jill-lepore|Jill Lepore]] published an essay in The Atlantic titled "How Originalism Has Killed the Constitution."[1] Writing in Law & Liberty, legal scholar Robert Natelson and historian Paul Moreno defended originalism against what one contributor called Lepore's "constitutional despair," and a subsequent Law & Liberty essay argued that her piece failed to meet standards of scholarship by distorting quotations out of context, misstating facts, and confusing concepts.[1]

## Originalism and Article V

The affirmative case advanced in that debate is that originalism, properly understood, preserves the Constitution and its [[article-v|Article V]] amendment process — the lawful mechanism for change that a constitutional republic requires.[1] By distinguishing between judicial and political processes, originalism is said to provide a framework for protecting democratic legitimacy, popular sovereignty, and the formal amendment machinery at the heart of the American Founding.[1] On this view, the charge that originalism kills the amendment process gets matters precisely backward, since it is the substitution of judicial revision for amendment that renders Article V dormant.[1]

## Commentary

Badlands Media's treatment of the debate shifted the focus to live Article V campaigns, noting efforts to certify both congressional term limits and a balanced budget requirement through state legislatures.[1] [[chris-paul|Chris Paul]] argued that if term limits were certified, the precedent would be used to argue for dismantling the [[twenty-second-amendment-third-term-loophole|Twenty-Second Amendment]], and characterized term limits — like debates over the [[smith-mundt-act|Smith-Mundt Act]] — as "non-solutions that sound like good solutions to people who don't understand the problems."[1]
