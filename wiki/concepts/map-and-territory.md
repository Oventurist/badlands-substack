# Map and Territory

**Map and territory** is the distinction between a mental model of reality and reality itself. In the Badlands Media essay "Expand Your Thinking," it is the organizing idea behind the claim that the brain is always doing two things at once: creating maps of the world and experiencing the world directly.[1]

## The thesis

The essay holds that maps are not meant to replace reality; their purpose is to help us navigate it and nothing more.[1] It argues that one of the greatest hazards of the left hemisphere, in the framework of [[hemispheric-specialization|hemispheric specialization]], is that it can mistake its map for the world itself — and that modern science is held hostage by its own maps and presuppositions about the nature of reality, depriving the world of a more comprehensive understanding of the environment in which it must live and work.[1]

## Perceptual evidence

Two illustrations are offered for the claim that internal maps literally edit perception. The first is hypnotic negative hallucination: the article states that a person can be hypnotized so that another person becomes invisible to them, citing Anthony Jacquin's *Reality Is Plastic* and James Tripp's Online Deep Apprenticeship as sources for the technique, and explains it by saying the mind has already constructed a map of the room that does not include the additional people.[1] The second is the everyday experience of taking a turn while driving and being startled by a car one did not see a moment earlier, presented as evidence that sensory experience is one part direct perception and one part projection of internal maps.[1]

The essay contends that a similar mechanism operates when someone is offered a truth that contradicts their belief system, and that people are especially prone to mistaking their map for the world when under social pressure to conform to a given narrative.[1] It adds that mainstream culture has "installed" certain maps of reality in the minds of many people, who credulously assume that anything coming from the mainstream must be true — while conceding that alternative media and culture have blind spots of their own.[1]

## Expanding one's thinking

To expand one's thinking, on this account, is to learn how to update one's internal maps with new information.[1] Because those maps constitute a person's comfort zone, the process can be uncomfortable; the essay warns that a comfort zone can become a coffin if never left, and compares expansion of thought to building muscle at the gym — unpleasant in the moment, best undertaken in manageable steps, and far more rewarding in the long run.[1] It rejects the adage that ignorance is bliss, holding that few things in life are as pleasurable as the moment one knows for certain that something new and valuable has been learned.[1]

The essay also distinguishes genuine expansion from its counterfeit, noting that it is surprisingly easy to mistake the reinforcement of a premature dogma — or even a narrowing of thought to a single point — for expansion, and that even very intelligent people fall into this error.[1] What is required is not a high IQ but a particular attitude toward novel information: to cast a wide net, consider as many possibilities as possible, and resist the left brain's urge to jump prematurely to belief or disbelief, which narrows thinking rather than widening it.[1] The author notes that prejudice literally means to pre-judge.[1]
