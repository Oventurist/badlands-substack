# New York Civil Fraud Trial

The New York civil fraud trial is the civil proceeding brought by New York State against former President [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and his business entities, which began in the first week of October 2023.[1]

## Appeals court stay

After a hearing on Friday, October 6, 2023, an appeals court granted a stay — a pause — on Judge [[arthur-engoron|Engoron]]'s order that Trump's businesses be dissolved.[1] This was only a partial victory for Trump, as the appeals court held that the trial which had begun that week could continue.[1] Trump's lawyers had filed a 1,000-page appeal amid the trial.[1] Prosecutors opposed the request for a delay, accusing the defense attorneys of attempting "to sow chaos" and disruption.[1]

## Trump Organization response

The [[trump-organization|Trump Organization]] issued a statement after the pause calling the judge's order to cancel the Trumps' business certificates "overzealous": "Judge Engoron's order erroneously sought to adjudicate the rights of non-party business entities that employ nearly 1,000 hard-working New Yorkers, have never been accused of any wrongdoing and, were never given their day in court – in clear violation of their fundamental Constitutional rights and Due Process. We will continue to vigorously defend our company and our incredible employees from this politically-motivated persecution."[1]

The item ran as a bonus entry in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] of October 9, 2023, which framed the domestic legal news as overshadowed by the [[october-7-attacks|October 7 attacks]] in the Middle East.[1]

## Appellate pause of the judgment (March 2024)

On March 25, 2024, a mid-level New York state appeals court granted [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] a pause of the $454 million civil fraud judgment, conditioned on his posting a $175 million bond within ten days.[2] The order averted the seizure of Trump's assets by state authorities and eased what Reuters described as an acute cash crunch, after Trump said he might have to sell properties at "fire sale prices."[2] The ruling delayed enforcement of the finding that Trump had overstated his wealth to dupe investors and lenders, but did not address the merits; a full appellate decision could take a year or longer.[2] Trump told reporters he would post the reduced amount "very quickly" and, asked about collateral, replied "Cash."[2] The office of Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] said Trump remained accountable for "his staggering fraud."[2]

## Trump's appearance at the trial's opening

On Sunday evening, October 1, 2023, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] said he would attend the trial's opening in person, posting on Truth Social, "I'm going to Court tomorrow morning to fight for my name and reputation."[3] His attendance had been expected, and law enforcement and court employees had been making security preparations for Monday and possibly Tuesday at the Lower Manhattan courthouse.[3] His plans became clearer after a federal judge in Florida granted a request to postpone a deposition in a separate case that conflicted with the New York trial's start.[3] Trump flew to New York on Sunday evening after a campaign event in Ottumwa, Iowa, and spent the night at Trump Tower; the case, filed in September 2022 by Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] against Trump, his eldest sons, their companies and several executives, was set to begin at 10 a.m. Monday.[3]

Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] dismissed the "walls are closing in" framing, arguing that [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] was the one on trial both in Congress and in the court of public opinion, and that establishment media and politicians would close ranks around the Biden Crime Family, the [[uniparty|Uniparty]] and the [[deep-state|Deep State]].[3]

## Pre-trial summary judgment motion (August 2023)

On Wednesday, August 30, 2023, New York's attorney general asked a state judge to declare, before the start of the trial, that [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] had committed fraud by submitting false statements to bankers and insurers and overstating his net worth by up to $2.23 billion.[4] In filings with a state court in Manhattan, Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] said evidence in her civil case showed "repeated and persistent fraudulent use" by Trump and his family business of false and misleading financial statements from 2011 to 2021.[4] She argued that inflating the values of more than a dozen assets by hundreds of millions of dollars established the defendants' liability for fraud under the state's Executive Law, writing that "the cumulative effect of these numerous deceptive schemes to inflate Mr. Trump's assets, and hence his net worth, is staggering," and was "just the tip of a much larger iceberg of deception" the state was prepared to expose at trial.[4]

Less than an hour after James' filing, lawyers for Trump and the other defendants filed their own papers seeking to end what they called James' "crusade against President Trump" and to dismiss the entire case.[4] Citing a recent appellate court decision, they argued that many of the transactions James challenged had taken place too long ago to be reviewed in court, and that the attorney general had offered no proof that anyone was harmed by the remainder.[4]

[[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] wrote for Badlands that "the witch hunt continues," calling it unusual for an attorney general to ask a judge to decide a case before proceedings begin because doing so effectively precludes the defendant from arguing before the court — "which is the whole point of a trial."[4] He described the attempt to establish key facts before hearings as "a big red flag," insisting that a court case is supposed to be a venue of impartiality where both sides have equal standing and capacity to argue and respond.[4] He argued the move was another attempt to try Trump in the court of public opinion in order to build a predicate for election fraud in 2024, predicting the establishment line would be that voters have a patriotic duty not to vote for a man who is "clearly guilty of something" but knows how to work the system.[4] Deschamps concluded that few voters were buying the framing, while whether "the sleeping giant" would wake enough to make a future steal untenable for the [[deep-state|Deep State]] remained to be seen.[4]

## $175 million appeal bond (April 2024)

On Monday, April 1, 2024, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] posted a $175 million bond, halting collection of the more than $454 million he owed and preventing New York from seizing his assets to satisfy the debt while he appeals, according to a court filing.[5] A New York appellate court had given him 10 days to put up the money after a panel of judges agreed the previous month to reduce the amount required to stop the clock on enforcement.[5] The bond functions as a placeholder guaranteeing payment if the judgment is upheld, in which case Trump would owe the state the full sum, which grows with daily interest; if he prevails on appeal he pays nothing and recovers the posted funds.[5] Until the appellate intervention, Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] had been poised to initiate collection, possibly by seizing marquee Trump properties.[5] Badlands commentary from [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] framed the moment as a reversal for "the regime players" and highlighted that the Appellate court would hear arguments in September.[5]

## Enforcement of the judgment

Judge [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]]'s order required Trump to pay a fraud penalty exceeding $450 million, with a bond of $464 million needed to appeal.[6] Trump offered in February 2024 to post a $100 million appeal bond instead of the full amount, but Engoron denied the proposal.[6] On March 6, 2024, New York Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]]'s office filed judgments with the Westchester County Clerk's Office — the first step a creditor takes to recover property — signaling preparations to seize Seven Springs, Trump's golf course and private estate, with liens or foreclosure as possible next steps.[6] Badlands contributor [[paul-fleuret|Paul Fleuret]] characterized the judgment as a "sham" and the enforcement effort as [[lawfare|lawfare]] aimed at draining Trump's personal funds before the 2024 election.[6]

## Appellate bond reduction

On March 25, 2024, a New York state appeals court agreed to hold off collection of Trump's more than $454 million civil fraud judgment if he posted $175 million within 10 days, a step that would stop the clock on collection and prevent the state from seizing the presumptive Republican nominee's assets during his appeal.[7] Legal analysts read the reduction as a signal of judicial skepticism toward the underlying ruling: Greg Germain, a law professor at Syracuse University, told Newsweek that "the $175 million reduction ... shows that the appellate division has serious concerns about the validity of Judge Engoron's decision," and said Trump may have a strong case to challenge the February order requiring him to pay $455 million.[7]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] noted that legal and financial experts across ideological lines were calling out Engoron's abuses and that the appeals court had rebuked the judge by cutting the bond to $175 million, which Trump planned to pay in cash — a plan she linked to [[trump-media-and-technology-group|Trump Media]] shares surging more than 50 percent following the DJT ticker debut.[7] She also drew a parallel between the administration's condemnation of Venezuela for excluding its leading opposition candidate and the domestic legal campaign against the leading U.S. candidate.[7]

## Judicial-conduct investigation (May 2024)

In May 2024, the case generated a new controversy when New York real estate attorney [[adam-leitman-bailey|Adam Leitman Bailey]] said publicly that he had approached Justice [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]] to offer unsolicited advice about a law at issue in the case, roughly three weeks before Engoron's February decision fining Trump $454 million.[8] Engoron denied impropriety through a court spokesman and said he was "wholly uninfluenced," while New York's judicial oversight body opened an investigation into the alleged interaction.[8] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] observed that a steady stream of investigations, probes, motions and inquiries was being directed not at Trump but at those presiding over his cases, suggesting the proceedings were exposing corruption in the justice system itself.[8]

## February 2024 appeal of the fraud definition

Following the $355 million judgment entered by Judge [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]], [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] moved in February 2024 to challenge the judge's underlying definition of fraud.[9] Trump's principal lawyer in the case, [[chris-kise|Chris Kise]], told Newsweek that the matter "raises serious legal and constitutional questions regarding 'fraud' claims/findings without any actual fraud," and said the appeal's timing would depend on many factors but would fall within the court's 30-day clock.[9] Kise accused Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] and Engoron of trying to run Trump out of New York, arguing that the campaign was bad for the city.[9]

## Enforcement threats after the judgment

On February 20, 2024, New York Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] said she was prepared to seize [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s buildings and assets if he could not pay the nearly $355 million penalty ordered by Justice [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]], who also barred him from doing business in the state for three years.[10] James told ABC News that Trump's ability to pay was "really not my business," and that if he lacked the funds the state would "seek, you know, judgment enforcement mechanisms in court, and we will ask the judge to seize his assets," pointedly noting that she looks at 40 Wall Street, the Trump Building, "each and every day."[10] Trump's attorneys vowed to appeal, describing the case as a "political witch hunt" and the verdict as "manifest injustice," and accused Engoron of judicial malpractice throughout the trial.[10]

## Opening Day, October 2023

The civil fraud trial opened in Manhattan on October 2, 2023, with Donald Trump personally attending the first day of proceedings that could determine the future of his business career.[11] Inside the courtroom, seated in a navy suit and light blue tie and surrounded by Secret Service agents posted on all sides of the room, Trump was a comparatively quiet presence, hunched at times and conferring with his lawyers while attorneys for the New York attorney general outlined their case; he sat directly facing Justice [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]] and offered only occasional scoffs and eye rolls.[11]

Outside the courtroom, by contrast, Trump was combative, telling reporters during the lunch break that Engoron "should be out of office" and that "some people say" the judge "could be charged criminally for what he's doing," while branding the judge deranged and dismissing the suit as a witch hunt.[11] Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] read the performance as Trump deliberately communicating with multiple audiences at once, arguing that the "Greatest Witch Hunt" framing signals that Trump himself is the one now conducting the hunt, and that the Trump trials would set both legal and mass-psychological precedent for a coming justice phase of the exposure and disclosure campaign.[11]

## Mistrial motion (November 2023)

On Wednesday, November 15, 2023, [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]]'s legal team filed a motion for a mistrial, claiming that purported bias on the part of the trial judge and his principal law clerk had "tainted" the case.[12] "This appearance of bias threatens both Defendants' rights and the integrity of the judiciary as an institution," Trump's counsel wrote in the 30-page motion.[12] The filing pointed to posts made by Judge [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]] to a Wheatley School alumni page that the judge appears to run, referencing the case or individuals involved in it, including Trump, his son [[eric-trump|Eric Trump]], and Trump attorney [[alina-habba|Alina Habba]].[12] The motion cited New York judicial conduct rules providing that "a judge shall not make any public comment about a pending or impending proceeding in any court within the United States or its territories."[12]

## Closing arguments (January 2024)

Closing arguments in the $370 million civil fraud trial took place on Thursday, January 11, 2024, with [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] bringing the campaign trail into the courthouse and delivering campaign-style speeches both inside and outside the courtroom to attack the case and the attorney general who brought it.[13] His decision to launch into a monologue at the conclusion of his lawyers' closing arguments reflected both the seriousness of the threat to his business and brand — New York Attorney General [[letitia-james|Letitia James]] was seeking to bar him from doing business in the state — and his eagerness to turn the proceeding to advantage while running for president.[13] Judge [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]], who had already found Trump liable for fraud, said he planned to issue a full decision by the end of the month.[13]

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] described the proceeding as "the Letitia James witch hunt about inflated business valuations where there has been no injury or harm either alleged or proven," and predicted Engoron's findings of fact would amount to declaring Trump satanic and his conclusions of law to liability for "eleventy billion dollars."[13] She wrote that CNN's record of the day showed more of the same, with the prosecution granted poetic license and wide berth while Trump and his attorneys were cut off mid-thought, and noted that Engoron was expected to rule by January 31 on the accompanying charges having already ruled on the top-line count.[13] She added that Trump would be back in court the following week for another round with [[e-jean-carroll|E. Jean Carroll]], framing the sequence as election interference rather than serious law.[13]

## November 2023 Trump testimony

Trump took the stand in the case on Monday, November 6, 2023, in a session from which no cameras were allowed.[14] The state's examination was conducted by [[kevin-wallace|Kevin Wallace]] of the attorney general's office, who opened by asking Trump to confirm that he had led the Trump Organization between May 1, 1981 and January 19, 2017.[14] Trump answered by describing Wallace as one of "every Democratic district attorney, attorney general and US Attorney who were coming after me from fifteen sides, all different cases, all Trump haters," and characterized the effort as "Weaponization."[14]

On the substance, Trump testified that banks had not treated his statements of financial condition as a significant factor, saying they "just weren't a very important element in banks' decision-making process" and that this would be explained "as this crazy trial goes along."[14] He further claimed that his brand added millions of dollars in value to his holdings, remarking, "I became president because of my brand."[14] Judge [[arthur-engoron|Arthur Engoron]] told the former president to "just answer the questions, no speeches," and twice asked defense counsel [[christopher-kise|Christopher Kise]], "Can you control your client?"[14] Kise objected that the state was asking "questions that call for narratives."[14]

Commentary from [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that despite the camera ban the trial generated headlines rivalling Trump's rallies and public appearances, and that the proliferation of simultaneous prosecutions had made it "almost comically difficult to keep track of WHICH Trump Trial we're discussing."[14] In that reading the litigation was not an interruption of the 2024 campaign but its central vehicle: "The Trial(s) of Donald Trump isn't interfering with his campaign. It IS his campaign."[14]
