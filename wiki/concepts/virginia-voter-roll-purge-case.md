# Virginia Voter Roll Purge Case

The Virginia voter roll purge case was an emergency application filed by Virginia Republican officials with the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|US Supreme Court]] on Monday, October 28, 2024, asking the high court to allow the state to implement a program removing suspected noncitizens from its voter rolls.[1] It was one of the first major voting cases to reach the court ahead of the November 5 presidential election.[1]

## Background and stakes

The appeal carried political salience because [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] and other Republicans had seized on the case; CNN characterized their framing as part of a false narrative of widespread voting by people in the country illegally.[1] It was one of several election-related suits already at or expected to reach the Supreme Court in the closing days of an extremely close contest between Trump and [[kamala-harris|Kamala Harris]], with early voting already underway and litigation proliferating nationwide.[1]

A CNN poll conducted by SSRS found that 56% of registered voters had just some or no trust in the Supreme Court to decide election-related cases correctly, with a substantial partisan split: Trump supporters were roughly twice as likely as Harris supporters to express at least a moderate amount of trust in the court.[1]

## Badlands analysis

Writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued the case was bizarre on its face, since the voters in question were self-identified noncitizens who are legally ineligible to vote, and the dispute existed only because the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] had intervened to demand the ineligible names remain on the rolls.[1] She noted Virginia could have procedurally flagged the entries and removed them after the election, and inferred that the state was deliberately staging a public fight during the election period.[1]

From a change-management standpoint, she wrote, the election period — particularly the two weeks bracketing Election Day — is the maximum exposure window when the greatest number of Americans are following the narrative, giving the litigation and the DOJ's forthcoming response brief maximum visibility.[1] She linked the case to election fraud schemes disclosed in Pennsylvania and Colorado and to reported early voting machine failures involving vote swapping in Texas, Tennessee, and Georgia.[1] She emphasized that the case implicated all three branches of Virginia government — governor, legislature, and lower courts — as well as constitutional questions about federal versus state authority over elections, concluding: "You can't tell people. You have to show them."[1]
