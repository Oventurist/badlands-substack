# Colorado Revised Statute 1-4-501

Colorado Revised Statute 1-4-501 is the provision of Colorado election law governing candidate qualification and challenges to it, which became central to the dispute over whether votes cast for [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] could be counted in the state's 2024 presidential primary.[1]

## Text and effect

Subsection (1) provides that "no person except an eligible elector who is at least eighteen years of age, unless another age is required by law, is eligible to hold any office in this state," and that "no person is eligible to be a designee or candidate for office unless that person fully meets the qualifications of that office as stated in the constitution and statutes of this state on or before the date the term of that office begins."[1]

Subsection (3) establishes the challenge mechanism: the qualification of any candidate may be challenged by an eligible elector of the political subdivision within five days after the designated election official's statement certifying the candidate to the ballot.[1] The challenge is made by verified petition setting forth the facts alleged concerning the candidate's qualification and is filed in the district court of the county in which the political subdivision is located.[1] A hearing must be held not less than five nor more than ten days after the certifying statement is issued, and the court must determine within forty-eight hours after the close of the hearing whether the candidate meets the qualifications for the office.[1]

## Application in the 2024 ballot dispute

Because Colorado law permits counting only ballots cast for "qualified" candidates, Secretary of State [[jena-griswold|Jena Griswold]] stated that if the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] upheld the [[colorado-supreme-court|Colorado Supreme Court]]'s decision in [[anderson-v-griswold|Anderson v. Griswold]] removing Trump from the ballot, he would be disqualified and votes for him would not count.[1] Viral claims in early January 2024 recast this as a declaration that Griswold would refuse to count Trump's votes; [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] wrote that Griswold had said no such thing and was simply describing the operation of the statute.[1]

Ashe noted that the statutory challenge process set out in 1-4-501(3) had in fact been followed in the Colorado litigation, and concluded that, however unfair the result might appear, it would be against the law for Griswold to count votes for Trump if the high court upheld the state ruling — making a Colorado write-in campaign for him "dead on arrival."[1] She characterized the statutory framework as evidence that political adversaries in the Centennial State were "way ahead" of [[america-first-movement|America First]] activists in preparing legal ground.[1]
