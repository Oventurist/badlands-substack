# Latchkey Generation

The "Latchkey Generation" is a label applied to [[generation-x|Generation X]], Americans born between 1965 and 1979, describing the first cohort of American children who commonly came home from school to an empty house or apartment.[1] In a December 2024 Badlands Media essay, [[erik-carlson|Erik Carlson]] called Gen X "the original Latchkey Generation," noting that every generation after X could arguably claim the same title.[1]

## Description

In many latchkey households both parents worked, leaving children on their own for two or three hours each afternoon with no supervision, no doorbell cameras, and no phone trackers.[1] Carlson characterizes the resulting free hours as a mix of unsupervised snacking and television — Top Ramen, Doritos, and reruns of *Gilligan's Island*, *The Flintstones*, or *Saved by the Bell* — and outdoor roaming on city street corners, suburban 7-Eleven lots, or rural woods, with risks ranging from trouble with the law to homemade bike ramps and high rope swings.[1]

## Causes

Carlson attributes the phenomenon to two intertwined causes: a sharp rise in divorce and the mass entry of mothers into the workforce during the [[womens-movement|Women's Movement]] of the 1960s and early 1970s.[1] He cites divorce rates of 2.5 per 1,000 Americans in the 1950s, 3.2 per 1,000 by the end of the 1960s, and roughly half of all marriages begun in the 1970s ending in divorce.[1] He further argues that the influx of second incomes drove up the cost of living, especially housing, so that even families who preferred a single-earner model were eventually forced into two jobs to make ends meet.[1]

## Consequences claimed

The essay presents the latchkey childhood as producing the first American generation to commonly face the trauma of divorce and broken homes, to eat predominantly processed food, and to be "raised by a TV," and links household reliance on convenience to the 1970s explosion of the processed and fast-food industries.[1] Carlson nevertheless frames the outcome positively, arguing this same unsupervised, less-propagandized upbringing left Gen X better able to see through later media narratives.[1]
