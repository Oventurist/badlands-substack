# Supreme Court IEEPA Tariff Ruling (February 2026)

On February 20, 2026, the [[supreme-court-of-the-united-states|U.S. Supreme Court]] ruled that the [[donald-trump|Trump]] administration's sweeping use of emergency powers to impose global tariffs was unconstitutional.[1] The Court found that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) did not authorize broad tariff actions absent an explicit congressional delegation, effectively invalidating the prior tariff regime.[1]

## Administration response

President Trump moved swiftly to preserve high tariffs by invoking an alternative legal authority, announcing a new global tariff of up to 15 percent under the Trade Act of 1974 — the maximum permitted by that statute — after initially setting the rate at 10 percent.[1] [[customs-and-border-protection|U.S. Customs and Border Protection]] halted collection under the struck-down authority while continuing to collect under the new framework.[1] The administration defended the pivot as essential to leveraging American trade interests; European and other international partners criticized the approach and urged adherence to existing trade agreements, while trade officials maintained that core policy goals were unchanged.[1]

Commerce Secretary [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]] remained central to the surviving trade architecture, with national-security tariffs on steel, autos and advanced technology holding firm after the decision — an outcome Badlands writer [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] said both Lutnick and Trump had pre-signaled before the ruling.[1]

## Badlands commentary

[[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] argued that the decision should be read as a separation-of-powers case rather than a defeat for Trump, writing that "the goal is not for Trump to win. The goal is for The People to win."[1] She highlighted Justice [[neil-gorsuch|Neil Gorsuch]]'s concurrence, which described the major questions doctrine as having been obscured by Chevron deference — a presumption favoring delegated power when Congress failed to speak clearly — and characterized the Court's renewed application of the doctrine as "not invention so much as return to form."[1]

She framed the 170-page argument among the justices over executive, legislative and judicial authority as healthy, emphasizing the opinion's stress on representation: the taxing power rests with the legislature because the legislature is the representative branch, which in her reading makes elections the decisive arena.[1] Addressing claims that Trump was enraged at two of his three appointees — he reportedly called them horrible and said they embarrassed their families — she countered that the most consequential constitutional debates at the Court are being driven by his three picks.[1]
