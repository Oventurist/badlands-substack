# Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction, in the legal framework laid out by Badlands Media's analysis of the American monetary system, is the condition of being subject to a particular set of rules by virtue of where one is and what role one is occupying.[1] The concept is developed at length because the author treats it as the key to understanding how a private credit issuer came to exercise authority over the money of the United States.[1]

## Law as a rule system

The article argues that a system of law is, in essence, a set of rules comparable to the rules of a game.[1] In basketball, players, coaches, referees and audience all accept rules that define each class of participant's permissible actions; a player may take a shot only during game time and from within the boundaries of the court, while a referee fills an entirely different role.[1] Generalizing from this, the article holds that a legal system is a set of rules defining *types of persons* and the *allowable actions* each type may perform.[1] Corporate Law thus concerns corporations, commerce, employees and shareholders, while Constitutional Law concerns citizens, officials and domestic security.[1]

## The grocery store analogy

To illustrate jurisdiction the article offers a grocery store scenario. The manager of a store cannot telephone a stranger at home and compel him to clean a spill on aisle eight, because the rules governing employees apply only to those who agreed to be employees.[1] If, however, the same person is inside the store as a customer and knocks over a jar of pickles, the manager may require him to clean or pay for it — but only if the governing law of that store makes customers liable for messes they create, as with a "you break it, you buy it" policy.[1]

At home one occupies a different territory from store policy; while acting as a customer one is obliged to observe the customer-facing sections of that policy.[1] This, the article says, is the essence of being under the jurisdiction of a given system of laws.[1] Within their territory, a proprietor may impose terms and conditions: closing the store, requiring shirts and shoes, designating employee-only zones, and charging for breakages.[1] The same toggling applies online — Facebook's in-house rules bind users while on the platform and cease to apply elsewhere.[1] The governing rule is that where you are and what you are doing usually determines what set of rules apply to you.[1]

## Persons and roles

Quoting Black's Law Dictionary, the article defines a *person* as "a man considered according to the rank he holds in society, with all the rights to which the place he holds entitles him, and the duties which it imposes," and suggests treating personhood as equivalent to a role one adopts from time to time.[1] Four such roles are deduced from the store scenarios: manager, customer, employee and foreigner.[1] A person foreign to the store — neither employed there nor present — stands outside its corporate law system entirely and is subject to none of its internal policy.[1] See [[legal-personhood]].

## Application to the Federal Reserve

The article applies these principles to argue that the jurisdiction of [[federal-reserve|the Federal Reserve]]'s corporate code does not automatically extend to an individual in his capacity as a state citizen, but does extend to him as a federal citizen under [[fourteenth-amendment|the 14th Amendment]].[1] The mechanism by which the individual is brought inside that jurisdiction is [[agency-law|agency]]: the federal government, acting as agent, became the Federal Reserve's customer on the people's behalf.[1] See also [[state-citizenship]], [[bastard-system-of-laws]] and [[natural-law]].
