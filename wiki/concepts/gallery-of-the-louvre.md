# Gallery of the Louvre

*Gallery of the Louvre* is the monumental tableau painted by [[samuel-morse|Samuel F. B. Morse]] during his stay in Paris beginning in 1831, and the centrepiece of Badlands Media commentary by [[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] on the role of art in republican politics.[1]

## Conception

America of the period had few museums and little access to Renaissance painting, so Morse spent months in the Louvre in order to bring back "an entire museum in one painting."[1] The finished tableau features thirty-seven reproductions of works Morse deemed the most sublime and influential in the museum, including [[leonardo-da-vinci|Leonardo da Vinci]]'s *Mona Lisa* and paintings by Titian, Velázquez and Raphael.[1] The canvas toured the United States to great acclaim in 1831, drawing thousands of paying viewers, and is still renowned for the fidelity with which Morse shifted between the styles and essential dignity of each master.[1]

## Composition and meaning

The room depicted, the Salon Carré or "Paris Room," genuinely exists, but none of the paintings Morse portrays within it were ever located there; each was placed for effect.[1] Ehret compares this to Raphael Sanzio's *School of Athens* of 1509, where philosophers separated by centuries — Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, Pythagoras and Anaxagoras — are assembled in dialogue, and where Raphael inserted himself beside Ptolemy.[1] Morse likewise inserted himself giving an art lesson to his daughter at the centre of the composition.[1] The article treats the work as evidence that artists need not flee into abstract surrealism to express creativity transcending the senses; classical realism can carry transcendental ideas.[1]

Other figures in the tableau include [[james-fenimore-cooper|James Fenimore Cooper]] with his wife and their daughter Susan Cooper, a renowned painter who had died two years earlier; Richard Habersham, an American painter and sculptor residing with Morse during his two years in France; and Lucretia Morse, Samuel's wife, who had died at the age of twenty-six in 1825, several years before the painting was begun.[1] The presence of the dead alongside the living makes the picture, like Raphael's mural, an expression of the simultaneity of eternity — participation in immortal ideas placing people in a higher order of time, Kairos, transcending the moment-to-moment Chronos.[1]

In *Samuel F.B. Morse's Gallery of the Louvre* (2011), [[pierre-beaudry|Pierre Beaudry]] wrote that Morse's design was "the passion of communicating to mankind the universal actions of discovery that the Italian Renaissance had made," particularly the power of immortality he had discovered in Italy around the themes of the Crucifixion and Resurrection, which became the central reference point of the work; no fewer than six large paintings within it treat that subject.[1]

## Contrast with Maillot and Géricault

Shortly before Morse began, the French artist Nicolas-Sébastien Maillot (1781–1857) completed his own painting of the same Louvre room, then devoted entirely to French works; Ehret judges most of the pictures within it unintelligible, the exception being Théodore Géricault's 1819 *The Raft of the Medusa* at its centre.[1] That canvas — inspired by the sinking of the ship *Medusa* off Mauritania on 5 July 1816, after which thirteen days adrift left all but thirteen of some 150 people dead amid suffering and cannibalism — is described as a painting of despair without hope, yet was celebrated as avant-garde and rewarded with acclaim and financial success across the salons of France.[1] Morse found it emblematic of the moral rot of French culture in his time, and the contrast with Frederic Edwin Church's *Cotopaxi* of the [[hudson-river-school|Hudson River School]] frames the article's argument about hope versus despair in art.[1]
