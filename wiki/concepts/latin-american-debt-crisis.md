# Latin American Debt Crisis

The Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s — a period often called the "lost decade" — was an episode in which many Latin American countries became unable to service their foreign debt, inflicting heavy losses on the US commercial banks that had lent to them.[1]

## Buildup

Latin American borrowing from US commercial banks and other creditors rose dramatically during the 1970s. Total outstanding debt from all sources stood at only $29 billion at the end of 1970, had skyrocketed to $159 billion by the end of 1978, and reached $327 billion by 1982.[1] The risk did not go unnoticed: in a 1977 speech at the Columbia University Graduate School of Business, then-[[federal-reserve|Federal Reserve]] Chairman Arthur Burns criticized commercial banks for assuming excessive risk in their Third World lending.[1] Even so, by 1982 the nine largest US money-center banks held Latin American debt amounting to 176 percent of their capital, with total less-developed-country debt near 290 percent of capital.[1]

## The 1982 rupture

Nominal interest rates rose globally and the world economy entered recession in 1981, while commercial banks began shortening repayment periods and charging higher rates, rendering Latin American debt burdens unsustainable.[1] The spark came in August 1982, when Mexican Finance Minister Jesús Silva Herzog informed the Federal Reserve chairman, the US Treasury secretary and the [[international-monetary-fund|IMF]] managing director that Mexico could no longer service its $80 billion debt.[1] Other countries quickly followed; ultimately sixteen Latin American countries rescheduled their debts, as did eleven less-developed countries elsewhere.[1]
