# Public-Private Partnership

The public-private partnership is the dominant governance model for U.S. [[critical-infrastructure|critical infrastructure]] cybersecurity, in which federal agencies, federally funded nonprofits, trade associations, and commercial vendors jointly set standards and priorities for privately or municipally owned systems.[1]

## Anatomy, per the 2024 water hearing

The Badlands account used the witness panel at the February 6, 2024 [[house-committee-on-homeland-security|House Homeland Security Committee]] hearing "Securing Operational Technology: A Deep Dive into the Water Sector" as a case study, arguing that identifying "who these guys are, their motivations and incentives helps us contextualize and better understand their testimony."[1] The panel comprised:

- [[robert-m-lee|Robert M. Lee]], CEO and co-founder of [[dragos|Dragos, Inc.]], an Air Force and [[national-security-agency|NSA]] veteran who advises the [[department-of-energy|Department of Energy]], Singapore's Cyber Security Agency, and [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] cybersecurity committees.[1]
- [[charles-clancy|Charles Clancy]], SVP and CTO at [[mitre-corporation|MITRE]], another NSA alumnus, whose organization supports [[cisa|CISA]], NIST, and [[us-cyber-command|U.S. Cyber Command]].[1]
- [[kevin-morley|Kevin Morley]] of the [[american-water-works-association|American Water Works Association]], also a NIST Disaster Resilience Fellow, a member of the President's National Infrastructure Advisory Council, and a member of the [[water-sector-coordinating-council|Water Sector Coordinating Council]].[1]
- [[marty-edwards|Marty Edwards]] of [[tenable|Tenable]], previously of the [[idaho-national-laboratory|Idaho National Laboratory]] and the last director of [[ics-cert|ICS-CERT]].[1]

The account characterized MITRE in particular as "the nonprofit glue that holds the public private partnerships together," by virtue of operating Federally Funded Research and Development Centers on behalf of the government while embedding expertise throughout the executive branch.[1]

## Critique

The article's central worry about the model is that centralized technical control creates complexity, and complexity "breeds and hides corruption" — either the corruption of security protocols or ordinary crony capitalism, sweetheart deals, and non-competitive contracts.[1] It read the witnesses' unenthusiastic response to Representative [[rob-menendez|Rob Menendez]]'s question about the [[biden-administration|Biden administration]]'s cybersecurity sprints — that utilities "were pushed very strongly to a government specific answer … with no resourcing behind it" — as evidence that particular vendors and technologies were being promoted by central agenda rather than sector need, and speculated openly about non-competitive contracting.[1]
