# Divine Pymander

The Divine Pymander is a Hermetic text attributed to [[hermes-trismegistus|Hermes Trismegistus]] which, according to the author, scholar and lecturer [[manly-p-hall|Manly P. Hall]], was Hermes' greatest work.[1] It was discussed by [[ryan-delarme|Ryan DeLarme]] in his February 2023 [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] survey of [[hermeticism|Hermeticism]].[1]

## Form

The Divine Pymander is written in a fashion similar to the [[emerald-tablet|Emerald Tablet]], reading as a discourse — here between Hermes, or the archetypical truth seeker, and a being known as Pymander, who may be understood as a source of higher wisdom.[1] In the tract Pymander instructs Hermes the sage in the capacity of divine teacher to human pupil, a conception paralleled in the Bhagavad Gita where Krishna teaches Arjuna.[1]

## The name

The name Pymander is of Greek rather than Egyptian type and is variously spelled Pimander and Poemandres.[1] Its derivation appears to be from *poimen*, a shepherd, and *aner*, genitive plural *andron*, giving "Guide of Man" or "Shepherd of men," nearly equivalent to Messiah.[1] [[helena-blavatsky|Blavatsky]], in *The Secret Doctrine*, refers to the Pymander as the "Thought Divine," the Inspired Teacher of that Race, and as "the oldest and most spiritual of the Logoi of the Western continent," while also noting the possibility of a Hebrew derivation from PI, meaning a mouth, command or instruction.[1]

## Relation to the Kybalion

DeLarme writes that beneath its flowery language and distorted translations, the Divine Pymander seems to put forth the same material found in the more recent [[the-kybalion|Kybalion]], which gives a concise summary of the seven Hermetic principles.[1] The text also supplied [[marsilio-ficino|Marsilio Ficino]] with his account of the epithet "Trismegistus," stating that "they called him Trismegistus because he was the greatest philosopher and the greatest priest and the greatest king."[1] DeLarme pointed readers to an online edition of the text at Universal Theosophy.[1]
