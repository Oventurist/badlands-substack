# Ukraine Aid Funding Controversy

The Ukraine aid funding controversy concerns questions over where the roughly $130 billion in U.S. assistance sent to [[ukraine|Ukraine]] for its war against [[russia|Russia]] actually goes.[1]

## Ukraine's own account

In January 2024 a spokesperson for Ukraine told Breitbart News that the money sent so far was apparently not actually going to Ukraine but was really "benefiting American interests."[1] The claim came in response to a Breitbart request for information about whether any American tax dollars had gone specifically to Uman, a Ukrainian city where a condominium was reportedly being built on a Jewish mass grave in violation of a bilateral agreement with the United States and of international law.[1]

The spokesperson explained that instead, a "significant portion" of the billions of dollars is "utilized in the United States for the construction of new weapons or to replenish those dispatched to Kyiv from U.S. reserves," adding that an unspecified analysis found that "nearly 90 percent" of aid granted by Congress "is benefiting American interests," and that "the allocation of funds doesn't imply immediate expenditure."[1] Breitbart observed that "American interests" in this context presumably means weapons manufacturers.[1]

## Political linkage to the border

Badlands commentary connected the funding stream to domestic negotiations, noting that President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] was seeking a deal that would "secure" the border in exchange for Ukraine funding — described by [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] as holding Americans hostage to expand a money-laundering operation — even as House Republicans pursued the [[impeachment-of-alejandro-mayorkas|impeachment of Alejandro Mayorkas]].[1]

## Congressional paralysis of October 2023

The [[ouster-of-kevin-mccarthy|removal of Speaker Kevin McCarthy]] on October 3, 2023, placed further U.S. aid to Ukraine directly at risk.[2] Hardline Republicans who forced the 216-to-210 vote were angered by McCarthy's willingness to fund Ukraine's war, arguing the money would have been better spent protecting the southern border and restoring law and order in major U.S. cities.[2] [[bloomberg|Bloomberg]] reported that the resulting paralysis in the chamber raised the chances of a government shutdown the following month and a delay in further Ukraine assistance, and [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] analyst Alec Phillips advised clients that the next Speaker would face even more pressure than McCarthy to avoid another temporary extension or additional Ukraine funding.[2] European defense stocks including Rheinmetall, Saab, BAE Systems, and Leonardo fell on the news.[2] [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] treated the prospect of reduced Ukraine funding as unambiguously good news, framing it as the end of "easy money" for the [[military-industrial-complex|Military Industrial Complex]].[2]

## The $95 billion supplemental (February 2024)

Early on Tuesday, February 13, 2024, the [[united-states-senate|Senate]] passed a $95.3 billion aid package for [[ukraine|Ukraine]], [[israel|Israel]] and [[taiwan|Taiwan]] after months of difficult negotiations and amid deepening Republican divisions over the American role abroad.[3] A small group of Republicans opposed to the $60 billion earmarked for Ukraine held the floor through the night, arguing the United States should address its own problems before sending more money overseas, but 22 Republicans joined nearly all Democrats to pass the bill 70-29.[3] Supporters argued that abandoning Ukraine would embolden [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]] and threaten national security globally.[3]

Majority Leader [[chuck-schumer|Chuck Schumer]], who worked closely with Republican Leader [[mitch-mcconnell|Mitch McConnell]] on the legislation, said that "with this bill, the Senate declares that American leadership will not waiver, will not falter, will not fail."[3] Passage was welcomed in Kyiv amid critical battlefield shortages, but the package faced a deeply uncertain future in the House, where hardline Republicans aligned with [[donald-trump|Donald Trump]] — the front-runner for the GOP nomination and a critic of Ukraine support — opposed it.[3]

## Johnson's April 2024 commitment

Speaker [[mike-johnson|Mike Johnson]] suggested at the start of April 2024 that the House would act in the coming weeks to get more funding to Ukraine, telling Fox News' "Sunday Night in America with Trey Gowdy": "When we return after this period, we'll be moving a product. But it's going to, I think, have some important innovations."[4] Both chambers were in the second week of a two-week recess with the House due back Tuesday, April 9.[4] Johnson said he had been "working to build consensus" on a supplemental national security and foreign aid package that would look different from prior attempts, and that the House would move it "right after the district work period."[4]

## Mid-2023 funding exhaustion warnings

In mid-May 2023, reporting cited in the [[badlands-news-brief|Badlands News Brief]] indicated that Ukraine aid funding was expected to run out by mid-summer, a warning that coincided with the approach of the U.S. [[debt-ceiling|debt ceiling]] deadline.[5] Badlands commentator [[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] grouped the funding shortfall with the arrest of the head of [[supreme-court-of-ukraine|Ukraine's Supreme Court]] for "large-scale corruption," Russian strikes on U.S.-supplied missile systems, and Ukraine's counter-offensive at [[battle-of-bakhmut|Bakhmut]] as a single picture of escalating strain drawn from one 24-hour news cycle.[5]

## February 2023 House Oversight demand and DoD OIG projects

In February 2023, Republicans on the [[house-oversight-committee|House Oversight Committee]] demanded that the Biden administration account for the billions of dollars [[congress|Congress]] had sent to [[ukraine|Ukraine]], amid growing questions about whether aid should continue at then-current levels.[6] Chairman [[james-comer|James Comer]] and other panel members wrote to the administration on February 22, 2023, seeking a detailed record of where funding had gone to date and what safeguards had been put in place to prevent wasteful spending.[6]

The same day, the [[department-of-defense-office-of-inspector-general|DoD Office of Inspector General]] published an "Oversight Update: Ukraine Assistance" stating that it was "currently planning five potential oversight projects related to Ukraine."[6] [[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[just-human|Just Human]] wrote that the committees were delivering on their promises and that the spending inquiry coincided with a shift in Congress and among the public against further Ukraine aid, complementing the parallel work of the DoD OIG and other inspectors general.[6]

## December 2023 Senate blockade

On December 6, 2023, Republican senators blocked $106 billion in new funding for Ukraine and Israel, rejecting appeals from President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] amid anger over the exclusion of the immigration reforms they had demanded as part of the package.[7] The measure included roughly $61 billion to help Ukraine sustain pressure on [[russia|Russia]] through the winter months, along with aid for Israel and Gaza.[7] Biden warned that failure would be a "gift" to [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]], said opponents were "willing to literally kneecap Ukraine on the battlefield and damage our national security in the process," and predicted that if Putin took Ukraine he would attack a [[nato|NATO]] ally and draw American troops into war with Russian forces.[7] The impasse coincided with Putin's warm reception in Riyadh by [[mohammed-bin-salman|Mohammed bin Salman]], a juxtaposition noted at the very hour Biden delivered a White House speech lamenting congressional inaction, with existing funding due to run out within three weeks.[7] Rep. [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] separately argued that such appropriations were effectively laundered back into the [[military-industrial-complex|military-industrial complex]], enriching contractors and lawmakers alike.[7]

## October 2023: Stopgap Without Ukraine Money

After President [[joe-biden|Joe Biden]] signed a stopgap funding bill over the weekend of September 30–October 1, 2023 that contained no aid for Ukraine — Congressional Republicans having blocked its inclusion — European leaders moved to fill the perceived gap.[8] EU foreign ministers convened what was billed as a "historic" meeting in Kiev in the war's twentieth month, with [[european-union|EU]] foreign policy chief [[josep-borrell|Josep Borrell]] describing Ukraine as a "candidate country and future member of the EU" while cautioning that the meeting was not aimed at concrete conclusions or decisions.[8]

Ukrainian foreign minister [[dmytro-kuleba|Dmytro Kuleba]] praised the EU at a moment of open disappointment with Washington, noting that the foreign affairs council was sitting for the first time outside the Union's current borders but "within future borders of the European Union."[8] France's foreign minister Catherine Colonna framed the gathering as a signal to Moscow of "resolute and lasting support for Ukraine, until it can win," as U.S. support appeared set to wane or halt for the first time.[8] Badlands writer [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] called the absence of Ukraine money the stopgap's one silver lining, tied it to Trump's burden-sharing foreign policy toward China, NATO, the WHO and Mexico, and read the [[kevin-mccarthy|Kevin McCarthy]]–[[matt-gaetz|Matt Gaetz]] conflict as a scripted fight producing an America First outcome — despite weekend reports that McCarthy had struck a secret deal with Democrats on Ukraine funding.[8]
