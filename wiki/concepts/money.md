# Money

Money is the central subject of the Badlands Media essay "Cracks in the Curtain," the first installment of the [[nature-of-the-control-system|Nature of the Control System]] series, in which [[justin-deschamps|Justin Deschamps]] argues that money is "a tool for freedom and prosperity—if it's used properly."[1]

## Definitions and their limits

The essay lists the common answers to the question of what money is and where it comes from: the currency received for work, a medium of exchange, a store of value, a unit of account, something that comes from the bank, something printed by the Mint, or something created "out of thin air."[1] These answers are described as correct but as providing little clarity.[1]

More precisely, the essay treats money as a unit of account that measures value the way a ruler measures length or degrees measure temperature — specifically, the value placed on the goods and services needed to maintain quality of life, such as food to eat, someone to plant crops, and someone to transport, package, and sell it.[1] Its primary purpose is therefore to help an advanced society measure work and resource contribution.[1] A related function is solving valuation challenges that arise as a society grows in population and complexity, since consumers cannot easily judge a fair price for a complex product such as a computer without a money system.[1]

## Trust as money's foundation

Money's most important feature, in this account, is trust.[1] In small communities people usually trust each other enough to avoid the added work of managing a monetary system, provided everyone pulls their weight; when they do not, general trust in the whole community breaks down, and those pulling their weight resent being taken advantage of.[1] Money serves as the measuring system for that contribution, and debt becomes the instrument for those who take community benefits without contributing — although reliance on debt is itself read as a symptom of a malfunctioning community.[1] These arguments connect money directly to the [[social-contract|social contract]] and to civics, government, and law.[1]

## Money as an instrument of control

The essay argues that while almost everyone on earth uses money to some extent, almost no one knows what money is made of at its core, and even fewer know how to maintain the money system — a state of ignorance the author asserts is no accident.[1] Citizens are described as players who have become almost totally blind to the rules governing the financial world, allowing the referees (government) and the other team (the bankers) to cheat honest players in myriad ways, in contrast to professional athletes who spend years learning every governing rule of their sport.[1]

Taking the phrase "follow the money" literally, Deschamps proposes investigating how money is defined within an economy, the laws and regulations managing it, how the general population uses it, and how controlling elites use it — an approach he says would pull back the curtain on the Wizard of Oz and expose one of the pillars of the engines of control.[1] In an author's note he records having studied central bank documents, historical treatises, and academic literature, and having spoken to bankers themselves, receiving different answers depending on whom he asked.[1]

The essay also disputes the assumption that money originates solely with bankers who decide everything about it, including its value, arguing instead that citizens exert incredible influence over money, especially if they become active players in its governance — something it says no citizenry has done in earnest in modern times.[1] This view, attributed in part to [[martin-armstrong|Martin Armstrong]], holds that money is intimately connected with human behavior.[1]

## Stakes

Money is described as the lifeblood of human civilization: properly run and managed, the financial system can help society become prosperous and bountiful, but mismanaged it can cause wars and seemingly endless human suffering.[1] The essay catalogues money's destructive potential — driving husbands from wives, causing parents to sell children into slavery, justifying murder, prompting industries to pollute, leading good people to do bad things, and giving powerful and insidious criminals control over entire countries and the world itself.[1] Given that power, it concludes, people should understand money as if their lives depend on it, because they do.[1]

The essay further argues that money's purpose of measuring work and resource contribution has been so thoroughly distorted over the centuries that some people need not work much at all to thrive — pointing not primarily to welfare but to abstract financial industries in which almost nothing is produced yet the proportion of money paid out is astronomical, with the [[rothschild-family|Rothschilds]] cited as the paradigm case.[1] Ultimately it holds that whether a money system is gold-backed, silver-backed, crypto-based, or made of leather skins, it is only as good as the people who use it.[1]
