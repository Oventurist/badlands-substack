# Epstein Files Transparency Act

The Epstein Files Transparency Act is federal legislation requiring the United States [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]] to produce records relating to [[jeffrey-epstein|Jeffrey Epstein]] and [[ghislaine-maxwell|Ghislaine Maxwell]] to [[congress|Congress]] and the public, subject to statutory protections for victim-identifying information.

## The February 2026 production letter

According to the February 17, 2026 [[badlands-brief-0a2|Badlands Brief]], the DOJ transmitted a formal production letter stating that it had complied with the Act and describing a review and redaction process aimed at protecting victim-identifying information and other material required by law.

The department reported identifying more than 6 million pages as potentially responsive. On the day of the production it released over 3 million responsive pages, together with more than 2,000 videos and 180,000 images; combined with prior releases, nearly 3.5 million pages had been produced. Approximately 200,000 pages were withheld or redacted on the basis of privileges including deliberative process, attorney work product, and attorney-client privilege.

The DOJ also supplied a general description of the categories of redaction applied and a list of "politically exposed persons" referenced in the files. Reporting cited in the Brief stressed that inclusion on the list is not an allegation, and that some named individuals may have had no interaction with Epstein or Maxwell and appear only in incidental material such as press clippings.

## Congressional and public reaction

The production drew immediate criticism. Rep. [[thomas-massie|Thomas Massie]] said he had no confidence in Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]], argued that the DOJ lacks accountability, and said the work on the files was not done, citing shifting redactions across productions and the absence of explanatory context when names were unredacted. Rep. [[nancy-mace|Nancy Mace]] wrote after a document review session at the DOJ that "many of the co-conspirator documents are missing or still redacted," that memoranda and attachments drafted by the DOJ on potential co-conspirators "remain hidden," and that the explanation offered was that the department had received the data in that condition. She concluded that "the American people deserve every page. Every name. And every truth the government is holding onto."

The disclosures were also associated with resignations and investigations across business, politics, and law, including [[tom-pritzker|Tom Pritzker]], [[brad-karp|Brad Karp]], and [[howard-lutnick|Howard Lutnick]]. Badlands commentators placed the entire episode within the contested [[epstein-narrative|Epstein narrative]], debating whether the wave of demands represented genuine accountability or a weaponized information operation.


## Compliance dispute and the Bondi subpoena (March 2026)

The March 18, 2026 [[badlands-brief-1cd|Badlands Brief]] reports that [[house-committee-on-oversight-and-accountability|House Oversight]] Chair [[james-comer|James Comer]] subpoenaed Attorney General [[pam-bondi|Pam Bondi]] for an April 14 deposition on the [[department-of-justice|Department of Justice]]'s compliance with the Act, citing more than 65,000 missing documents, over 2,000 unreleased videos and problematic redactions. The Department replied that lawmakers had been invited to review the unredacted files in person and that Bondi continued to hold calls and meetings with members of Congress on the transparency law.
