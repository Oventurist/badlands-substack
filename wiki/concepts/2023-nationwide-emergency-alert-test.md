# 2023 Nationwide Emergency Alert Test

The 2023 nationwide emergency alert test was a coordinated federal exercise conducted on Wednesday, October 4, 2023, in which a test of the United States emergency alert system pinged cell phones across the country and simultaneously sent alerts to radios and televisions.[1] The exercise was jointly planned by the [[fema|Federal Emergency Management Agency]] and the [[federal-communications-commission|Federal Communications Commission]], and it was reported at the time as the third nationwide test of the wireless emergency alert system but only the second directed at all cellphones.[1]

## Purpose and mechanics

The message delivered during the test included explanatory language making clear that the alert did not correspond to an actual emergency.[1] The federal agencies involved framed the exercise as a way to confirm that the alerting systems "continue to be effective means of warning the public about emergencies, particularly those on the national level."[1] In ordinary operation the system is used to warn the public about dangerous weather, missing children, and other situations the FCC describes as critical.[1] Reporting at the time noted that the wireless emergency alerts system had been used more than 84,000 times since its launch in 2012.[1]

## Precedents

The first test of the wireless component of the system was a "presidential alert" conducted in October 2018, and the most recent nationwide test of wireless alerts on cell phones prior to the October 2023 exercise had taken place in August 2021.[1]

## Anomalies and public reaction

[[badlands-media|Badlands Media]] contributor [[ashe-in-america|Ashe in America]] catalogued a set of anecdotal but widely shared observations about how the alert behaved in practice.[1] One person who powered his phone off did not receive the alert after rebooting, while a contact in Maryland who also powered her phone off received the alert roughly a minute after booting up — both on iPhones.[1] Other iPhone users reported that they had disabled alerts entirely in their settings and nevertheless received the message, whereas Android users reported that disabling notifications successfully suppressed it.[1] Conservative commentator Robby Starbuck posted, and then deleted, a message on X claiming the alert had given people headaches, and users circulating screenshots found that the images of the alert were not all identical.[1] Ashe in America concluded that the event functioned as a moment in which "the country took a collective, momentary breath" amid varied speculation, but that in the end the alert resembled every other emergency alert.[1]

[[burning-bright|Burning Bright]] argued that while a good deal of "grifter fear porn" preceded the test, the episode was inarguably a mass psychological event that became memetic because it simultaneously struck three chords: it was strange, it was funny, and it was worrying.[1] He attributed mastery of that combination to the [[deep-state|Deep State]] and its study and implementation of the [[hegelian-dialectic|Hegelian dialectic]], while asserting that the dynamic had since been reversed against its originators.[1] Burning Bright linked the episode to long-running speculation within the Truth Community that a [[nuclear-scare-event|"Scare Event"]] would take the form of the threat, rather than the actualization, of a nuclear attack initiating a third world war.[1] In his framing, the net effect under adversary control would be another [[false-flag-operation|false flag]] and its ensuing "Money Wars," whereas under patriot control the same stimulus would generate a public mandate for peacemakers to take center stage.[1]
