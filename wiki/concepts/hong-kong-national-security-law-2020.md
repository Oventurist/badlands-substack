# Hong Kong National Security Law (2020)

The National Security Law is the statute imposed on [[hong-kong|Hong Kong]] by Beijing in response to the [[2019-hong-kong-anti-extradition-protests|2019 anti-extradition protests]] and to overseas advocacy efforts.[1] It became the principal instrument through which the territory's remaining freedoms were dismantled.[1]

## Article 38 and extraterritorial reach

Article 38 of the law states that anyone, anywhere — whether or not they are a Hong Kong or Chinese citizen — can be charged under the National Security Law for saying or doing anything deemed threatening to the [[chinese-communist-party|Chinese Communist Party]].[1] Activist [[samuel-chu|Samuel Chu]], an American citizen since 1996, found himself on a CCP fugitives list under this provision despite having done nothing beyond advocating for the [[hong-kong-basic-law|Basic Law]].[1]

## Use against the press

The law was used to destroy [[apple-daily|Apple Daily]], Hong Kong's largest independent newspaper, which was raided by police on August 10, 2020 and again on June 17, 2021 before a freeze of its capital forced the 26-year-old paper to close in June 2021.[1] Its founder, [[jimmy-lai|Jimmy Lai]], has been jailed since late 2020 without substantial convictions.[1]

## Broader effects

[[human-rights-watch|Human Rights Watch]], reporting one year after the law took effect, found that the Chinese government was pressing residents to pledge public loyalty to Beijing and was turning the police and courts into "tools of Chinese state control rather than independent and impartial enforcers of the rule of law."[1] Candidates deemed insufficiently loyal have been barred from Hong Kong's electoral council; academic freedom is under attack; websites have been blocked, museums harassed, films canceled, political slogans banned, and school curricula rewritten.[1] Reporting by Vivian Wang and Alexandra Stevenson of the [[new-york-times|New York Times]] in June 2021 described neighbors urged to report on one another, children taught to look for traitors, officials pressed to pledge loyalty, and police officers trained to goose-step in the Chinese military fashion, replacing decades of British-style marching.[1]
