# Tax-Exempt Foundations

**Tax-exempt foundations** are charitable, grant-making legal entities — in the United States most commonly organized under section 501(c)(3) of the tax code — that Badlands Media's *The Men Behind the Curtain* series identifies as a central operational instrument of the globalist establishment.[1] Where the series treats bodies such as the [[council-on-foreign-relations|Council on Foreign Relations]], the [[bilderberg-group|Bilderberg Group]] and the [[world-economic-forum|World Economic Forum]] as the "heads" of a figurative cabal octopus, it casts the alphabet agencies and the foundations they employ as the tentacles — or, in a second metaphor used in the same article, the foundations are "the arms and legs that facilitate the desires and schemes of the brain."[1]

## Scale

Citing the National Center for Charitable Statistics, the article states that more than 1.5 million tax-exempt charities and foundations currently operate in the United States, most of them presenting "seemingly laudable intentions."[1] The article's contention is that a significant portion of these entities exist to advance the agendas of the globalist institutions catalogued throughout the series, and that a considerable bulk of what is popularly called the [[deep-state|Deep State]] exists in the legal form of not-for-profit foundations and 501(c)(3)s.[1]

## Original purpose and drift

Tax-exempt foundations were, in the article's account, originally set up for humanitarian purposes: to provide grants to existing institutions.[1] [[rene-wormser|Rene A. Wormser]], general counsel to the [[reece-committee|Reece Committee]], is quoted to that effect: "Foundations were originally created to support existing institutions and to undertake certain 'operating' functions."[1] The article argues that this grant-making capacity became one of the primary funding mechanisms by which international foreign-policy think tanks facilitate operations worldwide.[1]

The most frequently cited illustration of the alleged drift is the testimony of [[norman-dodd|Norman Dodd]], director of research for the House Select Committee to Investigate Foundations and Comparable Organizations, who reported in 1952 that the president of the [[ford-foundation|Ford Foundation]] told him the foundation was "operating under a directive from the White House" to "use our grant-making power so as to alter our life in the United States that we can be comfortably merged with the Soviet Union."[1]

## Named examples

The article supplies a long, explicitly non-exhaustive list of nonprofits it associates with this network, including the Agency for International Development, the [[american-civil-liberties-union|American Civil Liberties Union]], Race Forward (Applied Research Center), the American Press Institute, the [[anti-defamation-league|Anti-Defamation League]], the [[aspen-institute|Aspen Institute]], the Association for Humanistic Psychology, the Center for Advanced Studies in the Behavioral Sciences, the Center for Constitutional Rights, the Center for Cuban Studies, the Christian Socialist League, the Communist League, the Environmental Defense Fund, the [[fabian-society|Fabian Society]], the [[ford-foundation|Ford Foundation]], the Foundation for National Progress, the [[german-marshall-fund|German Marshall Fund]], the [[hudson-institute|Hudson Institute]], the International Institute for Strategic Studies, the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation and the Milner Group.[1] The article directs readers to InfluenceWatch and WikiSpooks as research tools for tracing such organizations.[1]

A separate cluster is identified as "policing journalism": the [[pulitzer-center-on-crisis-reporting|Pulitzer Center on Crisis Reporting]], the [[national-press-foundation|National Press Foundation]], the [[international-center-for-journalists|International Center for Journalists]], the [[solutions-journalism-network|Solutions Journalism Network]] and the [[poynter-institute|Poynter Institute]], all of which the article says are connected to individuals belonging to one or more of the clandestine think tanks covered in the series.[1] Other case studies given at length are the [[institute-for-policy-studies|Institute for Policy Studies]], the [[brookings-institution|Brookings Institution]], the Hudson Institute and the [[national-training-laboratories|National Training Laboratories]].[1]

## Taxation and the "tax-exempt complex"

The article frames foundations as a loophole through which the financial elite avoid taxes, and notes that some researchers believe tax avoidance was the purpose of their creation from the beginning.[1] By the time the [[federal-income-tax|federal income tax]] became law in 1913, the Rockefeller and [[carnegie-foundation|Carnegie]] foundations were already operating at full steam; the article argues the graduated income tax functioned less as a penalty on the rich than as a weapon against the middle class, insulating entrenched wealth from new competition.[1] Words such as "humanitarian" and "philanthropy" are described as dog whistles for the financial elite, with grant-making powers most often used to advance founders' interests.[1]

Wormser warned that individual foundations merge in "cartel-like" fashion to fund shared political projects, a tendency "to endanger the freedom of our intellectual and public life"; he called the resulting structure the **tax-exempt complex**.[1] Congressional investigations, the article says, found that various foundations funded political movements inclined toward a socialistic, one-world government — a theme it ties to the broader [[new-world-order|New World Order]] argument of the series.[1]

## Early investigations

The first genuine glimpse into foundation influence came, per the article, under the Congressional Act of August 23, 1912, which created the [[commission-on-industrial-relations|Commission on Industrial Relations]]; beginning with labor exploitation it moved on to concentrations of economic power, interlocking directorates, and the then-new large charitable foundations of Carnegie and Rockefeller as instruments of power concentration.[1] Future Supreme Court Justice [[louis-brandeis|Louis D. Brandeis]] testified on January 23, 1915 about the danger of a "state so powerful" developing within the State, and the commission's report concluded that the ultimate possibilities of such foundations were "so grave a menace" that abolition would be desirable.[1] The [[reece-committee|Reece Committee]] of 1953–1955 revisited the subject; the article places it alongside the [[church-committee|Church Committee]] as one of the two most revealing congressional inquiries in American history.[1]
