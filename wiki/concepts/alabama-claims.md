# Alabama Claims

The Alabama Claims affair of 1869-1871 produced what Matthew Ehret describes as the world's first international trial, held in Geneva to address Britain's military support for the Confederacy during the American [[american-civil-war|Civil War]].[1]

Britain's professed neutrality was betrayed by its construction of Confederate warships that "unleashed havoc on Lincoln's Navy."[1] The court ruled in favour of America, and Britain came close to losing its Canadian possessions as payment — a course advocated by Senator [[charles-sumner|Charles Sumner]] and Secretary of State [[william-seward|William Seward]].[1] Instead, weaker figures in America agreed to a "measly" $15 million settlement in 1872, after which, Ehret writes, all wrongs were forgotten.[1]

Ehret treats the affair as the last great hope for extending [[abraham-lincoln|Lincoln]]'s rail program through [[british-columbia|British Columbia]] into Alaska; with the moment squandered, the effort to assimilate British Columbia into London's northern confederacy accelerated.[1]

## Britain's covert support for the Confederacy

[[matthew-ehret|Matthew Ehret]] presents the Alabama Claims as the legal reckoning for Britain's covert military support of the Confederate cause during the [[american-civil-war|American Civil War]], support which was exposed by the end of that war and which led to Britain's payment of a $15 million settlement to America in 1872.[2] He places the episode within a longer pattern of British hostility that included [[lord-palmerston|Lord Palmerston]]'s 1860 threat to recognise the independence of the Southern slave power and [[robert-gascoyne-cecil|Lord Robert Cecil]]'s statement to Parliament that the Northern States could never be Britain's sure friends because they were political and commercial rivals.[2]
